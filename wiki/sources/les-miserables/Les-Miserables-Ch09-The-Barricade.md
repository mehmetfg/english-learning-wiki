# Les Misérables Chapter 9: The Barricade

**Graded Reader | A2/B1 | ~10,000 words | TTS-friendly**

---

## Before This Chapter

Marius discovered the truth about his father a brave colonel who had watched his son from a church gallery every Sunday for years, never coming forward. After reading his father's letter, Marius broke with his grandfather and went to live in poverty. He joined the ABC Society, a group of revolutionary students led by Enjolras. In the Luxembourg Garden, he fell in love with Cosette. Through a sharp-eyed street girl named Éponine Thénardier, he found Cosette's address on the Rue Plumet. For six weeks they met in secret in the garden. Then Cosette disappeared Valjean moved them away in the night.

When Marius found the empty garden, he felt he had nothing left. He put his father's letter in his pocket. He told Courfeyrac he was coming to the barricade. He went out into the dark streets of Paris.

---

## Scene 1 Paris in June

The summer of 1832 was not a calm summer.

In the months before, there had been many signs. The workers in the factories complained about long hours and low pay. Poor families in the old districts of Paris were getting poorer. The harvest had been bad. Bread was expensive. The prisons were full. And the government, which had been in place since the fall of Napoleon, was not listening.

In June, a general died.

His name was Lamarque. He was not a perfect man. But he was one of the last important men in France who still spoke openly about the rights of ordinary people. He had been ill for some time. Everyone knew he was dying. The students at the Café Musain had talked about his death for months not with pleasure, but with a dark certainty. When Lamarque died, they said, things would break.

He died on the first of June.

The news spread through Paris on a grey morning. People gathered on street corners and read the notice boards. In the poor quarters, small groups of men stood together and spoke quietly. In the wealthy streets, the shop owners pulled their shutters a little lower. The police were out in large numbers, walking in pairs, watching.

The city was holding its breath.

At the Café Musain, the members of the ABC Society read the news and knew the time had come.

---

## Scene 2 The Last Meeting

Enjolras arrived at the café in the early afternoon. He was wearing his usual coat, plain and dark. He did not look excited. He looked the way he always looked calm, certain, as if he had already seen the events of the day in his mind and they were exactly what he had expected.

The others were already there. Courfeyrac was at the table with a newspaper. Combeferre was reading something in the corner. Joly had a cold, as usual, and was drinking hot soup. Bossuet had lost his hat somewhere on the way there. Feuilly, who worked long hours making fans for a living and had spent his evenings educating himself, was writing something quickly in a small notebook.

They were young men all under twenty-five. They had spent many evenings at this table arguing about France and freedom and the future. They had made plans and debated principles and sometimes got so angry with each other that they shouted, and sometimes got so happy that they sang.

Tonight was not a shouting night or a singing night.

Enjolras stood at the end of the table and spoke quietly and directly. He told them what they already knew: Lamarque was dead. The city was tense. The funeral procession was the day after tomorrow. If they were ever going to act, it was now.

He did not ask them to come. He did not threaten them or try to persuade them. He simply said: I am going. This is the plan. This is where we meet. This is what we need.

Then he sat down and drank his coffee.

One by one, the men at the table said: I am coming.

Courfeyrac was the loudest. Joly said yes in between sneezes. Feuilly closed his notebook and nodded. Combeferre asked three careful questions about the plan, got three careful answers, and said yes. Bossuet said yes and then immediately knocked over his own cup.

There was a chair at the end of the table that no one was sitting in. It was the chair that had belonged, once, to a law student who had grown up with twelve francs in his pocket and his father's letter in his coat. That chair was empty.

Courfeyrac looked at it.

"Marius will come," he said. "He always does."

But Enjolras was already thinking about other things.

## Scene 3 The Street

The barricade went up in the Rue de la Chanvrerie.

It was a narrow street in the old part of Paris, in the district they called the Marais. The buildings on either side were tall and close together. The street itself was barely wide enough for a cart to pass. It connected to several other small streets that connected to other small streets, so that the whole area was a kind of maze of passages and courtyards that a person who did not know it well could easily get lost in.

Enjolras had chosen it carefully.

It began on the evening of the fifth of June. At first, it looked like an accident. A cart appeared at the end of the street. Then another. Then a man came out of a building carrying a large wooden table. Then a woman on the upper floor of one of the buildings threw an old mattress from her window. It fell into the street. She did not ask any questions about what it was for. She simply threw it and went back inside.

The cart drivers left their carts. They walked away quickly. They did not look back.

Men with rough hands and quick arms began to move. Paving stones came up from the road. Old furniture appeared chairs, crates, broken doors. A barrel rolled out of a courtyard. A second mattress came from another window. Someone had rope, and wound it through and around the growing pile. Someone else had a ladder.

Within an hour, the barricade was waist-high. Within two hours, it was shoulder-high. By the time the lanterns on the street were lit though nobody had lit them, this evening it was higher than a man's head. It blocked the street completely from one side to the other.

It was solid. It was rough. It was exactly what it was a wall built by people who were not soldiers, from things they had found.

Enjolras climbed to the top and looked out over the dark street. The city was quiet on the other side. But in the distance, there was movement. He could see the glint of helmets.

He climbed down.

"They know we are here," he said.

"Of course they do," said Courfeyrac.

---

## Scene 4 Gavroche

Among the people at the barricade was a boy.

He was perhaps twelve years old. He was small and thin, with a quick face and bright eyes and a general air of someone who has never had very much and has found this to be interesting rather than sad. He moved through the streets of Paris like he owned them which, in some ways, he did, or at least he knew them better than anyone.

His name was Gavroche.

He had no fixed home. He slept where he could in doorways, under bridges, in the great stone elephant on the Place de la Bastille, which was hollow inside and warm in winter if you knew the way in. He ate what he could find or earn or occasionally steal, though he preferred earning. He had a particular talent for knowing what was happening in the city before it appeared in any newspaper, and for being in the middle of events without quite being noticed.

He had arrived at the barricade early in the evening, before most of the men. He had helped carry paving stones without being asked. He had brought bread from somewhere nobody knew where and passed it around. He had climbed to the top of the barricade twice to look out, each time with total calm, as if barricades were a perfectly ordinary feature of the street.

Enjolras sent him on errands. Gavroche went without complaint and came back quickly, reporting what he had seen with the accuracy of someone who had been listening at windows and counting soldiers all his life.

"Three companies," he said, the second time he came back. "They are at the end of the rue Saint-Denis. They have a cannon."

"Good," said Enjolras, which was not the response Gavroche had expected, but which Gavroche accepted with a small shrug.

The men at the barricade liked Gavroche. He was impossible not to like. He was honest in a way that children sometimes are when they have nothing to pretend. He was cheerful in a way that people sometimes are when they have known enough bad things to know that being unhappy about them doesn't help.

He found a rifle that was too big for him and cleaned it methodically and propped it against the barricade. He made it understood, without saying so directly, that the rifle was his.

Nobody argued with him.

## Scene 5 The Girl at the Gate

Late that evening, someone appeared at the barricade who should not have been there.

It was difficult to tell, at first, because of the dark and because the person was wearing a long coat and a cap pulled low. The figure climbed the side of the barricade carefully and sat at the top, looking out over the empty street.

Courfeyrac climbed up beside the figure and looked.

"Who are you?" he said.

The figure turned.

It was a girl.

She was perhaps sixteen, thin, with sharp eyes and cheeks that were hollow from too little food. She had the look of someone who had grown up in the streets not the open, cheerful look of Gavroche, but a harder look, watchful and careful. She was wearing a man's coat that was much too big for her.

"I want to be here," she said. She looked directly at Courfeyrac. She was not asking for permission. She was stating a fact.

Courfeyrac opened his mouth to say something.

Marius had climbed up beside them.

He looked at the girl. "Éponine," he said.

She looked at him. Something changed in her face very slightly, a kind of brightening that she could not completely hide and that she did not completely want to.

"I knew you would come here," she said.

Marius had not expected her. He had not thought about her in weeks not since the day she had given him Cosette's address, her thin hand closing around the coins, her sharp eyes watching him with that unreadable expression. He had thought about little else since then except the garden on the Rue Plumet.

"You should not be here," he said. "It is dangerous."

"I know it is dangerous," she said. "I am here anyway."

There was nothing more to say. She had made her decision before Marius made his. She stayed.

She sat at the top of the barricade through the first part of the night. She did not carry a rifle. She did not try to give orders. She simply sat there, looking out over the dark street, with the patience of someone who has decided to wait for whatever comes.

---

## Scene 6 Javert

Around midnight, Gavroche came back from one of his errands at a run. He was breathing fast, which was unusual. He stopped at the base of the barricade and said: "I found a spy."

The man Gavroche had found was standing at the corner of a side street. He was dressed in plain clothes not the uniform of the police, not the coat of a soldier. He looked like a workman or a merchant, someone who might live in the neighbourhood. But he was not a workman or a merchant. Gavroche had known it immediately, the way street children know things, by some combination of instinct and observation that is difficult to explain.

"The boots," Gavroche said simply.

The man had workman's clothes but a policeman's boots the heavy, polished kind that you do not wear on an ordinary evening walk.

The men from the barricade brought him in.

He came without fighting. He walked calmly between the two men who held his arms, and he looked around the barricade with a steady, professional gaze noting the height, the construction, the positions of the defenders. He was gathering information even as he was being captured.

Enjolras looked at him.

The man looked back. His face was flat and hard and absolutely without fear.

"Your name," said Enjolras.

"Javert," the man said. "Inspector Javert of the Paris police."

He said it as if it were a perfectly ordinary thing to say. As if stating your name to the people who have just captured you is simply a matter of honesty.

"You came to spy on us," Enjolras said.

"Yes," said Javert. He did not look apologetic.

Enjolras considered him for a moment. Then he said: "Tie him to that post. When this is finished, we will deal with him."

They tied Javert to the post at the back of the barricade, near the wall of the building. He stood against it with his shoulders back and his eyes open. He watched everything. He said nothing more.

Marius looked at Javert from across the barricade. Javert did not look back. He was not interested in Marius. He was watching the barricade as a whole calculating.

Marius thought: this is the man who has hunted Valjean for years. This is the man who came to the Gorbeau house, who appeared in the street outside their window. This is the man in the heavy boots.

He turned away.

---

## Scene 7 The First Attack

The attack came before dawn.

There was a sound first the sound of many boots on pavement, moving in unison. Then the sound of orders being called. Then silence, and then a flash of light from the end of the street.

"Down!" Enjolras shouted.

The first shots hit the barricade cracking into the wood and stone, sending chips flying. The men crouched behind the structure. Gavroche, who was crouched at the top with his rifle that was too big for him, returned fire with total calmness. He shot once, ducked, moved three metres to the left, appeared again, shot again.

Courfeyrac was shouting. Joly was bleeding from a cut above his eye but was still fighting. Combeferre was loading rifles and passing them along the line. Bossuet, who had been unlucky his whole life, was having the most accurate shots of the group.

The first attack lasted perhaps twenty minutes.

It felt like much longer.

Then the sound of the boots retreated. The shouting stopped. The street went quiet again.

The men looked at each other. They had held. No one was dead. Joly's cut was not serious. The barricade had a new hole in it on the left side, which two men quickly repaired with a cabinet door and a length of rope.

Enjolras walked the length of the barricade. He checked everything. He spoke to each man briefly. His face was as calm as it had been at the café.

"They will come back," he said. "Stronger."

Nobody disagreed.

They settled in to wait. The night was very dark. The street was very quiet. Somewhere on the other side of the city, Paris was sleeping.

---

## Scene 8 Éponine's Last Act

It happened just before the second attack.

There was a moment of stillness the kind of stillness that comes between things. The men were resting, drinking from a water bucket that Gavroche had found somewhere, talking quietly. Marius was sitting against the base of the barricade with his rifle across his knees, thinking about Cosette.

He was thinking about the garden. About the stone bench and the last evening when it was still warm. About the way she had said *since the first day*.

He heard a sound. Very small a soft sound, like something settling.

He looked up.

At the top of the barricade, a soldier had appeared. He had come up quietly from the other side, moving through the shadow. He was very close. He was raising his rifle.

The rifle was pointing at Marius.

And then something stepped between them. Something thin, in a large coat.

The shot was very loud.

Marius was on his feet immediately. He climbed to the top of the barricade. The soldier had retreated back into the dark. But at the top of the barricade, a figure was lying across the stones.

He reached her. It was Éponine.

She had been shot. The bullet had gone through her hand and into her chest. Her hand was pressed flat against the wound. She was breathing, but each breath was short and effortful.

Marius lifted her and carried her down from the barricade. He sat on the ground with her in his arms. She weighed almost nothing. She had always been too thin.

She was looking up at him. Her eyes were very clear. There was no panic in them.

"That was for you," she said. Her voice was quiet and steady.

"Why?" he said. He was not thinking clearly. "Why did you do that?"

She looked at him for a moment. "Because I wanted to," she said. It was a simple answer. She said it without self-pity or drama.

He held her. She did not struggle. She was still looking at him.

"I have something for you," she said after a moment. "In my coat. Left side. There is a letter."

He reached into her coat with one hand, keeping the other arm around her. He found a small, folded piece of paper. He took it out.

"A girl gave it to me," Éponine said. "Some time ago. She asked me to deliver it to you. I kept it." She paused. Her breathing was more difficult now. "I am sorry I kept it. I should have given it to you sooner."

Marius looked at the folded paper. He could see handwriting on the outside neat, careful handwriting. He knew it.

"Éponine," he said. He could not find anything else to say.

She was still looking at him. The look was very direct.

"It is all right," she said. "I think I am going somewhere warmer than this street." She said it without irony. She seemed to mean it sincerely.

Her eyes stayed on his face for a little while longer. Then they stopped focusing. The thin body in his arms became still.

Marius sat on the ground of the barricade and held her for a moment. Then he laid her down carefully, on her back, on the stones. He straightened her coat. He looked at her face, which was young and sharp and still.

Then he stood up and opened the letter.

## Scene 9 The Letter

It was from Cosette. He knew it before he read the first word.

She had written it some weeks before. It was not long. It was the kind of letter a person writes when they are afraid they will have no other chance to speak.

She wrote that she did not know where they were going or when she would be back. She wrote that Valjean had told her they had to move, quickly, at night, and she had obeyed him because she trusted him. She wrote that she did not know if Marius would ever read this. She wrote that she thought about him every day. She wrote: *since the first day.*

Those three words again. The same three words she had said in the garden.

She wrote that she did not know where to find him, but that she would give this letter to the only person she could think of the girl who had found Valjean's address for Marius, who seemed to know the streets. She hoped the letter would reach him.

She gave, at the end, an address. It was in the north of Paris, near the Champs-Élysées. A house with a small garden. She wrote: *if you are ever looking for us, we are here.*

Marius read the letter twice. He folded it and put it in his pocket, next to his father's letter.

He found Gavroche.

Gavroche was eating a piece of sausage and watching the end of the street. He looked up.

"I need you to take a letter," Marius said.

Gavroche looked at him. Then he looked at Éponine, lying still at the base of the barricade.

"Who was she?" he said.

"A brave person," Marius said. It was the most honest thing he could say.

Gavroche nodded slowly. He was young, but he was not a stranger to death. He had seen it before. He knew when it was time to ask questions and when it was time to be quiet.

"Who does the letter go to?" he said.

"A house near the Champs-Élysées," Marius said. He wrote the address on a piece of paper. He tore a page from his notebook the only paper he had and wrote quickly. He wrote to Cosette. He told her where he was. He told her what he was doing and why. He told her he was sorry he could not say goodbye in person. He told her he was glad she was safe. He wrote: *Whatever happens tonight, I am glad I met you. I am glad I called your name in the garden. I am glad you left the gate ajar.*

He folded the paper. He gave it to Gavroche with the address.

"Take this tonight," he said. "Before the next attack. Leave now and don't come back."

Gavroche looked at the letter. Then he looked at the barricade, at the men he had been working with, at his rifle propped against the stone.

"I'll go," he said. "But I'm coming back."

Marius said nothing.

Gavroche tucked the letter into his shirt, picked up his rifle, climbed the barricade, and disappeared over the other side into the dark.

---

## Scene 10 The Man Who Appeared

Around two in the morning, a man appeared at the barricade.

He came from inside from the street that the barricade protected, not from the soldiers' side. He climbed up over the back with no noise, then down the front, and stood in the middle of the men as if he had been there all along.

He was old or at least not young. Tall, with white hair and large hands. He was wearing ordinary clothes. He had a rifle.

Nobody had seen him arrive. Nobody knew how he had got there. The street behind the barricade was empty and watched.

Courfeyrac looked at him. "Who are you?" he said.

"A man who wants to help," the man said. His voice was deep and calm.

Courfeyrac looked at Enjolras. Enjolras looked at the man. There was a long moment.

"Do you know how to fight?" Enjolras said.

"I know how to be useful," the man said.

Enjolras nodded. He had no time for long investigations.

Marius looked at the old man across the barricade. Something was familiar about him the height, the way he held himself, the particular quality of stillness that seemed like a physical thing. He could not place it.

The old man did not look at Marius.

He took a position near the left side of the barricade and stood there quietly, watching the dark street. He was competent it was visible in his posture, in the way he held the rifle, in the way his eyes moved along the shadows.

He was not a revolutionary. He was not there for the cause. Marius could see this, though he could not explain how. He was there for another reason entirely.

Marius turned away and looked at his own rifle and thought about Cosette's letter in his pocket.

---

## Scene 11 Javert

At around three in the morning, Enjolras walked to the post where Javert was tied.

The attack would come before dawn. Everyone knew this. There was no more waiting. When it came, there would be no time for prisoners.

Enjolras looked at Javert.

Javert looked back. His face was unchanged. He had been standing tied to the post for hours. He looked uncomfortable but not afraid.

"We have no more time for prisoners," Enjolras said. "And no space in the barricade for guards."

Javert said nothing.

Enjolras looked around. "Someone must take him and "

"I will do it," said a voice.

It was the old man with white hair.

Enjolras looked at him. The old man had not spoken for hours. He had simply been there, useful and quiet.

"You are sure?" Enjolras said.

"Yes," the man said.

Enjolras untied the rope from the post. He handed Javert's arms to the old man. The old man held them with one hand and held a pistol with the other.

They went through a door in the side of the building an inner passage that led to a small courtyard at the back.

In the courtyard, the old man stopped. He looked at Javert.

Javert looked back. In the darkness of the courtyard, with no one watching, his face was different. Something had come into it not fear, but something adjacent to it. A kind of waiting.

The old man pointed the pistol. He looked at Javert for a long moment.

Then he fired the pistol into the air.

The shot echoed in the courtyard. Then silence.

The old man lowered the pistol. He looked at Javert.

Javert stared at him. He did not understand.

"Go," the man said.

Javert did not move.

"Go," the man said again. "Walk north. Don't stop walking until you are away from here."

"Why?" Javert said. It was barely a word. He said it as if the question had come out before he could stop it.

The old man did not answer. He turned and walked back through the door, back toward the barricade.

Javert stood alone in the dark courtyard for a moment. He looked at the door. He looked at the walls. He looked at the open passage that led to the street.

Then he walked through the passage and into the street. He walked north. He did not stop.

---

## Scene 12 The Long Night

After that, the barricade was quiet for a while.

The men rested. Some of them slept the kind of short, shallow sleep that you can do sitting against a wall with a rifle in your hands. Some of them talked, very quietly. Combeferre recited a poem from memory something about flowers and the light of morning and nobody told him to stop. Joly had cleaned the cut above his eye and was no longer bleeding. Bossuet had found his hat.

Courfeyrac sat beside Marius.

"Are you all right?" he said.

"No," Marius said.

Courfeyrac looked at Éponine, still lying at the base of the barricade. Then he looked at Marius.

"She saved your life," he said.

"Yes," Marius said.

"Do you know why?"

Marius looked at his hands. "Yes," he said.

Courfeyrac was quiet for a moment. "She was a good person," he said. "In difficult circumstances."

"Yes," Marius said again.

They were quiet after that.

The old man with white hair sat a little way apart from the others. He was not sleeping. He was looking at Marius. Marius did not notice. He was reading Cosette's letter for the third time, running his finger over the words in the dark.

The old man watched him and said nothing.

He had come here for Marius. He had seen the farewell letter the letter Marius had sent to Cosette, which Gavroche had delivered. He had gone to the address in the north of Paris. He had knocked at the door late in the evening. Cosette had opened it, read the letter, and looked at him with wide, frightened eyes.

He had made a decision then. He had told her to stay inside and lock the door. He had told her he would come back.

Then he had gone to find the barricade.

He did not know what he was doing or why. He knew only that this young man this thin, serious young man who had been in Cosette's garden, who had given her a reason to look forward to evenings was going to die on this street if no one helped him. And that Cosette had looked at him with those large, frightened eyes that were so much like her mother's eyes.

He had not been able to walk away.

---

## Scene 13 Gavroche

Gavroche came back.

Marius had told him not to. But he came back, just before dawn, climbing over the top of the barricade with the quiet efficiency of someone who has spent his whole life going over walls.

"The letter is delivered," he said. He said it directly to Marius, then turned to look at the street.

Marius felt something release in his chest. He nodded.

Dawn was coming. The sky above the narrow rooftops was turning from black to dark grey. In an hour, it would be light.

Enjolras called the men together. He spoke quietly and directly, as he always did. The attack, when it came, would be in force. They had held the first attack. They might hold the second. But the soldiers had numbers and weapons and the full authority of the government behind them. Enjolras did not pretend otherwise.

He said: whatever happens, we were here. We built this. That has a meaning.

The men looked at him. Some of them nodded. Some of them were quiet.

Then, on the far side of the barricade, a rifle cracked. A soldier had come forward alone, perhaps to test the defences. The shot went over the barricade.

Gavroche looked at the barricade. They were running low on cartridges the small metal cases that hold the gunpowder and the ball. There was a basket of them, dropped by the soldiers during the first attack, lying in the street on the other side.

He looked at the basket. He looked at the barricade. He looked at the street.

"I'll get them," he said.

"Gavroche " Combeferre began.

But Gavroche had already gone. He went over the barricade and into the open street, moving fast and low. He reached the basket. He filled his pockets. He was fast, but the street was long.

The shots started.

Gavroche kept moving. He was dancing, almost ducking, changing direction, keeping low. He was singing something. Nobody could hear what it was. The sound of shooting was too loud.

He reached the barricade. He threw the cartridges over and grabbed the top to pull himself up.

He was almost over when the last shot came.

He fell.

The men on the barricade went very still.

Courfeyrac went over the barricade before anyone could stop him. He came back carrying Gavroche. He laid him down at the base of the barricade.

Gavroche was dead. He was small. His hands were still full of cartridges.

The men stood around him for a moment in silence. Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say. He had been twelve years old, perhaps, and he had died in the street with his pockets full of cartridges for other people's rifles.

Enjolras bent down and covered him with a coat.

Then he stood up. "Take your positions," he said. His voice was steady.

They took their positions.

---

## Scene 14 The Final Assault

The sun rose.

With it came the soldiers.

They came from both ends of the street, which meant they had found a way around the barricade, through the side passages. There were more of them than before many more. They had a cannon now. They set it up at the far end of the street.

Enjolras looked at the cannon. He looked at his men. He looked at the barricade.

"The ground floor of the building," he said. "Everyone who is still fighting goes to the ground floor. Use the windows."

The men climbed down from the barricade and moved into the building. It was an old wine shop the Café Corinthe. Dark inside, with wooden tables and barrels and the smell of old wine. The windows faced the street. The men took up positions behind them.

The cannon fired.

The barricade shook. Part of the left side came down. Stone dust and splinters filled the air.

The cannon fired again. The barricade shook again.

Then the soldiers came forward.

The fighting became close and confused. The men in the windows shot at the soldiers in the street. The soldiers shot at the windows. Men fell. Combeferre, who had been loading and passing rifles all night, was shot at a window. Joly fell back from his position and did not get up again. Bossuet, who had never had good luck in his life, fired twelve shots before he was hit.

Courfeyrac fought until there was nothing left to fight with.

Enjolras was the last.

He was standing in the middle of the room when the soldiers came through the door. He put down his rifle. He stood straight. He did not look afraid. He looked at the soldiers with the same calm certainty that he had always had.

They shot him where he stood.

He fell against the wall. He slid down slowly. His face, even then, did not change.

---

## Scene 15 Marius Falls

Marius was at the window of the ground floor when something hit him.

He did not feel it at first. There was a sound, and then his left arm was not working properly, and then his leg. He tried to keep standing. He could not.

He went down. The floor came up toward him. His face was against the stone floor and he could see the legs of a table and the feet of a man lying nearby and the dust in the air from the cannon shot.

He thought: Cosette.

He thought: I should have told her more clearly.

He thought: I am glad I came.

Then he thought nothing.

---

## Scene 16 The Sewers

There were hands under him.

Large hands, strong, lifting him from the floor. The movement hurt a deep, distant pain that told him something was very wrong with his body, but that was also muffled, as if the pain were on the other side of a thick wall.

He was being carried.

He could not see properly. The light was wrong too dark, then too bright. He had the impression of moving very fast and then of a door. A street. Then darkness again, but a different kind of darkness. Wet and closed.

The sewers of Paris.

He knew it without seeing it, from the smell stone and water and the accumulated history of the city, all compressed into a long, low passage under the ground. The man carrying him was moving fast despite the weight. Marius could hear his breathing steady, controlled, the breathing of a man who knows what he is doing and is doing it.

The passage was narrow and the water was ankle-deep. The man did not slow down.

They went on like that for a long time.

Marius drifted in and out. There were moments when he was aware of the passage around him the low ceiling, the water sound, the dark. And moments when there was nothing.

At one point he heard a voice. The voice of the man carrying him, speaking very quietly, almost to himself.

"Not yet," the man said. "Not yet."

Marius did not know if the man was talking to him or to himself or to God or to no one at all.

He closed his eyes.

## Scene 17 Daylight

Then there was light.

Not the sudden, total light of the street, but a grey, growing light the light of a morning that has not yet decided whether it will be bright or cloudy.

They had come out of the sewer.

There was a grate a heavy iron grate at the end of the passage, which opened onto the bank of the river. The man had stopped here. He was working at the lock. Marius lay against the curved wall of the passage, breathing, not quite conscious.

The grate opened.

The man lifted Marius again and carried him through.

They were on the bank of the Seine. The river was grey and wide and quiet. The city was still sleeping. A bird crossed the sky above the rooftops.

The man set Marius down carefully, on the stone bank. He straightened up. He looked at the young man he had been carrying at the pale face and the wounded arm and the coat that was dark with blood.

He looked for a long time.

Then a voice came from behind him.

"Stop."

He turned.

A man in a coat was standing at the top of the bank. He was still. He was watching with flat, expressionless eyes. He had been waiting here. He had known where the sewer opened.

"Javert," said Valjean.

He said it quietly. Without surprise. He had known, somewhere, that this was how it would end.

Javert looked at him. He looked at the young man on the ground. He looked at Valjean's hands, which were empty.

He was silent for a long moment.

"This young man needs a doctor," Valjean said. "His name is Marius Pontmercy. His grandfather lives in the Marais Monsieur Gillenormand. Take the boy there. Or let me take him."

Javert looked at Marius. He looked at Valjean.

Something was happening in his face. Something that Javert had never shown before a kind of confusion. The rigid certainty that had always been there was not entirely there anymore. It had been shaken. It had been shaken the night before, in the dark courtyard, when this same man had held a pistol to his head and fired into the air.

Javert had always known what was right and wrong. The law was right. Everything outside the law was wrong. It was simple. It had always been simple.

But the man in front of him had saved his life. Twice, now once in the courtyard, and once at Montreuil-sur-Mer, years ago, pulling a cart from an old man's body. He had saved his life and then gone back into danger to carry a wounded stranger through the sewers of Paris.

Javert did not know what to do with this.

"Take him," he said. His voice was strange. It sounded like a voice that had been used a certain way for forty years and was suddenly being asked to do something different.

Valjean turned back to Marius. He lifted him. He began to walk.

He walked along the bank of the river in the grey morning light. Marius was unconscious in his arms. The city was waking up around them a cart appeared on a bridge, a baker opened a window.

Valjean walked.

Behind him, on the bank, Javert stood and watched until they were out of sight.

Then he turned and looked at the river.

He stood there for a long time, looking at the water.

## Grammar Focus

### Narrative Tenses Three Uses

**Past Simple** completed actions in the story:
- *Gavroche went over the barricade.*
- *The cannon fired.*
- *He fell.*

**Past Continuous** background actions happening at the same time:
- *The men were resting while Enjolras was checking the barricade.*
- *Éponine was sitting at the top, watching the dark street.*

**Past Perfect** something that happened before another past event:
- *He had come here for Marius.* (= he came to the barricade, but this happened before the scene being described)
- *She had always been too thin.* (= she was thin long before this moment)

### "Despite" + noun contrast without "but"

- *He moved fast **despite** the weight.* (= although Marius was heavy)
- *She sat calmly **despite** the danger.*
- *He came back **despite** being told not to.*

Compare: *He was heavy, **but** the man moved fast.*
Both sentences mean the same thing. "Despite" is more formal and efficient.

### Reported Speech past reporting verbs

In this chapter, characters report what was said without using direct quotes:
- *Enjolras called the men together. He told them the attack would come in force.*
- *He said that they had held the first attack. He said they might hold the second.*
- *She said she wanted to be there.* (direct = "I want to be here.")

Note: when reporting in the past, present verbs shift back:
- "I **want**" → she said she **wanted**
- "We **are** going" → he said they **were** going
- "It **will** come" → he told them it **would** come
