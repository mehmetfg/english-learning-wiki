# Les Misérables Chapter 10: The End of the Road

**Graded Reader | A2/B1 | ~10,000 words | TTS-friendly**

---

## Before This Chapter

The barricade in the Rue de la Chanvrerie fell at dawn. The students fought until there was nothing left to fight with. Marius was shot and lay unconscious on the floor of the wine shop. Jean Valjean who had appeared at the barricade without being asked carried Marius through the sewers of Paris. He came out on the bank of the Seine as the city was waking up.

Inspector Javert was waiting at the sewer exit. He had known where it opened. He had been standing there in the grey morning light with his flat, expressionless eyes. But something had broken in him the night before, when Valjean had held a pistol to his head and fired into the air instead of pulling the trigger. He let Valjean pass.

Valjean walked through the waking streets of Paris, carrying Marius Pontmercy, until he reached the house of Monsieur Gillenormand Marius's grandfather in the Marais. He knocked at the door.

Marius was alive. But only barely.

---

## Scene 1 Javert by the River

After Valjean disappeared around the corner, Javert stood on the bank of the Seine and did not move.

The river was running fast and dark. The morning light was cold and grey. There were already people on the bridge workers crossing, a woman with a basket, a cart loaded with vegetables. The city was waking up and moving forward the way it always did, as if nothing had happened. As if there had been no barricade, no night of shooting, no boys dying in a narrow street.

Javert looked at the river.

He had been a policeman for forty years. In those forty years, he had never questioned his work. The law was the law. It was not cruel and it was not kind it was simply what it was, and he was its instrument. He had believed this completely. It was the only thing he had ever believed completely.

Last night, a man had held a pistol to his head and let him go.

The man was a convict. He was a criminal who had broken his parole and spent twenty years running from the police. He was, according to every principle Javert had built his life on, wrong. He was on the wrong side of every line that Javert had ever drawn.

And yet.

Javert tried to think about what had happened in the courtyard. He tried to understand it the way he understood everything through rules, through categories, through the clean logic of who was right and who was wrong.

He could not.

The man had saved his life. Not because he had to. Not because anyone was watching. He had done it because because why? Because he did not want Javert to die? Because it was not in him to shoot a man in cold blood? Because something in him, even after everything that had been done to him, still operated by rules that were different from the law but also not entirely unlike it?

Javert did not know what to do with this.

He had always known what to do. That was the one thing he had always been sure of. And now he was standing on the bank of the river at dawn, and he did not know.

He walked along the bank for a long time. Back and forth. His boots were wet from the sewer. He did not notice.

He thought about going to the police station. He thought about writing his report. He thought about Valjean where he was now, where the wounded young man had been taken, what a report would mean.

He walked to the police station. He sat at his desk. He took out a piece of paper. He wrote the date. He wrote the first line of a report.

He looked at the first line for a long time.

Then he put down the pen. He put on his coat. He walked back to the river.

He stood on the bridge.

He looked at the water a long time.

Then, at last, he stepped off.

The river was very fast. The city continued.

## Scene 2 The House in the Marais

The door of the Gillenormand house opened at the second knock. A servant looked out at the old man standing on the step holding a young man who was very clearly badly hurt. The servant's mouth opened. He called into the house.

Within a minute there were three people at the door. Then five. Gillenormand himself came down the stairs, holding the banister and moving carefully, because he was ninety-one years old and the stairs were steep, but moving as fast as he could because someone had shouted the name Marius.

He came to the door. He looked at Marius's pale face, at the dark stain on his coat, at the closed eyes.

He made a sound that was not a word.

He had spent three years being angry at his grandson. Three years refusing to talk about him. Three years telling himself that the boy had been foolish and ungrateful and that it was his own fault and that he, Gillenormand, had no regrets.

He had regrets.

"Bring him inside," he said. His voice was shaking.

They brought Marius inside and carried him up to a room. They laid him on a bed. Someone ran for a doctor. The doctor came quickly the neighbourhood was good and the house was known, and doctors came quickly to known houses.

The doctor examined Marius for a long time. Then he came out of the room and spoke to Gillenormand in the corridor.

He said: the young man has been shot twice. One bullet went through his arm. One is lodged near his left shoulder. He has lost a great deal of blood. He is young and strong. These are both advantages. The next twenty-four hours will tell us more.

Gillenormand listened. He nodded. He thanked the doctor. He went back to the door of Marius's room and stood in the doorway and looked at the still figure on the bed.

He stood there for a long time.

On the stairs, the old man who had brought Marius sat down on a step. He was very tired. He had been awake all night. He had carried a man through a kilometre of sewer and through the streets of Paris at dawn. He was not young.

He sat on the step. He put his hands on his knees. He breathed.

After a while, Gillenormand came back to the stairs. He looked down at the man sitting on the step. He looked at the large hands and the white hair and the face that showed nothing except tiredness.

"Who are you?" Gillenormand said.

The man looked up. "A friend of the family," he said. "I happened to be nearby."

Gillenormand looked at him for a moment. He was ninety-one and he had been a shrewd man all his life. He could see that this answer was not the whole truth. But he could also see that the man was exhausted and that Marius was alive and that questions could wait.

"Come," he said. "There is a room. You can rest."

The man stood. "Thank you," he said. "But I have to go. There is someone I need to see."

He went down the stairs and out of the door.

Gillenormand watched him go. Then he went back to his grandson's room.

## Scene 3 Cosette Learns

Valjean went directly to the house near the Champs-Élysées.

He had left Cosette there the night before. He had told her to lock the door and not open it for anyone except him. She had looked at him with wide, frightened eyes and done what he said. She had spent the night sitting in the front room with a candle, listening to the distant sounds of the city the shouting, the shots, the silence that came after and not sleeping.

He knocked. She opened the door before the second knock.

She looked at him. She looked at his coat. She looked at his face.

"Is Marius alive?" she said.

"Yes," he said. "He is at his grandfather's house. He was hurt, but the doctor says he is strong."

She put one hand on the door frame. She stood very still for a moment. Then she said: "Take me to him."

"Not yet," Valjean said. "Let the doctor work. He needs to sleep. Tomorrow."

She looked at him. He could see that she wanted to argue she wanted to put on her coat and go immediately, that very moment, through the streets. He could see it in her face, which was so clear that she had never been able to hide anything from him.

"Tomorrow," he said again.

She nodded. She let him in.

They sat together in the front room. She made tea and he drank it without tasting it. She did not ask him where he had been or what he had done. She understood that he had found Marius and brought him to safety. The details did not matter yet.

After a while she said, very quietly: "He is really alive?"

"Really," Valjean said. "He is in a bed in a warm house with a doctor who has seen worse."

She wrapped her hands around her cup.

"And the others?" she said. "The students he was with?"

Valjean was quiet for a moment. "Most of them did not come through," he said. He said it as gently as he could. There was no gentle way to say it.

Cosette was quiet. She looked at the candle.

"There was a boy," she said after a moment. She had read Marius's letter carefully. "He mentioned a boy called Gavroche."

"He was very brave," Valjean said.

Cosette understood. She did not ask any more questions.

They sat together until morning. Neither of them slept.

---

## Scene 4 The Bedside

The next afternoon, Valjean brought Cosette to the Gillenormand house.

The servant showed them up to Marius's room. Gillenormand was sitting in a chair by the bed when they came in. He looked at Cosette. Cosette looked at him. They had never met before.

Gillenormand was a sharp man. He looked at the young woman in front of him the dark eyes, the composed face, the way she was looking at his grandson on the bed with an expression that was entirely unambiguous and he understood immediately who she was and what she was to Marius.

He stood up from the chair.

"Sit here," he said, indicating the chair. "He will know you are there."

Cosette sat. She took Marius's hand carefully, because his left arm was bandaged from the shoulder to the wrist. She held his hand and looked at his face.

Gillenormand watched her for a moment. Then he left the room and went to sit in the corridor, where he could see through the open door.

He sat there for most of the afternoon.

He thought about his grandson. About the Sunday mornings he had spent bringing the child to church in Paris, not knowing that a man was watching from the gallery above the child's own father, who had given up everything so the boy would have a better life. He had known about the father's visits eventually, because gossip travels in old buildings. He had not mentioned it. He had not known what to say.

He thought about the night Marius left. He could still hear the door closing. He had not run after him. He had sat at the dinner table and been angry, which was the easier emotion.

He thought: I have wasted too much time being angry.

Marius opened his eyes for the first time that evening. He was confused at first he looked at the ceiling, then at the window, then at the hand holding his.

He turned his head.

He looked at Cosette.

He said nothing for a moment. He seemed to be checking that she was real the way a person does when they have wanted something very badly and have been afraid of being wrong.

"Cosette," he said. His voice was very rough and quiet.

"Yes," she said. "I am here."

He looked at her for a long time. Then he closed his eyes again. But his hand tightened around hers.

She stayed.

## Scene 5 Recovery

The weeks that followed were slow and quiet.

Marius's recovery was not simple. He had lost a great deal of blood. The wound to his shoulder was deep. For the first week, he had a fever, and there were two nights when the doctor came at midnight and looked serious. Cosette sat by the bed on both nights and did not leave. She held his hand and talked to him quietly about the garden on the Rue Plumet, about the Luxembourg Garden, about the stone bench, about small ordinary things that she wanted him to come back to.

He came back.

The fever broke on the seventh morning. He opened his eyes and they were clear. He asked for water. He drank it. He looked at Cosette and said: "You look tired."

"You look worse," she said.

He almost smiled.

After that, the recovery was gradual but real. He ate a little more each day. He sat up, first with help, then alone. He began to speak in proper sentences rather than fragments. He asked questions about the barricade, about Enjolras, about Gavroche. Cosette answered as gently as she could.

He was quiet for a long time after she told him about Gavroche.

"He was the bravest of us," Marius said.

"Yes," said Cosette.

Marius looked at the window. "He came back. I told him not to come back, and he came back."

"That is who he was," Cosette said.

He nodded slowly. He was learning how to carry things that could not be put down. He was learning this the way everyone learns it not all at once, but gradually, in the daily practice of continuing to live.

Gillenormand came to the room every morning. He sat in his chair by the door and talked. He talked a great deal about the old days, about his own youth, about funny things that had happened in the neighbourhood fifty years ago. He did not talk about the years of silence or about Marius's father or about the night Marius had left. Those things were there, behind the talking. But the talking was also real, and Marius could hear in his grandfather's voice something that had not been there before a kind of humility, a kind of relief.

One morning, Gillenormand came in and sat down and looked at his grandson for a moment without saying anything. Then he said: "I am glad you are alive. I should have said that earlier. I have said a great many things I should not have said. I am sorry."

He said it quickly, in the manner of an old man who is not used to apologies and is getting them over with before he loses his nerve.

Marius looked at him. "I am glad to be here," he said. He said it simply, and he meant it.

Gillenormand nodded. He cleared his throat. He began talking about the neighbourhood again.

## Scene 6 The Question

When Marius was strong enough to sit in a chair by the window, he asked Gillenormand for a conversation.

Gillenormand sat down across from him. He had the look of a man who expects either very good news or very bad news and is prepared to receive either with dignity.

"There is a young woman," Marius said.

"I know," said Gillenormand.

"Her name is Cosette. I want to marry her."

Gillenormand was quiet for a moment. He looked at his hands, which were old and spotted and had done everything a man could do in ninety-one years.

"The young woman who has been sitting at your bed for three weeks," he said.

"Yes," said Marius.

"The young woman who has not gone home to sleep on the two nights when the fever was very bad."

"Yes," said Marius.

Gillenormand was quiet for another moment.

"Her guardian," he said. "The old man who brought you here. What do you know about him?"

Marius hesitated. It was a short hesitation, but Gillenormand noticed it. "He is a good man," Marius said. "He has been he has taken care of Cosette since she was eight years old. Her mother was not able to." He paused. "There are things about him I do not entirely understand. But he is a good man. That I am sure of."

Gillenormand nodded slowly. He had his own thoughts about the man with white hair who had brought his grandson to his door. The man with the large hands and the expression that gave nothing away. He had his thoughts and he had decided, for now, not to voice them.

"Very well," he said.

Marius looked at him. "Very well yes?"

"Yes," Gillenormand said. He said it with some difficulty, because he was an old man who had had strong opinions about everything his whole life, and having a strong opinion and then setting it aside required considerable effort. "Yes. Bring the girl. Bring her guardian. We will discuss the details like civilised people."

He stood up. He straightened his coat.

"And Marius," he said.

"Yes?"

"I am glad you asked."

He went out before Marius could see his face.

## Scene 7 The Wedding

They were married in February, on a cold clear morning.

The church was not large. The guests were not many this was not the kind of wedding that announces itself to the whole neighbourhood. It was a quiet ceremony with plain flowers and a priest who spoke clearly and a small group of people who were genuinely happy.

Gillenormand was there. He had dressed with great care his best coat, his good shoes, a white flower in his buttonhole. He sat in the front row and looked at the proceedings with the expression of a man who has come through a great deal and arrived at something worth arriving at.

Cosette was wearing a white dress that she had chosen herself and a small wreath of white flowers in her hair. She was not vain about how she looked, and she had not spent a great deal of time on the choice she had pointed at the dress and said that one, and that had been the end of it. She was radiant in the way that people are when they are exactly where they want to be.

Marius was pale still the recovery had been long and he was thinner than before. But his eyes were clear and his hand, when he took Cosette's, was steady.

Valjean was there. He sat near the back, slightly apart from the others. He was wearing a good coat and his white hair was combed. He watched the ceremony with an expression that was very still and very complete the expression of a man who has arrived somewhere he was not sure he would arrive and who is trying to fix the image in his memory.

When the priest spoke the words, and Marius and Cosette answered them, Valjean watched.

When Cosette turned, briefly, and looked toward the back of the church to find him, he was there. He met her eyes. He nodded, very slightly. She smiled.

Then she turned back. The ceremony continued.

After the church, there was a small dinner at the Gillenormand house. Gillenormand had arranged it himself and had been very involved in the menu and the table setting and had driven the cook to the point of giving notice, but had also apologised afterward and the cook had stayed. There was good food and good wine and the table was warm and bright.

Valjean sat at one end. He ate a little. He listened to the conversations around him. He watched Cosette, who was laughing at something Gillenormand said and who looked who looked happy. He had thought about this many times. He had spent years thinking about what it would mean when Cosette was happy in a way that did not require him. He had known it would come. He had thought he was ready.

He found, sitting at the end of the table, that he was and he wasn't. Both at the same time.

He left before the others. He said he was tired. Cosette walked with him to the door. She took his arm.

"You don't have to go," she said.

"I am a little tired," he said. He smiled. It was a real smile.

She kissed his cheek. "Come on Sunday," she said. "Come for dinner. Every Sunday."

"I will," he said.

He put on his coat and went out into the cold February street. He walked home through the empty streets.

He was happy. He was also, for the first time in a long time, aware of being very alone.

## Scene 8 The Truth

Two weeks after the wedding, Marius asked Valjean to come and see him alone.

Cosette was at Gillenormand's house for the afternoon. Marius sat across from Valjean in the front room of the house on the Rue des Filles-du-Calvaire, where he and Cosette now lived. It was a warm room Cosette had made it warm, with rugs and books and a plant by the window. Marius sat in his chair and Valjean sat across from him, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

Marius said: "I want to know who you are."

Valjean looked at him. He had expected this question. He had been expecting it for a long time.

He sat quietly for a moment. Then he said: "My name is Jean Valjean. I was born in 1769 in a village near Brie. I had no education. I was very poor. When I was twenty-five, I stole a loaf of bread for my sister's children, because they were starving. I was caught. I was sentenced to five years in the prison at Toulon."

He paused.

"The sentence became nineteen years," he said. "Because I tried to escape. Four times. Each time added to the sentence. I left Toulon in 1815."

Marius was listening without expression.

"I was given a yellow passport," Valjean continued. "A yellow passport means the police know who you are and watch you everywhere you go. Nobody will give you work or let you sleep in their house. I spent days walking from village to village. Then I came to Digne and a bishop took me in."

He stopped again. He looked at his hands.

"The bishop was the first person in nineteen years who treated me like a man," he said. "I stole from him. I stole his silver. The police brought me back. He told them he had given it to me. He gave me the candlesticks as well." He lifted his eyes. "You have seen the candlesticks."

"Yes," Marius said. "Cosette has always kept them."

"I took a new name," Valjean said. "I went north and started a factory and became the mayor of Montreuil-sur-Mer. I tried to become a different person. I was not sure that was possible. For a while I thought it was."

He told Marius about Fantine. About the dismissal from the factory, and the hospital, and the promise. About going to find Cosette at the Thénardier inn. About coming to Paris, and the Gorbeau house, and the convent.

He told him everything, plainly and without softening. He told him about Javert, and the years of running, and why they had moved so many times. He told him who the old man in the Luxembourg Garden had been, and why he had watched the young man on the bench with such attention.

When he finished, the room was quiet.

Marius was looking at the floor. He was thinking. Valjean waited.

After a long time, Marius said: "The man who carried me through the sewers."

"Yes," Valjean said.

Marius looked up. "That was you."

"Yes."

Another silence.

"Why?" Marius said.

Valjean thought about this carefully, because it deserved a careful answer. "Because Cosette loves you," he said. "And because I promised her mother that she would have a good life. A good life includes being loved by someone who loves her back." He paused. "And because you were there, and I could."

Marius looked at him for a long time.

"What do you want from me?" Marius said.

"Nothing," Valjean said. "I am not telling you this to ask for something. I am telling you because you asked, and because Cosette is your wife, and you should know who you have allowed into your home."

He stood. He put on his hat. He looked at Marius.

"I am a convict," he said. "I have broken my parole. If that matters to you if you decide it matters I will understand. I only ask that you do not tell Cosette. Not all of it. It would hurt her in a way she has not earned."

He went to the door.

"Thank you," Marius said. It came out before he had quite decided to say it.

Valjean stopped. He looked back.

"For what?" he said.

Marius could not explain it all. Not at once. "For bringing me home," he said.

Valjean nodded. He went out.

## Scene 9 The Distance

After that conversation, Marius changed.

He did not do it deliberately. He was not a cold man or a cruel man. He had values he believed in honesty and justice and doing what was right. But he was also twenty-two years old and had grown up in a house where the law was a simple and absolute thing, and the idea of a man with a convict's past living quietly near his wife and dining with them every Sunday was something his mind turned over and over without settling.

He did not ban Valjean from the house. He was not capable of that. But he found reasons why Sunday dinners were not convenient. He forgot to send the usual note. He mentioned to Cosette that perhaps Monsieur Fauchelevent he still used that name was the kind of man who preferred to be on his own. He suggested that the visits might be once a month instead of every week.

Cosette noticed.

She did not say anything at first. She trusted Marius and she trusted that he had his reasons. But she noticed that the visits became less frequent. She noticed that when she suggested they visit Valjean, Marius always had another engagement. She noticed that her father's name came up in conversation less and less.

She wrote Valjean a letter every week. She sent it to his address a small set of rooms on the Rue de l'Homme-Armé, a quiet street in the Marais. He always wrote back, briefly. His letters were warm but careful. He told her about the street, about the weather, about small things he had seen. He did not complain.

But the Sundays stopped.

## Scene 10 The Rooms on the Rue de l'Homme-Armé

Valjean had taken two rooms on the Rue de l'Homme-Armé.

They were plain rooms a main room with a table and a chair and a fireplace, and a small room beyond it with a bed. He had brought very few things. The candlesticks were on the shelf above the fireplace. He lit them every evening.

He had nothing particular to do anymore.

At Montreuil-sur-Mer, there had been the factory the daily decisions, the workers, the accounts, the problems that needed solving. At the Gorbeau house, there had been Cosette coming home at four o'clock, her bag full of school things, her face full of the day's events. At the convent, there had been the garden, the planting, the physical work that kept him useful.

Here, there was nothing.

He was not, precisely, unhappy. He had been unhappy in his life and he knew what it felt like, and this was different. This was more like stillness. Like a clock that has wound down. The mechanism is all there. Nothing is broken. But there is no movement.

He ate small meals. He went for walks in the early morning when the streets were empty. He read, sometimes, though his eyes were not as good as they had been. He wrote his letters to Cosette.

He thought about the bishop. He thought about the candlesticks and what they meant not the silver, but the moment. The moment when a man who had spent nineteen years being treated as less than human was treated, for the first time, as a person who could still be trusted. He had spent the rest of his life trying to be worthy of that moment.

He thought: I think I have done what I could.

He thought: I think I am tired.

He sat by the window in the evenings and looked at the street. The street was quiet. Children sometimes played in it in the afternoons. He watched them with an expression that was peaceful and a little distant, the expression of someone watching things that are very good from a comfortable remove.

He ate less each week. He moved more slowly. The doctor who lived two streets away came by once, uninvited, sent by the woman downstairs who had noticed that the old man was not eating. He examined Valjean and looked at him with the honest eyes that good doctors have.

"You are not ill," the doctor said carefully. "But you are not well. You are not trying."

"I am not trying not to," Valjean said. "I am simply very tired."

The doctor left his card and said to send for him. Valjean thanked him and put the card on the table and did not send for him.

## Scene 11 Thénardier

One afternoon in the spring, a man came to the door of the house on the Rue des Filles-du-Calvaire.

He was not a pleasant man. He had a thin face and quick eyes and the manner of someone who has spent many years telling people exactly enough of the truth to get what he wanted. He gave a false name to the servant. He asked to see Monsieur Pontmercy.

Marius came to the door.

He looked at the man. He did not know him.

The man introduced himself his real name, this time. Thénardier. The former inn-keeper of Montfermeil. He said he had information about Marius's father, who had mentioned him in a letter. He said he deserved gratitude. He said it with his hands in his pockets and his eyes moving around the hallway with quick, calculating attention.

Marius looked at him with flat eyes. He had read his father's letter many times. He knew what the letter said: *A man named Thénardier carried me to safety. He saved my life. If you ever meet him, help him.*

He also knew, from Cosette, who Thénardier was. He knew about the inn. He knew about the grey dress and the bucket of water in the cold and the doll that was not allowed. He knew what this man had done to Cosette for five years.

"What do you want?" Marius said.

Thénardier wanted money. He was careful about how he said this, wrapping it in stories about his difficult circumstances and his various misfortunes and the great service he had done for Marius's late father. But the core of it was: money.

Marius listened to all of this without expression.

Then Thénardier, to make his case stronger, said something else. He said: "And I also know something about the old man. The one who calls himself Fauchelevent. Your wife's guardian." He paused for effect. "He is a convict. His real name is Jean Valjean, and he was at number 24601 in the prison at Toulon for nineteen years."

He watched Marius's face. He expected surprise or shock or disgust. Those were the faces he had seen before when he used this information.

Marius looked at him.

"I know," Marius said.

Thénardier's expression flickered. "You know."

"Yes."

Thénardier recalculated quickly. "Did you know," he said, "that it was he who carried you out of the barricade? Through the sewers? I saw him with my own eyes, dragging you through the water on the night of the fifth of June."

He said it as a final weapon a piece of information so large that it would surely produce the reaction he was looking for.

Marius stood very still.

He had known, in a general way, that Valjean had brought him out. He had not asked for details. He had thanked him briefly and then, in the weeks that followed, he had done what he had done. Stepped back. Made the visits fewer.

He had not thought about the sewer.

He had not thought, specifically, about the weight of a man being carried through a kilometre of dark, filthy water, in the dark, after a night of fighting, by a man who was not young. He had not thought about that because it was easier not to.

He thought about it now.

"I see," he said. His voice was very even. "Thank you for coming."

He reached into his pocket. He had a significant amount of money on him, as it happened a sum he had been going to take to the bank that afternoon. He took it all out. He gave it to Thénardier.

Thénardier looked at the money. He had not expected this much.

"Get out of France," Marius said. "Take your family and go. There is enough there for passage and for starting somewhere new. Do not come back."

Thénardier took the money. He looked at Marius for a moment. He seemed to want to say something. He decided not to. He left.

Marius stood in the hallway for a moment after the door closed.

Then he called for his coat.

## Scene 12 The Rue de l'Homme-Armé

Marius went to Cosette first.

He found her at Gillenormand's house, sitting with the old man going over household accounts, which was something she had taken to doing because Gillenormand's eyesight was not good and he trusted her arithmetic.

He said: "We need to go and see your father."

She looked at him. Something in his face made her close the account book immediately. "Now?" she said.

"Now," he said.

They went together through the streets. Marius walked fast. Cosette matched his pace without asking why. She had been waiting for something she had felt it building for weeks, some resolution that hadn't come yet and now she could feel it arriving, though she didn't know its shape.

They came to the Rue de l'Homme-Armé. They went up the stairs.

Marius knocked.

There was a long pause. Then footsteps slow, careful footsteps. The door opened.

Valjean looked at them. He looked first at Cosette and then at Marius. His face was calm.

But he was clearly not well. He was thinner. He moved carefully, like a man who is conserving something. His eyes were clear, but the skin around them was drawn. He was wearing his indoor coat, which was warm, and the rooms behind him were warm, but his face was cold in the way that faces are when the body is not getting enough of something.

"Come in," he said.

They came in.

Marius looked at the candlesticks on the shelf. He looked at the table, which had a single plate and a single cup on it, and both of them were small. He looked at the man he had been slowly pushing away for the last three months.

He sat down. He looked at Valjean. He said: "I owe you an apology."

Valjean looked at him with surprise a small movement of the eyes, quickly controlled.

"You carried me through the sewers," Marius said. "You carried me through the water in the dark for a kilometre, after a night at the barricade, to my grandfather's door. You saved my life. And in return, I " He stopped. He started again. "In return, I treated you as a problem to be managed. I reduced the visits. I made excuses. I told myself it was complicated, because of who you are. And it was. But it was also " he stopped again. He could not find a word that was honest enough. "It was wrong," he said. "It was small of me."

Valjean was quiet for a moment.

"You were not wrong to be troubled by it," he said. "What I told you is true. I am what I am."

"Yes," Marius said. "And what you are is the man who carried me out of the sewers."

The room was quiet.

Cosette was looking at her father. She was piecing things together the conversation she hadn't known about, the months of fewer visits, the careful letters, the way Valjean had accepted each reduction without complaint. She understood it now, and the understanding was painful.

"Papa," she said. "How long have you been ill?"

"I am not ill," he said. He said it without conviction.

She looked at him for a long time with those dark eyes that were her mother's eyes and also entirely her own. "You stopped eating," she said. It was not a question.

He looked at the plate on the table. He was quiet.

"There was not much reason to," he said. And then, because he could see that she was about to say something, and because he was tired in a way that was beyond ordinary tiredness, he said: "I was not unhappy. I want you to know that. I was not miserable. I was simply " he searched for the right word, " finished. I thought I was finished."

"You are not finished," Cosette said. She was not crying. She was very clear and very still, the way she became when something important was happening. "You are not finished. You are going to eat something now. And tomorrow you are going to come and live with us, in the room at the back of the house that gets the morning light. And you are going to eat every evening with us. And on Sunday "

She stopped because her voice had changed.

"On Sunday," she said, more quietly, "I want you there. Every Sunday. Because I have always wanted you there, and I should have said so more clearly."

Valjean looked at her. He looked at her for a long time.

He had always known, from the first evening in the barn near Montfermeil, that she would be more than he expected. He had known it from the way she said *this is all right* about a cold barn with a straw mattress. He had not known that she would be this this person sitting across from him, clear-eyed and firm and entirely sure of what she wanted, still calling him *Papa* after everything.

"All right," he said. He said it quietly, as if it were a small thing. It was not a small thing.

---

## Scene 13 The Morning Light

He moved into the room at the back of the house three days later.

It got the morning light. Cosette had put a good chair in it, and a small table, and a lamp. On the first morning, she brought him breakfast herself not much, because he was not yet eating much, but real food, warm, with care in it. She sat with him while he ate it.

He was not well yet. But he was eating. And he was, in some way that was difficult to describe but visible, more present than he had been in the rooms on the Rue de l'Homme-Armé. As if the thing that had been winding down had been wound again, a little.

Marius came to the room that first morning. He sat across from Valjean and they spoke for an hour about the factory at Montreuil-sur-Mer, about the work, about the workers. It was the kind of conversation that men have when they are learning to know each other, and it was the first such conversation they had ever had.

Valjean told him about the factory's methods the production processes he had invented, the way he had tried to treat the workers fairly, the systems that had made the place work. Marius listened carefully. He asked good questions. He was training to be a lawyer but he was also the grandson of a businessman, and he understood these things.

At the end of the hour, Marius said: "The factory you built when you left, what happened to it?"

"I don't know," Valjean said.

"The methods you developed they were never published. Never used anywhere else."

"No."

Marius looked at him. "Would you tell them to me?" he said. "I would like to write them down."

Valjean looked at him for a moment. Then he said: "Yes."

It was a small thing. But it was something. It was a reason.

---

## Scene 14 The Last Months

The summer passed.

Valjean grew stronger slowly not strong the way he had been, but present. He ate. He walked in the garden in the mornings, which was small but had a fig tree and a stone bench and good light. He sat with Gillenormand in the afternoons sometimes, and the two old men talked with the particular ease of people who have lived too long to pretend about things.

He told Marius the methods of the factory. Marius wrote them down in his careful, lawyerly hand. He said he was going to show them to an industrialist he knew. He said he thought they were good. Valjean listened to this without much reaction, but something in him was satisfied by it the idea that the work had not been entirely lost.

He got worse in the autumn. Not dramatically he did not have a crisis or a collapse. He simply slowed down. He ate a little less. He went to bed earlier. He sat in his chair by the window more and moved around less. The doctor came and said what doctors say when they cannot find a specific illness: he is old, he is tired, his body has worked very hard for a very long time.

Cosette did not leave him alone.

She came to his room every morning and every evening. She brought him food herself. She read to him in the afternoons, sitting in the chair by the window while he rested, her voice steady and warm, going through a long book about natural history that he had asked for and that she found genuinely interesting and that they talked about in the gaps between chapters.

She did not cry in front of him. She was not pretending she was sad, and she let him see that she was sad. But she did not collapse, and she did not make him feel that he was causing her pain by being ill. She was, as she had always been, very clear about what she felt and what she wanted, and what she wanted was to be with him.

Marius came in the evenings. He was not a man of many words, but the words he used were honest. He talked about the day, about the legal cases he was working on, about things Gillenormand had said. He asked Valjean questions about the old days about the factory, about the mayor's work, about what the north of France had been like before the wars. He was, Valjean understood, collecting a history. Recording it. Making sure it was not lost.

This gave Valjean, in his last months, a sense of order that he had not expected. He was being seen. Not as a convict, not as a man on the run, not as a problem but as a man who had lived and done things and who had things worth recording.

It was more than he had thought to have.

---

## Scene 15 The Candlesticks

The bishop's candlesticks stood on the shelf in his room.

He had moved them from every place he had lived. The barn near Montfermeil. The Gorbeau house. The convent. The rooms on the Rue de l'Homme-Armé. Now here. They had been with him longer than any other object, longer than any person except Cosette.

He lit them every evening.

One evening in November, Cosette came to his room and found him sitting in his chair with the candlesticks burning on the shelf and his eyes open but not focused on anything in the room. She said his name. He turned toward her.

"I was thinking about the bishop," he said.

She sat in the chair across from him. She had heard about the bishop many times in pieces, across many years, never all at once.

"Tell me about him," she said.

He told her. He had not told her everything before some parts he had kept back, because they were about who he had been before she knew him, and he had not wanted her to know all of it. But sitting in the room with the candlelight warm on the walls and Cosette across from him with her patient, attentive eyes, he told her.

He told her about the prison the nineteen years, the yellow passport, the doors that closed. He told her about walking from village to village in the dark, in the rain, and nobody letting him in. He told her about Digne, and the bishop's house, and the meal and the bed and the silver.

He told her about stealing the silver in the night. About the gendarmes who brought him back. About the bishop who looked at him and said: *I gave it to him. And I gave him the candlesticks too. Here they are.*

He told her what the bishop had said when the gendarmes left: *Jean Valjean, I have bought your soul. I am giving it back to you. Give it to God.*

Cosette was quiet for a moment after he finished.

"And did you?" she said. "Give it to God?"

He thought about this for a moment. "I gave it to something," he said. "I was not sure what to call it. I tried to do good things. I tried to be honest. I tried to be useful. Whether that is giving it to God " he paused "  I think it might be close enough."

Cosette looked at the candlesticks.

"He was right to give them to you," she said.

"He was right about a great many things," Valjean said.

They sat together for a while in the candlelight. It was a comfortable silence the kind that two people have when they know each other very well and don't need to fill the air.

After a while, Cosette said: "Do you remember what you told me, when I asked who gave you the candlesticks? When I was very young, in the Gorbeau house?"

"I told you they were a gift from a good man," he said.

"A man who changed your life," she said.

"Yes."

She looked at him. "I think you changed his too," she said. "I think a man who does what he did who gives his silver to a stranger and then tells the police he gave it willingly that man is changed by what he does. I think you gave him something back too."

Valjean had not thought about it that way before. He sat with it for a moment.

"That is a kind thought," he said.

"It is a true thought," she said. "There is a difference."

He smiled. It was a real smile the kind he had learned from her, years ago, watching her in the garden or at school or sailing a wooden boat on the pond.

He was very tired. But he was at peace.

---

## Scene 16 December

In December, he stopped getting out of the chair to light the candlesticks.

Cosette lit them for him. She came in the evening and lit them herself, and the candlelight was warm on the walls the same as always, and he sat in his chair and watched the flames and was content.

Marius came every day now. He brought the notes he had been keeping the records of the factory methods, written out in clean pages. He read them to Valjean. He asked questions and corrected things and filled in gaps. Valjean listened and sometimes added something and sometimes shook his head and said no, that is not quite right, it was more like this. They worked through the notes like two men finishing something important together.

On a Thursday in December, Cosette came in the morning and found him still in his chair from the night before. He had not gone to bed. He was awake, and he looked peaceful, but he was very still.

She brought Marius.

They sat with him, one on each side. He was aware of them. He turned his head toward Cosette. He looked at her with clear eyes.

"Cosette," he said.

"I am here," she said.

"I want you to know," he said, "that the years I spent with you were the best part of my life. The part that I would not exchange for anything."

She took his hand.

"I know," she said. She was very steady. "I know, Papa."

He looked at the candlesticks. The flames were still.

He looked at Marius. "Take care of her," he said.

"Always," Marius said.

He was quiet for a while. His breathing was slow and even.

"I am not afraid," he said. He said it as if it were a small surprise to him. "I thought I might be. I am not."

Cosette held his hand.

"The bishop said " he began. He paused. He was very tired. "The bishop said to give it to God. I believe I have done what I could."

He closed his eyes.

The candles burned on the shelf. The room was warm and quiet. Outside the window, Paris was continuing the carts and the voices and the pigeons and the river. The city that had been his hiding place and his home for so many years, moving through its morning the way it always had.

Jean Valjean died quietly, in a warm room, with Cosette's hand in his.

He was at peace.

---

## Epilogue The Stone

They buried him in the Père-Lachaise cemetery, in a simple grave.

Cosette chose a plain stone. She had thought about what to write on it, and had gone through many possibilities, and in the end had written nothing at all no name, no date. She had wanted to write his name, but his name was complicated, and the truth of him was not in a name.

On the plain stone, she had the mason carve four lines from a poem she had found in his rooms, written in his own hand on a piece of paper, folded small, as if he had carried it for a long time:

*He sleeps. Although he was refused so much,*
*he lived. Although he lost his angel, he loved.*
*He is there now in darkness and in peace.*
*God took him when God saw fit.*

Marius and Cosette came to the grave on the first Sunday of each month. Cosette brought flowers simple ones, white, the kind that grow in gardens rather than in shops. She placed them at the base of the stone and stood for a moment.

She did not stay long. She was not a person who believed that the dead wanted long silences. She believed that the dead had said what they had to say while they were alive, and that the best thing you could do afterward was to go home and live as they would have wanted you to live.

She took Marius's arm. They walked through the cemetery in the morning light.

They talked about ordinary things dinner, and Gillenormand's health, and a book she was reading, and a case Marius was working on.

It was, Cosette thought, exactly as he would have wanted.

---

## End of the Story

The bishop of Digne gave his silver to a man who had no one. That man gave nineteen years of work to a town that needed a factory. He gave a promise to a dying woman. He gave a childhood to a girl who had none. He gave his testimony to save a stranger. He gave his name to no one. He gave his freedom, again and again, so that others could have theirs.

And in the end, he gave his story to the people who loved him, who kept it, who carried it forward.

This is what Victor Hugo called miserable *les misérables*: the wretched, the left-out, the ones the world forgets. But Jean Valjean was not only miserable. He was also, in the fullest sense, human. He was capable of becoming something more than what he had been. And he proved it, year by year, for the rest of his life.

That is the story.

## Grammar Focus Looking Back at the Whole Story

### Past Simple throughout a long narrative
Most of this story uses Past Simple as its main tense. It tells a sequence of completed events:
- *He knocked. She opened the door. He came inside.*
- *He lit the candlesticks. Cosette looked at them.*

### "Would" for past habits (reviewing from earlier chapters)
In the epilogue, "would" describes repeated actions in the past:
- *She would bring white flowers simple ones.*
- *They would walk through the cemetery together.*

### "Had + past participle" Past Perfect for earlier events
When we describe something that happened before the main story event:
- *He had carried him through the sewers.* (this happened before the conversation)
- *She had always called him Papa.* (this started long before this scene)
- *He had built a factory and become a mayor.* (this was before the current scene)

### Reported speech what was said vs what is reported
Direct: *"I am giving your soul back to you," the bishop said.*
Reported: *The bishop told him he was giving his soul back.*

Direct: *"Take care of her," he said.*
Reported: *He asked Marius to take care of her.*

### Discourse markers connecting the whole story
The story uses these words to connect events across many scenes:
- **And yet** introduces a contrast: *He was a convict. And yet he saved lives.*
- **After that** shows sequence: *After that, Marius changed.*
- **In the end** shows the final result: *In the end, he gave his story to the people who loved him.*
- **Over the years** shows long, gradual change: *Over the years, she had learned to trust him.*
