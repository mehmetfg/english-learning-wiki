# Les Misérables
## Chapter 3 A New Life
### Extended A2/B1 Reader | ~10,000 words

---

*Before this chapter: Jean Valjean spent nineteen years in prison for stealing bread. When he came out, the world refused him. He was hungry, cold, and angry. Then a bishop gave him silver and showed him kindness. Valjean wept on the road. Something inside him changed. Now he must decide: what kind of man will he be?*

---

## Part One The Road After the Bishop

The morning was cold and grey. Jean Valjean walked alone on the road south of Digne. The sky above him was the colour of old stone. There was no sun. The trees on both sides of the road were bare. Their black branches reached up into the grey sky like thin arms.

Valjean walked slowly. He had the bishop's silver in his bag. He had the two silver candlesticks too. The bishop had given them to him freely. He had given them as a gift. Not as a punishment. Not as a lesson. As a gift.

Valjean could not stop thinking about this. He had been in prison for nineteen years. In prison, nobody gave anything freely. If you received something, it cost you something. A piece of bread cost you a favour. A warm corner of the room cost you your silence. A moment of peace cost you your dignity. Nothing was free in prison. Nothing was kind.

But the bishop had been kind. For no reason. With no expectation. He had called Valjean his brother. He had looked at the yellow paper the paper that said "ex-convict" and he had simply set it aside. He had not seen a criminal. He had seen a man.

Valjean walked and thought about these things. His heavy boots made a dull sound on the road. His bag was on his back. The silver was heavy. But it was not the silver that weighed him down. It was something else. Something inside his chest. Something that he did not have a name for yet.

He had not cried in many years. He had cried the night before, sitting by the road. He had cried for a long time. He felt ashamed of this. Men did not cry, he told himself. Men who had survived Toulon prison did not sit by the road and cry. But the tears had come anyway. And after they came, he felt strangely lighter.

He was still thinking about the bishop's words.

"I have bought your soul," the bishop had said. "I give it to God."

Valjean did not know much about God. God had not helped him when he was poor and hungry. God had not helped his sister's seven children when they had nothing to eat. God had not shortened his sentence when he tried to escape. God had not made the yellow paper disappear.

But the bishop believed in God. And the bishop was kind. So perhaps there was a connection.

Valjean did not know. He kept walking.

---

The road went through open fields. In the distance, there were low hills. The grass was brown and flat. There were no other travellers on the road that morning. Valjean was completely alone.

He had been walking for about two hours when he heard a sound behind him. A light, quick sound. Footsteps.

He turned around.

It was a boy. A very young boy, perhaps ten or eleven years old. He had dark hair and a round face with red cheeks. He was a chimney sweep Valjean could see the black marks on his hands and on the side of his neck. He wore old clothes that were too big for him. He carried a small bag over one shoulder and a long stick in his other hand.

The boy was running to catch up with him. He was smiling.

"Monsieur!" the boy called. "Please wait!"

Valjean stopped and waited. The boy ran up to him and stopped, slightly out of breath.

"Monsieur," the boy said again. He had a cheerful voice. "My coin fell. It rolled into the grass. Can you help me find it?"

Valjean looked at the boy. "What coin?"

"My coin. It's a forty-sous piece. I had it in my hand and it fell. It rolled over there, near your feet. I saw it go."

Valjean looked down at the ground near his boots. He saw nothing. He looked at the grass beside the road. He saw nothing there either.

"I don't see it," he said.

"Please," said the boy. "It's all the money I have. Please help me look."

Valjean put down his bag. He looked carefully at the ground. He moved slowly, looking to the left and to the right. After a minute, he found it. The coin was in the short grass, just at the edge of the road. A small, shiny circle.

He picked it up. He held it in his fingers.

The boy reached out his hand. "Thank you, monsieur! Thank you so much!"

Valjean looked at the coin. He looked at the boy's open hand. He looked at the boy's face his red cheeks, his bright eyes, his simple smile.

And then something happened that Valjean did not plan and did not understand.

He did not give the coin back. He stepped back. He put his boot on the coin and pushed it into the dirt. He stood still.

The boy's smile disappeared. He looked confused.

"Monsieur? My coin?"

Valjean said nothing. He stood very still. He looked at the boy, but he did not see the boy. He was somewhere else inside his head. Something dark had taken hold of him. The old Valjean the hard, bitter, angry man from prison had come back, just for a moment.

The boy stood there for a minute, waiting. He looked at Valjean's face. He looked at his boot. He did not understand what was happening. He was frightened.

"Please, monsieur," he said in a small voice. "It's my coin. I need it."

Valjean still did not move.

The boy took a step back. Then another step. Then he turned and ran. He ran quickly, back down the road, away from the strange man with the cold eyes. His stick bounced on his shoulder as he ran. Valjean could hear his footsteps getting smaller and smaller until there was silence again.

Valjean stood alone on the road.

After a moment, he looked down at his boot. He moved it. The coin was there in the dirt. He bent down and picked it up. He held it in his palm and looked at it.

What had he done?

He looked down the road. The boy was gone. He had disappeared around a bend. Valjean stared after him.

"Petit-Gervais!" he shouted. He did not know why he shouted the boy's name. He had not heard the boy's name. But he shouted it anyway. "Petit-Gervais!"

Nothing. Only the wind in the bare trees.

He started to walk down the road after the boy. He walked faster and faster. He was almost running. He reached the bend in the road and looked around it.

The road was empty.

"Petit-Gervais!" he called again. His voice went out across the empty fields and disappeared.

He stood in the middle of the road. The coin was still in his hand. He stared at it. Forty sous. A small coin. For him, it meant nothing. For the boy a chimney sweep with no family and no home it was everything he had.

And Valjean had stood on it. He had stood on it and said nothing while a frightened child begged for his own money.

The feeling in his chest came back. But it was different now. It was not the warm, broken feeling from the night before. This was sharp. This was pain. This was something very clear and very awful.

This was shame.

Valjean sat down on a stone at the side of the road. He put his head in his hands. He sat there for a long time. He did not cry this time. He was too ashamed to cry. He just sat there in the cold, grey morning with a small coin in his hand, and he thought about what he had just done.

He had been given silver and kindness by the bishop. He had walked away with warmth in his heart. And within two hours, he had stolen a child's coin. He had not stolen it with his hands. He had stolen it with his coldness. His old self had come back without warning. Without permission.

"I am a terrible man," he said quietly. He said it to the empty road. "I am still a terrible man."

He put the coin in his pocket. He stood up. He picked up his bag. He began to walk again.

But now he walked differently. He walked with a decision. A slow, painful, important decision that he made with every step.

He would not be that man anymore. He could not go back and find the boy. But he could go forward and be different. He would take the bishop's silver. He would use it to build something. And he would spend the rest of his life being the kind of man the bishop had believed he could be.

This is how Jean Valjean for the last time in a long while thought about himself as Jean Valjean.

After this day, he would become someone else.

## Part Two Wandering

He did not go to any particular place at first. He walked north. He walked through small towns and quiet villages. He slept in barns when he could and in open fields when he could not find shelter. The spring came slowly. The days grew a little longer and a little less cold.

He was very careful about his identity. He knew that Javert the police inspector at Toulon was looking for him. He knew that any police officer could arrest him if they saw his yellow paper. He needed a new name and a new life in a place where nobody knew him.

He thought about this carefully as he walked.

He needed a place that was far enough from Toulon that nobody would recognise him. He needed a place that was large enough to disappear into, but not so large that he could not find work and build something. He needed a place where a new man could arrive and be accepted without too many questions.

France in those years the early years of the nineteenth century was a country of movement. People moved from the countryside to the cities. People moved from the south to the north to find work. A new man arriving in a town was not always suspicious. Sometimes a new man was simply a worker looking for a job.

Valjean had a skill. During his years in prison, he had worked in many different workshops. He had worked with wood and metal and fabric. He had learned how things were made. He had learned how to look at a process and see where it could be done better, faster, cheaper. This kind of thinking had kept his mind alive during the long, dark years of his sentence.

He thought about what he knew. He thought about what he could do. And as he walked north through the spring countryside, an idea began to form in his mind.

Black glass beads.

He had seen them being made in the south. They were decorative buttons, small shiny beads, used in the clothing industry. They were popular and there was always demand for them. But the way they were made was slow and expensive. The craftsmen in the south used a method that had not changed for many years.

Valjean had watched them work. He had thought about their process. He had seen a different way to do it a way that used less heat, less time, and less expensive materials. He had turned this idea over in his mind for months during his time in prison. He had never had any reason to use it. But now, perhaps, he did.

He needed a place where this kind of work was already known. A place with a history of small manufacturing. A place in the north of France.

He had heard about a town called Montreuil-sur-Mer. It was a quiet town near the northern coast. The bead-making industry had been there before, but it had died out many years ago. The town was not rich, but it was not completely poor either. And most importantly, it was far from Toulon. Nobody in Montreuil-sur-Mer would look at a new man and think: I know who you are.

Valjean walked north. He walked for weeks.

## Part Three Arriving in Montreuil

He arrived in Montreuil-sur-Mer on a warm afternoon in late spring. He came over the hill and saw the town below him. It was a modest place. Not beautiful, not ugly. Grey stone buildings, a church in the centre, a marketplace with a fountain. Around the town were flat fields and, in the distance to the north, the grey line of the sea.

He walked into the town. He found a cheap room in a workers' boarding house. The woman who ran the house was old and did not ask many questions. She asked for a week's money in advance. He paid.

He spent his first few days just watching and listening. He walked through the market and listened to people talk. He sat in the small café near the square and drank coffee and listened. He walked along the streets in the evening and looked at the buildings and the people going home from work.

He needed to understand this town before he could become part of it.

What he heard was this: the town was struggling. There had been work here once the black bead industry had employed many people. But that was a long time ago. The method had become too expensive. A manufacturer from another region had offered cheaper beads, and the local factories had closed, one by one. Now the people of Montreuil-sur-Mer worked in other trades farming, small workshops, the fishing trade on the coast but there was not enough work for everyone. Many families were poor.

Valjean listened to all of this and said very little. He asked careful questions. He asked where a man could buy raw materials glass, jet, and the chemicals needed for the work. He asked about empty workshop spaces. He asked about the cost of renting a building.

People answered him without much interest. He was just another traveller passing through, or so they thought.

But Valjean was not passing through. He had already decided.

---

## Part Four The Workshop

He found an empty building on the edge of town. It had been a small factory once, years before. The roof was in poor condition, but the walls were solid. There was a courtyard behind it with space for equipment. The owner an old man who lived across the street had not found a use for it in years. He was happy to rent it for very little money.

Valjean paid for three months in advance. He bought tools. He bought materials. He fixed the roof himself, working in the evenings and at weekends. He hired two men to help him local workers who needed money and were willing to work without knowing much about their new employer.

He began to test his idea.

The process was slow at first. He made many mistakes. He had carried this idea in his head for years, but an idea in the head is different from an idea in the hands. The materials behaved differently than he expected. The heat was difficult to control. The first batch of beads was ugly and misshapen.

He threw them away and started again.

The second batch was better. Not perfect, but better. The beads were rounder and smoother. The colour was deeper. The cost of making them was lower than the old method.

He started again. And again.

By the end of the first month, he was making beads that were as good as any on the market. By the end of the second month, he was making beads that were better harder, shinier, more consistent in size and shape.

By the end of the third month, he had orders from three different buyers.

---

He hired more workers. Six, then twelve, then twenty. He was careful about who he hired. He looked for people who needed work most women whose husbands were sick, men who had been out of work for a long time, older workers who other employers would not take. He paid them fairly. He paid them a little more than fair, in fact. And he told them clearly what he expected: honest work, done well.

The workers talked to each other. They talked to their families. They talked to their neighbours. The word spread through Montreuil-sur-Mer like a quiet fire.

There is a new man. He has a factory. He pays well. He is fair. He does not shout. He does not cheat.

More people came to ask for work. Valjean hired them if he could. He expanded the workshop. He rented the building next door. He bought better equipment.

His method was clearly superior to the old way of making beads. Other small manufacturers in the region came to watch and to learn. Some tried to copy him. None of them succeeded completely, because the most important part of his method was not a machine or a chemical. It was his eye his ability to see where a process could be improved, and his patience to keep trying until it was right.

Within two years, Montreuil-sur-Mer was producing more black beads than it had ever produced before. The money came into the town. People had jobs. Families were not as hungry as they had been. The market was busier. New businesses opened.

The town began to breathe again.

---

## Part Five Monsieur Madeleine

The man behind all of this was known simply as Monsieur Madeleine.

Nobody in Montreuil-sur-Mer knew anything about his past. He had arrived from nowhere and said nothing about his history. He had a slight southern accent. He was physically very strong strong in a way that was unusual for a man of his age. He did not drink. He did not gamble. He was not seen at parties or social events. He lived alone.

His apartment was above the factory. It was simple. He had a bed, a table, a chair, and a shelf. On the shelf, there were two silver candlesticks. They were the only beautiful or expensive things in the room. Sometimes his workers who came to speak with him would glance at the candlesticks and wonder. They seemed too fine for such a simple room.

Every evening, Madeleine ate a small meal alone. He sat at his table and ate bread and soup or bread and cheese. He drank water. After eating, he read for a while he had a small collection of books, mostly history and science. Then he lit the two silver candlesticks and sat quietly for some time. What he thought about during these quiet evenings, nobody knew.

He went to bed early and rose early. He was always the first person in the factory. He checked the machines. He checked the materials. He sometimes worked alongside the workers with his own hands, not because he needed to but because he seemed to enjoy the physical work. He rolled up his sleeves and lifted and carried without complaint, and the workers respected him for this.

He had one rule that never changed. If a worker came to him and said they could not come to work because someone in their family was sick, Madeleine said: "Take what time you need. Your wages will continue."

This was not normal. It was not what factory owners did. It was remarkable.

People noticed.

---

The people of Montreuil-sur-Mer did not know how to feel about Monsieur Madeleine at first. He was generous, yes. He was honest, yes. But he was also quiet and private in a way that made some people uncomfortable. He did not go to church regularly, which some of the older townspeople found suspicious. He did not socialise. He did not tell stories or make jokes in the café.

He was not warm. He was not cold. He was correct. He was fair. He was dependable. He was there.

Some people found this admirable. Others found it strange.

But what won the town over more than the factory, more than the wages, more than the fairness was the small things he did when he thought nobody was watching.

He was seen, one winter morning, stopping on the street when he noticed that the old man who swept the square was barefoot in the snow. Madeleine took off his own boots. He asked the old man for his foot size. He went to the shoe shop and came back with new boots. He gave them to the old man and walked the rest of the day in his socks. He did not say anything about it to anyone. The shoe shop owner told the story to his wife. His wife told her sister. The sister told her neighbours.

He was seen, on a cold night in autumn, carrying a basket of food to the house of a woman who had lost her husband and had four small children to care for. He knocked on the door and set the basket down. When the woman opened the door, he was already walking away.

He was seen, more than once, stopping to help when there was an accident in the street. He did not step back and watch, as most people did. He moved forward. He lifted what needed to be lifted. He did what needed to be done.

He gave money to the church, though he did not always attend. He gave money to the hospital. He gave money to the school. He gave quietly and without wanting recognition.

The town looked at all of these things and made up its mind.

Monsieur Madeleine was a good man.

Perhaps he was a private man. Perhaps he had his secrets, as all men do. But he was good. He had brought the town back to life. He had given people work and dignity. And he cared about people in a real way not a showy way, not a proud way, but a real, practical, daily way.

After three years, the mayor of Montreuil-sur-Mer died. The town council met and discussed who should take his place.

The name on everyone's lips was Monsieur Madeleine.

---

## Part Six The Mayor

He refused the first time they asked him.

The council president came to his office with a formal letter. Madeleine read the letter carefully. He thanked the council president for the honour. He said that he was not the right man for such a responsibility. He said that he was a simple manufacturer, not a politician. He returned the letter.

He refused the second time too.

The second request came from the whole council together. They came to his factory on a Tuesday afternoon. They stood in his office in their formal coats and hats. They spoke about how much he had done for the town. They spoke about their confidence in his judgment. They spoke about the town's need for a strong and honest leader.

Madeleine listened to all of this standing behind his desk. He thanked them again. He said again that he was not the right man. He said there were others in the town with more experience and more knowledge of civic matters. He mentioned two or three names. The council said that those men had already declined or were unsuitable for other reasons.

Madeleine shook his head. He said he was sorry. He asked them to continue their search.

They left. He sat down at his desk and looked out the window for a long time.

He was afraid.

Not of the work. He was not afraid of responsibility or hard decisions. He had made hard decisions every day since he arrived in Montreuil-sur-Mer. He knew how to work and how to lead.

He was afraid of visibility.

A mayor was visible. A mayor was in documents. A mayor was in newspapers. A mayor was spoken about and written about and noticed. And Valjean Madeleine had spent years trying not to be noticed. He had built his life on quietness. On staying at the edge of things. On being useful but not famous.

A mayor could not be quiet.

A mayor would eventually be found.

But then the third request came. And it came differently.

It was not the council this time. It was an old woman named Madame Victurnien. She was seventy years old and she had lived in the town her whole life. She had seen it when it was prosperous and she had seen it when it was poor. She had seen her grandchildren grow up without enough to eat. And now, for the first time in many years, she could see that the town was growing again, and the people were working again, and there was hope in the faces on the street.

She came to Madeleine's factory alone on a Wednesday evening. She knocked on the door of his apartment. When he opened it, she looked at him without embarrassment.

"I am not here to ask you anything officially," she said. "I am here to tell you something."

He let her in. She sat down in the one chair and looked around the simple room. Her eyes rested on the silver candlesticks for a moment.

"Monsieur Madeleine," she said, "I have watched you for three years. I know what you do and I know how you do it. I know about the boots you gave to old Bernard in the square. I know about the basket you left at the widow Leroux's door. I know about the money you gave the hospital that they used to buy beds."

Madeleine said nothing.

"I am not here to thank you," she said. "Others have done that. I am here to tell you that the people of this town need you. Not your factory. Not your money. They need you. Your judgment. Your fairness. Your eyes that see what other people miss."

She stood up.

"I have seen many mayors in my life," she said. "I have seen men who took the job because they wanted power. I have seen men who took it because they wanted people to admire them. I have even seen men who took it because they genuinely wanted to help. The last kind are the rarest. And the last kind are the only ones who make any real difference."

She walked to the door. She put her hand on the handle.

"We need the last kind," she said. "We need you."

She left.

Madeleine stood in his apartment for a long time after she was gone. He looked at the silver candlesticks on the shelf. He thought about the bishop. He thought about the boy on the road Petit-Gervais and the coin. He thought about the promise he had made to himself on that grey morning.

He had promised to be the man the bishop believed he could be.

What would that man do now?

The next morning, he sent a letter to the council. He accepted the position of mayor.

## Part Seven The Mayor's Way

Monsieur Madeleine became mayor of Montreuil-sur-Mer with the same quiet efficiency that he brought to everything.

He did not make speeches. He did not celebrate his position. He did not redecorate the mayor's office or add to his staff. He sat down at the mayor's desk and began to work.

He read all the town's documents. He read the accounts. He read the letters that had accumulated and never been answered. He read the complaints and the petitions and the proposals that had been sitting in piles for months. He worked through them methodically, one by one, every day.

He met with the people of the town who had problems. He listened to each person for as long as they needed to speak. He did not interrupt. He did not make promises he could not keep. He said clearly what he could do and what he could not do. And then he did what he said he would do.

He was particularly interested in the conditions of the poorest people in the town. He visited the workers' neighbourhoods. He walked through the poorest streets in the evenings. He talked to people in their doorways. He asked questions. He listened to the answers.

What he found was what he had expected, because he had lived it himself. He found poverty that was not the result of laziness or weakness. He found people who worked hard and still could not afford enough food for their children. He found old people who had worked all their lives and had nothing to live on. He found sick people who could not afford a doctor.

He made changes. He was not a revolutionary he did not try to change everything at once and make enemies of people with power. He was patient. He was strategic. He moved carefully and deliberately.

He expanded the hospital. He hired a second doctor. He arranged for the poorest families to be able to see the doctor without payment. He set up a small fund from his own money, as well as from the town's budget to help families during particularly hard times.

He improved the school. He made sure it was warm in winter. He made sure it had enough books. He paid for the schoolmaster's salary from his own pocket when the town's budget was too small.

He built a shelter for old people who had nowhere to live.

He fixed the roads.

He did all of these things without drawing attention to himself. He was the mayor, and these were the things a mayor could do. He did them the same way he had given the boots to old Bernard in the square quietly and without expecting thanks.

But the town noticed. The town always noticed. And the town was grateful in the simple, powerful way that ordinary people are grateful not with words and ceremonies, but with loyalty. The people of Montreuil-sur-Mer felt that they had a mayor who was on their side. This was not a feeling they had had before. It made a great difference.

Madeleine walked through the market every morning on his way to the factory. He greeted people by name. He stopped when someone looked worried or sad and asked if everything was all right. He did not always have an answer, but he always had time to listen.

The women who sold bread in the market looked forward to seeing him. The children who played in the square were not afraid of him, as they were of some powerful men. He was serious, but not frightening. Firm, but not cold.

He was the mayor. And he was, simply and genuinely, a good one.

## Part Eight What He Carried

But at night, alone in his apartment with his books and his two silver candlesticks, Madeleine was someone else.

At night, he was still Jean Valjean.

He had never forgotten this. He had never forgotten who he was and where he had come from. He had never forgotten the nineteen years, or the yellow paper, or the closed doors. He had never forgotten the face of the bishop old and kind and entirely without judgement.

And he had never forgotten Petit-Gervais. The boy on the road. The coin.

He thought about the boy often. Not every night, but often enough. He wondered where the boy was now. He hoped the boy had found someone else to help him. He hoped the boy had not been permanently frightened by the encounter. He kept a small amount of money forty sous, always exactly forty sous in a separate part of his desk drawer. He had never touched it. He did not know entirely why he kept it. Perhaps as a reminder. Perhaps as a kind of debt he could never repay.

He thought about his sister too. He had a sister she was somewhere in France with her children. He did not know where. He did not know if she was alive. He had lost contact with her during the prison years. He thought about her sometimes and felt a weight in his chest that he did not speak about to anyone.

He thought about Toulon. He thought about the men he had known there some brutal, some broken, some just unlucky. He thought about the guards, some of whom had been cruel for no reason. He thought about the inspectors. He thought about Inspector Javert.

Javert.

Even in these years of safety and work and peace, the name Javert was like a cold hand on his shoulder. He had not seen the man in years. He did not know where Javert was. But he knew that Javert existed, somewhere in France, with his notebooks and his sharp eyes and his absolute belief in the law.

If Javert ever came to Montreuil-sur-Mer, and if he looked at the mayor really looked at him, with those careful, cold eyes would he see through the name and the position and the respectability?

Would he see Jean Valjean?

Madeleine tried not to think about this. He had a life now. He had a purpose. He had people who depended on him. He could not afford to be paralysed by fear.

He lit the candles. He read his book. He went to sleep.

And in the morning, he was the mayor again.

## Part Nine The Accident

The accident happened on a Tuesday in autumn, about four years after Madeleine had arrived in Montreuil-sur-Mer.

The market was busy that morning. There were people everywhere women buying bread and vegetables, men standing in groups talking, children running between the stalls. The sun was out for the first time in several days, and people were glad of it. There was an atmosphere of mild, tired happiness that comes after a string of grey days.

A man named Fauchelevent was moving a cart through the market. Fauchelevent was old in his sixties and he had worked his whole life in small trades. He was not a strong man. He was thin and slow and he moved carefully. He had a cart loaded with grain sacks that he needed to deliver to a merchant on the far side of the market.

The cart was too heavy for him, really. He should have had help. But he was proud and also he could not afford to pay for a helper. So he pushed the cart alone.

Halfway across the market square, one of the wheels hit a loose stone in the pavement. The cart lurched. Fauchelevent tried to hold it. He grabbed the side of the cart and pulled back. But the weight was too much. The cart tilted sharply. Fauchelevent lost his footing. He fell. As he fell, the cart came down on top of him.

He was trapped under the weight of the cart and the full grain sacks. His left leg was pinned beneath a wheel. He cried out. The sound was sharp and terrible.

People around him stopped. They looked. They moved closer. A small crowd formed quickly, as crowds always do around accidents.

"Help him! Somebody help him!"

People looked at each other. The cart was very heavy. Four or five grain sacks, each one weighing many kilograms, were piled on top. The combined weight was enormous.

Two young men tried to lift one side of the cart. They could not. A third man joined them. They strained and pushed. The cart did not move. Fauchelevent was making a low, continuous sound of pain. His face was white.

"We need a horse! Somebody get a horse!"

But there was no horse nearby. It would take time to find one. And Fauchelevent's leg was being crushed.

Madeleine was walking through the market on his usual morning route. He heard the commotion. He pushed through the people at the edge of the crowd and saw what had happened.

He saw Fauchelevent on the ground. He saw the cart on top of him. He saw the three men who had tried and failed to lift it.

He did not think. He acted.

He took off his jacket. He gave it to someone standing nearby. He knelt down beside the cart and pushed his shoulders under the underside of it. He pressed his back against the frame. He put his feet flat on the ground.

He lifted.

It was not easy. Even for Madeleine, it was not easy. The blood came into his face. His neck muscles stood out. His whole body shook with the effort. The people watching were silent.

But slowly very slowly the cart began to rise.

One centimetre. Two. Five.

"Pull him out!" someone shouted.

Two men reached under the cart and grabbed Fauchelevent's arms. They pulled him along the ground. He cried out as they moved him, but they got him clear. As soon as he was out, Madeleine let the cart fall. It hit the ground with a crash.

He stood up. He was breathing hard. His face was red. He took his jacket back from the man holding it and put it on. He looked at Fauchelevent, who was lying on the ground with people kneeling around him.

"Is the doctor coming?" Madeleine asked.

"Yes, monsieur le maire! Someone went for him."

"Good." He looked at Fauchelevent's face. "You will be all right," he said. "The leg may be broken, but you are alive. The doctor will help you."

Fauchelevent looked up at him. He was in pain and his eyes were wet. "Thank you," he whispered. "Thank you, monsieur le maire."

Madeleine nodded. He put his jacket straight. He walked away through the crowd.

The people in the square looked after him.

One man among the crowd did not look at Madeleine with gratitude. He looked with something else. He looked with careful, suspicious attention. He noted the mayor's physical strength the extraordinary, remarkable strength of a man who had just lifted a cart that three younger men could not move. He noted the way the mayor had acted the absence of fear, the complete physical confidence.

This man had seen that kind of strength before. He had seen it at Toulon prison. In the exercise yard, among the hardest and most experienced convicts. Men whose bodies had been made powerful by years of physical labour and survival.

The man's name was Javert.

---

## Part Ten Inspector Javert

Inspector Javert had arrived in Montreuil-sur-Mer three months earlier.

He had requested the posting himself.

Javert was a man of about forty-five. He was tall taller than most men with a heavy build and a face that seemed made of harder material than ordinary human faces. His jaw was strong and square. His eyes were grey, almost colourless, and they moved slowly and deliberately, like a mechanism. He did not blink very often. When he looked at you, the feeling was that he was not just looking at your face but at everything behind it.

He had been born in a prison. His father was a convict; his mother was a woman of the lowest class. This was the beginning of his life born inside the walls that most people feared and despised. He had grown up with this fact and made of it something very particular.

Some people, born into darkness, spend their lives reaching for light. They look at the world and see what it could be. They feel the wrongness of injustice personally because they have experienced it personally.

Javert looked at the world and saw order and disorder. He had decided, very early in his life, that he was on the side of order. He was not going to be like his parents. He was not going to be weak or criminal or pitiful. He was going to be the law. He was going to be the thing that stood between the decent world and the chaos beneath it.

He had joined the police. He had worked hard. He had advanced. He was now an inspector, which was not the highest rank but was a position of considerable responsibility. He was known, in police circles, as someone who never let a case go. He was known as someone who could look at a man and see what the man was hiding.

He had requested the posting to Montreuil-sur-Mer because of the mayor.

Not because he knew, for certain, that the mayor was a wanted man. He did not know this with certainty. He had a feeling. He had a memory. He had noticed, in his files, a gap a period of seven years during which an escaped convict named Jean Valjean had not been located.

Jean Valjean had been sentenced to hard labour for life after his fourth escape attempt. Then, at some point during the fourth year of this sentence, he had escaped again. He had not been found.

Javert had worked at Toulon during part of Valjean's sentence. He remembered the man. He remembered the grey eyes. He remembered the voice low and careful. He remembered the strength.

When Javert had heard stories about a factory owner in Montreuil-sur-Mer who had revived the town's bead industry through his own intelligence and hard work, who lived simply and gave generously and was known for his physical strength, who had appeared from nowhere with no past and no connections Javert had been interested.

He had studied a description. He had studied what little was known about this Monsieur Madeleine. And he had thought: I want to see this man for myself.

So he had requested the posting. He had arrived in Montreuil-sur-Mer three months ago and he had been watching.

---

He was careful. He was always careful. He did not rush to judgement. He did not make accusations without evidence. This was both his strength and, in a deeper sense, his only real code of ethics. Evidence. Proof. The law.

He observed the mayor at official meetings. He observed him in the street. He noted his habits, his routines, his interactions with the townspeople.

He noticed things. Small things that other people would not notice.

He noticed that the mayor never talked about his past. In normal conversation, people mention their past constantly their hometown, their family, their old friends, their former work. The mayor never did. He redirected every such conversation smoothly, without apparent effort. He had clearly done this many times.

He noticed that the mayor's hands were rough in a way that was inconsistent with a man who had only ever worked in manufacturing. The roughness was older than that. It was the roughness of someone who had done much harder work for much longer.

He noticed that the mayor sometimes seemed, for just a fraction of a second, to hesitate when someone called him Monsieur Madeleine. A very small hesitation. The kind that comes when a name is not entirely your own.

All of these things were small. They proved nothing. They were not evidence.

But Javert collected them carefully in his mind and his notebooks, and he waited.

Then the accident with the cart happened. And Javert watched Madeleine lift the cart. And the feeling in his chest the feeling that he had been right to come here became much stronger.

That evening, Javert sat in his office and wrote a letter. He addressed it to the police prefecture in Paris. He explained his suspicion. He did not accuse directly he was too careful for that. He simply said that he had observed a man in Montreuil-sur-Mer who had certain characteristics that were consistent with the description of the escaped convict Jean Valjean. He requested that the prefecture look into it.

He sent the letter.

Then he waited.

---

## Part Eleven The Watch

Life in Montreuil-sur-Mer continued. The factory worked. The market opened every morning. The mayor walked his route and greeted people by name.

Madeleine was aware of Javert. He had been aware of him since the inspector's arrival. He had not been able to explain exactly why, but every time he saw Javert on the street or at an official function, he felt a coldness pass through him.

He told himself he was being foolish. There were always police inspectors. Javert was just doing his job. There was no particular reason for the man to suspect the mayor of anything.

But he watched Javert watching him. And the look in Javert's eyes was not the look of a man who suspected nothing.

Madeleine ate his small meals alone and thought about this. He read his books and thought about this. He lit his candlesticks and thought about this.

He thought about what he would do if Javert came to him one day and said: I know who you are. He thought about the choice he would face: run again, or stay and face the consequences.

Running was what he had always done before. It had kept him free. But it had also kept him always frightened, always looking behind him, always building a life that he knew could collapse. He was tired of running. He was fifty years old. He had built something here. He had helped people. He had for the first time in his adult life felt like a man with a purpose and a place.

Could he give all of that up and run again?

He thought about the bishop's words. He thought about the soul that the bishop had claimed and given to God. Was a soul that spent its life running the same as a soul that stayed and did good?

He did not reach a conclusion. He blew out the candles and went to sleep.

And in the morning, he walked through the market, and greeted people by name, and went to his factory, and worked.

That was all he could do. That was what he chose to do.

---

The weeks passed. The seasons changed. Autumn became winter. Winter became, slowly, the beginning of spring.

Javert received a reply from Paris. The prefecture had investigated. They had a suspect in custody a man who matched the description of Jean Valjean, who had been arrested in another town for a minor offence and who, under questioning, seemed to match the files on the escaped convict.

The trial was scheduled. In Arras.

Javert read the letter. He read it twice.

He felt something that he was not used to feeling. He felt uncertain.

He had been so sure about Madeleine. He had watched the man for months. He had collected his small observations. He had built, in his mind, a solid structure of suspicion. And now Paris was telling him that the real Jean Valjean was someone else. That his structure was wrong.

He had to report this. He was a man of honour. He could not simply hide the letter and continue to suspect Madeleine. He had been wrong, and he had to say so.

Javert did not like being wrong. He had spent his life trying never to be wrong. Being wrong was a kind of disorder in the world that he believed in a world of clear categories, clear rules, clear identities. Being wrong meant that his eyes, which he trusted completely, had failed him.

But he would report it. He would go to the mayor. He would confess his mistake. Because that was what the law required. And Javert always did what the law required.

He put on his coat. He walked to the mayor's office. He knocked on the door.

"Come in," said the mayor's voice.

Javert went in.

---

## Part Twelve A Conversation

Madeleine was sitting behind his desk. He was reading a document. He looked up when Javert entered and waited.

Javert was uncomfortable. Anyone watching would have found this strange Javert, one of the most feared men in the region, standing in front of the mayor like a child who had done something wrong. But it was true. He was uncomfortable.

"Monsieur le Maire," he said. "I need to speak with you about something important."

"Please sit down," said Madeleine.

Javert sat. He put his hands on his knees. He looked directly at the mayor that direct, grey, unblinking look that never changed.

"I have done something that was unjust," said Javert. "I reported you to the police in Paris. I told them that I suspected you were an escaped convict named Jean Valjean."

Madeleine's face did not change. Not a muscle moved.

"I was wrong," said Javert. "They have found the real Jean Valjean. He is in Arras. His trial is tomorrow. I want to apologise to you for my suspicion and for the report I made."

Madeleine looked at him for a long moment.

"I see," he said. His voice was very calm.

"I should not have made the report without more evidence," said Javert. "I acted on a feeling. Feelings are not evidence. I am sorry."

This was a remarkable thing for Javert to say. He had probably said "I am sorry" fewer than a dozen times in his life. Each time it cost him something real.

Madeleine leaned back in his chair. He was quiet for a moment.

"I appreciate you telling me this," he said at last.

"I also want to say," Javert continued, "that I would understand if you feel I cannot continue in my position here. Given my conduct, you have the right to request my removal. I will accept whatever decision you make."

Madeleine shook his head slowly. "No," he said. "You were doing your job as you understood it. An inspector who suspects nothing is no use to anyone. Stay in your position."

Javert stood up. He picked up his hat. He held it in his hands.

"Thank you, monsieur le maire," he said.

He left.

Madeleine sat very still after Javert was gone.

An innocent man. In Arras. Going to trial for being Jean Valjean.

Because of his silence. Because of the life he had built here. Because of his carefully constructed identity.

He put his hands flat on the desk. He looked at the wood grain of the desk surface. He thought.

Outside, the town was going about its morning. He could hear the distant sounds of the market voices, wheels on stone, a dog barking. The ordinary sounds of an ordinary place where ordinary people lived their ordinary lives, which were not ordinary to them at all.

He had given these people something. He had given them work and hope and a sense that someone in power was on their side. If he left if he confessed, if he was taken back to prison all of that would stop.

But a man in Arras would go to prison for something he had not done.

Madeleine sat at his desk for a very long time.

---

## Epilogue to Chapter Three

That night, Madeleine did not read and he did not sleep. He sat at his table with the two silver candlesticks burning. He looked at the small flames. He thought about the bishop. He thought about what the bishop had asked of him.

Not safety. Not comfort. Not a quiet life. 

Goodness.

He had tried to be good. He had tried very hard, for many years, in this town. And he had succeeded, in many ways. He had done real good for real people.

But goodness was not a thing you achieved once and kept forever. It was a choice you had to make again and again. And now he had to make it again. In the hardest possible way.

He thought about the innocent man in Arras.

He thought about the bishop's voice: I have bought your soul.

He made his decision.

He called for his carriage. He told the driver to prepare for a long journey. He packed a small bag. He took nothing from his apartment except his clothes and the two silver candlesticks, which he wrapped carefully and put in the bag.

He drove through the night, north and east, toward Arras.

Toward the truth.

Toward whatever came next.

## Reading Notes

**Grammar in this chapter:**
- **Past Simple** for story events: *He arrived, he refused, he lifted, he decided*
- **Past Continuous** for background: *The market was busy, snow was falling*
- **"Used to" for habits:** not used here the chapter uses "he always..." and "every morning he..."
- **Modal verbs:** *could not, would not, had to* expressing ability and obligation

**Vocabulary from this chapter worth remembering:**
- *manufacturer* a person or company that makes goods in a factory
- *petition* a written request signed by many people
- *suspicion* a feeling that something is wrong or someone is lying
- *evidence* facts or information that show something is true
- *dignity* the feeling of being respected and having self-worth
- *methodically* in a careful, organised, step-by-step way
- *conviction* being found guilty of a crime by a court
- *reluctant* not wanting to do something
- *confession* when you admit to doing something wrong
- *accumulated* collected or gathered over time
