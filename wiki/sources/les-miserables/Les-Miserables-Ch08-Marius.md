# Les Misérables Chapter 8: Marius

**Graded Reader | A2/B1 | ~10,000 words | TTS-friendly**

---

## Before This Chapter

Jean Valjean and Cosette lived safely inside a convent in Paris for several years. Valjean worked as a gardener, and Cosette studied at the convent school. They were hidden from the world. No one looked for them there. Cosette grew from a frightened child into a calm, thoughtful young woman. She called Valjean "Father" and loved him completely.

But convent walls do not last forever. When Cosette was seventeen, Valjean decided it was time to leave. She needed to see the world. She needed a real life.

They moved to a quiet house on the Rue Plumet, near the Luxembourg Garden. The house had a garden full of old trees and long grass. It was private and peaceful. Cosette was happy there.

One afternoon, they went for a walk in the Luxembourg Garden. A young man was sitting on a bench. He looked up. His name was Marius.

---

## Scene 1 The House on the Rue Plumet

The house on the Rue Plumet was old and quiet. It stood at the end of a short lane, behind a tall iron gate. The gate was covered in ivy, and the garden inside was wild. Long grass grew between the stones. Old trees spread their branches over the path. In spring, white flowers opened along the walls.

Valjean chose this house carefully. It was not easy to find. The lane had no name sign. The neighbours did not ask questions. The nearest street was busy, but back here, among the trees, it was silent.

He paid the rent in advance, as always. He used the name Fauchelevent, as always. He told the landlord that he was a retired gentleman and that his daughter was delicate and needed fresh air. The landlord nodded and asked nothing more.

Cosette loved the house immediately. She walked through every room, touched the old wooden furniture, and stood for a long time in the garden. The light came through the branches in golden pieces. She could hear birds singing. She turned to Valjean and smiled.

"It is beautiful," she said.

"We are safe here," he replied.

He meant the same thing.

They settled into a gentle routine. Valjean went out in the mornings, walked by the river, and came back by midday. Cosette read, sewed, and tended the garden. In the afternoons, they sometimes walked to the Luxembourg Garden, which was only a short distance away. It was a large park with wide paths, old statues, and well-kept flower beds. Families came there on Sundays. Students came there on weekdays.

Cosette enjoyed the park. She liked watching the children play. She liked the sound of the fountain. She felt free in a way she had not felt inside the convent. The world, she thought, was larger than she had imagined.

She did not yet know that the world was about to change her life.

---

## Scene 2 A Young Man on a Bench

His name was Marius Pontmercy. He was twenty years old. He was thin and pale, with dark eyes and untidy hair. His coat was old, but he wore it with a kind of dignity. He had very little money. He had even fewer friends.

Marius came to the Luxembourg Garden most days. He liked to sit on a particular bench near the fountain and read. Sometimes he studied his law books. Sometimes he just sat and thought. He was serious by nature, but not unfriendly. He simply had a great deal on his mind.

One Tuesday afternoon in spring, Marius arrived at the garden as usual. He sat on his bench, opened his book, and began to read. After a few minutes, he looked up. He often looked up it was a habit when he was thinking.

An old man was walking slowly along the path. The man was tall and had white hair. He walked carefully, as if he was watching the ground. Beside him was a young woman.

Marius looked at the young woman. He could not look away. She had brown hair that caught the light. She was wearing a simple white dress. She walked easily, without hurry, and she was speaking to the old man beside her. She laughed at something he said. The laugh was soft and real.

Marius stared. He knew he was staring. He could not stop.

The man and the young woman walked the full length of the path, turned, and came back. As they passed Marius's bench, the young woman glanced in his direction. She did not stop. She did not smile. But she looked.

Marius sat very still after they had gone. His book was still open on his knee. He had not read a single word.

He came back the next day. And the day after that.

---

## Scene 3 Marius's History

To understand Marius, you need to know where he came from.

His father was Colonel Georges Pontmercy. The colonel had fought in Napoleon's armies. He was brave and honest. He was wounded three times and survived. After Napoleon's fall, he was given a small pension and sent away to live in the country, far from Paris. He was a soldier with no war to fight, and a father with no son to raise.

He had a son. But the son was not with him.

Marius's mother had died when Marius was a baby. His grandfather, Monsieur Gillenormand, was a wealthy man who lived in the Marais quarter of Paris. Gillenormand had strong opinions about everything, especially politics. He hated Napoleon. He hated soldiers. He hated the revolution. He loved the old kings and the old ways.

When Marius's mother died, Gillenormand came to take the boy. He told Colonel Pontmercy that if he wanted his son to inherit the Gillenormand money, he had to step aside. He told Pontmercy to stay away. The colonel, who had very little money of his own, agreed. He did not want his son to grow up poor.

So Marius grew up with his grandfather. He called Gillenormand "grandfather," went to school in Paris, and studied law. He was a quiet boy who read a great deal. Gillenormand was proud of him in a distant, gruff way. He taught Marius to support the king and distrust all ideas that came from soldiers.

Marius believed him. He knew no better.

He did not know his father at all. He thought of him as a stranger a rough soldier of no importance.

Then his father died.

---

## Scene 4 The Letters

Marius was nineteen when a letter arrived from the country. It said that Colonel Pontmercy was ill and wished to see his son. Gillenormand read the letter and threw it in the fire. He told Marius that the colonel was not an important person. He told Marius to continue his studies.

Marius obeyed. But a few weeks later, another letter came. This one said that the colonel was dying. Gillenormand hesitated. He gave Marius permission to go.

Marius travelled to Vernon. He arrived too late. The colonel had died that morning.

Marius stood in the small, plain room where his father had lived. He looked at the bare walls, the simple furniture, the single window with a view of the garden. His father had lived here for years, alone. He had asked to see his son once, and the answer had been silence.

A local priest came to speak to Marius. He had known the colonel well.

"He was a good man," the priest said. "He came to church every Sunday and sat in the back, in the gallery. He never came forward. But every week he looked at you at your family pew. He came because he wanted to see you."

Marius did not understand. "He came to see me? But I was never here."

The priest looked at him sadly. "Your grandfather brings you to church every Sunday in Paris. Your father knew which pew you sat in. He would take the coach from Vernon early each Sunday morning, attend the service, sit in the gallery above, and watch you. Then he would take the coach back. He did this for years."

Marius stood very still. He could not speak.

"He left you a letter," the priest said. He handed Marius a small, folded piece of paper.

Marius opened it. It was written in his father's hand. The writing was careful and a little unsteady, as if the colonel had taken a long time over every word.

*My son I fought at Waterloo. I was wounded and left on the field. A man named Thénardier carried me to safety. He saved my life. I do not know where he is now. If you ever meet him, help him. He deserves it. Your father, who loves you.*

That was all.

Marius folded the letter and put it in his pocket. He walked out of the house into the grey morning light. He walked for a long time. He thought about a father who had sat in a gallery every Sunday morning just to see his son. He thought about a man who had given up his child rather than let him be poor. He thought about the letters he had never received.

By the time he returned to Paris, he was not the same person.

---

## Scene 5 Breaking with the Grandfather

Marius went home to Gillenormand's house. He said nothing for a few days. He was thinking. Then one evening at dinner, he spoke.

"I have been reading about Napoleon," he said. "I have been reading about my father."

Gillenormand looked up from his plate. His expression changed.

"Your father," he said, "was a soldier. Nothing more."

"He was a colonel," Marius said. "He fought at Waterloo. He was brave. He was honourable."

"He was a Bonapartist," Gillenormand said. The word came out like a stone. "He supported that criminal emperor who destroyed France. We do not speak of him in this house."

"Then I cannot stay in this house," Marius said.

There was a long silence.

Gillenormand's face went red, then white. He stood up from the table. He was old and small, but he looked very large in that moment.

"If you go," he said, "you take nothing from me. Not a coin. Not a coat."

"I do not want anything from you," Marius said.

He packed a small bag that night. He took his books, his father's letter, and very little else. He walked out into the street.

He was nineteen years old. He had twelve francs in his pocket. He had nowhere to go.

---

## Scene 6 Life in the Gorbeau House

Marius found a room near the Gorbeau house, on the Boulevard de l'Hôpital, at the edge of the city. The building was old and dirty. The staircase creaked. The walls were damp. His room was small and low, with one window that looked onto a dark courtyard.

He paid very little rent. This was important, because he had very little money.

For the first few months, he struggled. He ate bread and soup. He sold some of his books. He walked instead of taking the coach. He learned how to live without things. It was not pleasant, but it was not impossible.

Gradually, he found work. He copied legal documents for a notary. He translated articles from German and English for a small newspaper. He wrote summaries of court cases. The work was dull, but it paid enough to eat and to keep his room.

He also read everything he could find about Napoleon and the battles. He read everything about his father's regiment. He learned what his father had done and where he had fought. He felt, for the first time, proud of the name Pontmercy.

A friend found him in this period. His name was Courfeyrac. He was a law student cheerful, talkative, and generous in the way that people are when they are not yet poor. Courfeyrac lived in the Latin Quarter and seemed to know everyone. He brought Marius into his circle of friends.

This circle met at the Café Musain, near the university. They were young men students, mostly who talked about France, about liberty, about the future. They were not criminals. They were not violent. They simply believed that things could be better. They believed that the poor deserved better. They believed that France deserved a republic.

Their group had a name: the Society of the Friends of the ABC. ABC, in French, sounds like *abaissé* the oppressed. It was a small joke that was also serious.

Their leader was a young man named Enjolras. He was twenty-two, handsome, and absolutely certain about everything. He did not joke. He did not hesitate. He spoke about the future of France the way other men speak about the weather as a plain fact.

Marius watched him and listened. He was not yet a revolutionary. He was a student who had just discovered that his father was a hero and that the world was more complicated than he had thought.

He was ready to be changed.

---

## Scene 7 The Luxembourg Garden: First Looks

It was in his second year of poverty that Marius began going to the Luxembourg Garden. He went there to read, because his room was dark and the café was sometimes too loud. He liked the quiet bench near the fountain. He liked the sound of the water.

He had noticed the old man and the young woman before. They came on Tuesdays and Thursdays, in the afternoon. The old man walked slowly. The young woman walked beside him and held his arm when the path was uneven. They were clearly father and daughter. They looked happy together.

Marius paid them little attention at first. He was too absorbed in his reading.

Then one Thursday in early spring, the young woman looked at him as she passed. It was only a glance. But something happened to Marius.

He did not know how to explain it. He had seen many young women before. He had never felt like this before. Her face was clear and open. Her eyes were quick and intelligent. When she smiled at something her father said, the smile went all the way to her eyes. She was not performing happiness. She simply was happy.

Marius came back on Tuesday. He sat on the same bench. He tried to read. He could not read.

The man and the young woman came at the usual time. They walked the usual path. Marius watched them from behind his book. This time, the young woman did not look at him. She was talking to her father about something that made her wave her hand in the air as she spoke. She looked full of life.

Marius walked home slowly that evening. He felt strange lighter and heavier at the same time.

He came back Thursday. He came back the following Tuesday. Each time, he arrived a little earlier and sat a little closer to the path they walked. Each time, he watched them pass. The young woman sometimes glanced in his direction. He was never sure if she was actually looking at him, or simply looking.

He did not know her name. He did not know where she lived. He only knew that she came to the Luxembourg Garden twice a week with her father, and that something about this ordinary fact had become very important to him.

Courfeyrac teased him. "You look like a man in love," he said one evening at the café.

"I am not in love," Marius said immediately.

"Then why haven't you finished that drink in an hour?"

Marius looked at his glass. It was true. He had barely touched it.

---

## Scene 8 The Mistake

After several weeks of watching, Marius decided to act. He could not simply sit on a bench forever. He had to do something. He was not sure what but something.

He walked past their bench one afternoon. He did this slowly and deliberately. He hoped the young woman would look up. She did look up. Their eyes met for a moment.

Then the old man looked up too. His expression changed immediately. It was not angry it was sharp. He had noticed. He had understood.

The following Tuesday, the man and the young woman did not come to the Luxembourg Garden.

Marius waited for two hours on his bench. He watched every person who entered the garden. They did not come. He went back Thursday. They did not come. He returned the following week. Still nothing.

He felt sick with worry. He had ruined everything. He had been too obvious. The old man had seen him looking, and now they would never come back.

He asked Courfeyrac what he should do.

Courfeyrac laughed. "Find out where she lives. Follow them."

Marius stared at him. "Follow them? Like a thief?"

"Like a man in love," Courfeyrac said. "It is different."

Marius thought about it. He decided it was wrong. He decided it anyway.

The next Tuesday, they came back. Marius nearly fell off his bench when he saw them come through the gate. He stood up without thinking. The young woman passed his bench and this time, without question, she looked directly at him. It was a long, clear look. Not a glance. A look.

Marius's heart was doing something very uncomfortable.

After that, the Tuesday and Thursday visits continued. But something had changed between them, although they had never spoken. When she walked past, she looked. When she looked, he looked back. The old man walked ahead, looking at the path, not looking at Marius. Perhaps he did not notice. Perhaps he chose not to.

Weeks passed in this way. Marius was happy and miserable at the same time. He wanted to speak to her. He did not know how. He did not know her name. He did not know anything about her except what he had observed: she was kind to her father, she loved the outdoors, she laughed easily, and she wore white dresses.

One day, she dropped her glove on the path. Marius picked it up. He stood with it in his hand for several seconds, not knowing what to do. He walked quickly after them and held it out.

"Excuse me," he said. "I think you dropped this."

She turned. She looked at the glove, then at him. She said, quietly, "Thank you." She took the glove and walked on.

Those were the only words they ever spoke in the Luxembourg Garden.

Marius walked home feeling that the afternoon had been, in some complicated way, the best of his life.

---

## Scene 9 Disappearance

Then she was gone.

It happened without warning. One Tuesday, they did not come. Marius waited. He told himself it was nothing they were busy, or the weather was wrong. But Thursday also passed without them. The following Tuesday, he arrived at the garden early in the morning and sat there until evening. Nothing.

He went to the garden every day for two weeks. He sat on the bench and watched the gate. He arrived at dawn and left at dark. He forgot to eat. He forgot to study. He forgot everything except the Luxembourg Garden and two people who were no longer there.

He thought about the old man's sharp look, weeks earlier. Had he decided to stop coming? Had he recognised the danger and simply removed his daughter from it?

Marius felt ashamed and desperate at the same time. He had been wrong to stare, he knew that. But he had not thought he was doing any harm. He had only looked. People look at each other in parks. It was ordinary.

But it had not been ordinary, and he knew it.

He went back to Courfeyrac. "They are gone," he said. "I do not know where they live. I do not know her name. I have nothing."

Courfeyrac was more serious this time. He could see that Marius was genuinely suffering. "Do you know anyone who might know them?" he asked. "Anyone from the garden?"

Marius thought. He had noticed one person who sometimes looked at the man and the young woman with a curious expression a young girl who often sat on a bench near the gate. She was perhaps sixteen, thin, with sharp eyes and old shoes. She was not a student. She was not a lady. She looked like someone who belonged to the streets.

"There is a girl," he said. "She always sits near the gate."

"Then find the girl," Courfeyrac said.

---

## Scene 10 Éponine

Marius found her the next day, in the same place by the gate. She was sitting on the bench with her arms crossed, watching the people come and go. Her dress was thin and too large, and she had a small bruise on her cheek. She looked both much older and much younger than her age.

"Excuse me," Marius said. He sat on the bench beside her, at a respectful distance. "I think you sometimes see the people I see here. An old man and a young woman. Thin man, white hair, blue coat. The young woman wears white."

The girl looked at him with sharp, careful eyes. She said nothing.

"I am trying to find them," Marius said. "They do not come here anymore. I am worried about them."

The girl almost smiled at this. "Worried," she repeated. It was clear she did not believe it.

"Yes," Marius said. He did not explain more.

The girl looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, "I know who you mean. I have seen them."

"Do you know where they live?"

She shrugged. "Maybe. What will you give me for it?"

Marius hesitated. He was poor, but he had a few coins. He took out what he could find not much. The girl looked at the coins and seemed to calculate something in her head.

"Come back tomorrow," she said. "I'll find out."

She stood up and walked away through the gate. Her shoes made no sound.

Marius did not know it yet, but her name was Éponine Thénardier. She was the eldest daughter of the inn-keeper he had read about in his father's letter the man who had saved his father at Waterloo. She had grown up at the inn in Montfermeil. She had grown up alongside a little girl called Cosette, who wore rags and carried buckets.

And now she lived in Paris, in a dark house on the Gorbeau block, a few doors from Marius himself.

The world, as it turned out, was very small.

---

## Scene 11 The Thénardiers in Paris

Marius did not yet know his neighbours well. He knew that the family next door was large and loud. He heard shouting through the thin walls. He heard children crying. He heard a man's rough voice late at night.

The family called themselves the Jondrette family. That was not their real name. Their real name was Thénardier. They had left Montfermeil years ago, after their inn failed. They had come to Paris with nothing. They lived by tricks and schemes by writing letters to wealthy strangers, by pretending to be poor artists or ruined soldiers. Sometimes their schemes worked. More often, they did not.

The father, Thénardier, was clever in a low, mean way. He could tell a story that made people reach into their pockets. He had no honest work and wanted none. He spoke about his many misfortunes with great feeling. He blamed the government, the weather, and unlucky circumstances. He never blamed himself.

His wife was large and angry, and she hit the children when she was in a bad mood, which was often.

They had several daughters. Éponine was the eldest and the sharpest. She ran errands for her father. She knew the streets of Paris better than most adults. She could find anyone, if the money was right.

She had not recognised the old man and the young woman from the Luxembourg Garden as having any connection to her past. She had been too young when Cosette left the inn, and Cosette had changed beyond recognition. But she knew the old man's face from the park, and she was good at following people.

She knew which street they lived on. She had noticed, in that careful way of hers, which gate they used and which windows lit up in the evenings.

She told Marius the next day.

"Rue Plumet," she said. "Number 55. There is a gate at the end of a lane. You can hear the garden through the gate in the evening." She paused. "There is also a garden gate at the back, on the lane. It is less obvious."

She said this without expression. But her eyes were watching Marius carefully.

"Thank you," Marius said. He pressed the coins into her hand.

She closed her fingers around them. She did not move to leave. "She is pretty," Éponine said. It was not a question.

Marius said nothing.

Éponine looked at him for another moment. Then she walked away.

---

## Scene 12 The Garden on the Rue Plumet

Marius found the lane on a grey evening. He walked down it slowly, his hands in his coat pockets. The gate at the end was covered in ivy. He could hear the sound of leaves moving in the garden beyond. A light was on in an upstairs window.

He stood at the gate for a long time. He did not go in. He was not a thief. He was not going to climb over walls or force locks. He simply stood there, in the lane, and felt that she was nearby. It was enough, for that evening.

He came back the next day, and the day after. Each time he stood at the lane for a while, then went away. He told himself he was not doing anything wrong. He told himself he was simply walking in the neighbourhood.

Then one evening, the back garden gate was ajar. It had not been latched properly. A small gap opened between the gate and the post. Marius stopped. He looked through the gap. He could see the garden the long grass, the old trees, the path of stone flags. The evening light was gold between the branches.

And then he saw her.

She was sitting on a low stone bench at the far end of the garden, reading. Her book was in her lap. She was very still, except for the slight movement of turning a page. She was close enough that Marius could see the way her hair fell forward as she looked down.

He did not speak. He did not move. He stood at the gate and watched her read, in the evening light, in the garden. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

He came back the following evening. The gate was ajar again. She was there again.

On the third evening, he pushed the gate open, just a little. He stepped onto the garden path. He walked slowly to the stone bench.

She looked up. She was not frightened. She looked at him for a moment with an expression he could not read.

"I know you," she said. "From the garden."

"Yes," he said. He sat down on the bench, a little way from her. He did not know what else to say.

She did not seem to mind the silence.

They sat together in the garden for half an hour, mostly without talking. She told him her name was Cosette. He told her his name was Marius. She asked what he studied. He said law. She said she read a great deal. He said he did too. They spoke about books for a while, in short, uncertain sentences, the way that people do when they are not really talking about books at all.

When the light faded, she stood up and said she had to go inside.

"Will you be here tomorrow?" he asked.

She looked at him. "I might be," she said.

She was there the next evening. And the evening after that.

---

## Scene 13 The Garden Meetings

For six weeks, Marius came every evening to the garden on the Rue Plumet. Cosette was always there. The garden gate was always ajar. Later, he understood that she left it ajar herself. She looked forward to the evenings. She counted the hours.

They talked more easily as the weeks passed. Cosette told him about the convent, about the nuns, about learning Latin and music and needlework. She did not tell him everything she did not yet know everything herself. She knew that she had lived somewhere difficult before the convent, but the memory was vague, like a dream that fades in the morning.

Marius told her about his father and the letters. About the church in Vernon. About leaving his grandfather's house with twelve francs and a bag of books. He told her about the ABC Society and Enjolras, about the long discussions at the Café Musain, about the students who talked about freedom in the evenings and copied legal papers in the mornings.

Cosette listened to everything. She asked good questions. She made him feel that what he said mattered.

One evening in late summer, they were sitting closer together than usual. The sky was turning from blue to dark. The first stars appeared above the trees. Neither of them spoke for a while.

Marius turned to her. He said, very quietly, "I think about you all the time."

Cosette looked at her hands. Then she looked at him. "I think about you too," she said. "Since the Luxembourg Garden. I thought about you every day."

Marius felt the words land in his chest like something solid. He had not known that certainty could feel like this warm, and a little frightening.

"Cosette," he said. He stopped. He could not find the right word after her name. There was no word that was right. So he simply said her name again.

She smiled. It was the real smile, the one he had first seen in the park, the one that went all the way to her eyes.

They sat in the garden until it was quite dark. Then she went inside, and he walked home, and the night seemed full of light.

## Scene 14 What Valjean Noticed

Valjean was not a foolish man. He had survived twenty years by noticing things that other people missed. He noticed when a street was too quiet. He noticed when a face appeared twice in the same afternoon. He noticed when something in the ordinary routine had shifted, even slightly.

He noticed that the garden gate was not latched in the evenings.

He noticed that Cosette came in from the garden later than usual, with grass on her shoes and colour in her face.

He noticed that she had stopped telling him everything.

He said nothing at first. He watched. He waited. He told himself that Cosette was seventeen and that the world was full of young men and that this was natural and that he should not be afraid.

He was afraid.

One evening he came down to the garden quietly. He stood in the shadow of the old tree by the wall. He could see the stone bench. Two young people were sitting on it. They were not doing anything improper. They were just sitting, talking softly. But their voices were low, and their heads were slightly bent toward each other, and the intimacy of the scene was visible from twenty metres.

Valjean stood very still. He watched for a moment. Then he turned and went back inside.

He did not confront Cosette. He did not forbid her. He was not that kind of father. But he went upstairs to his room and sat for a long time in the dark.

He thought about who might be watching them. He thought about Javert. He thought about what Javert's reappearance, months ago on the street, had meant. He thought about how safe the Rue Plumet truly was, now that someone even just a young man from a park had found their address.

He thought about his own old fear: that happiness, when it came, came just before something was taken away.

He also thought, for the first time clearly, that Cosette did not belong only to him. She had her own heart. She had her own future. And her future, he could see now, would not be shaped entirely by him.

This thought was the saddest one he had ever had. And it was, he knew, also the right one.

---

## Scene 15 The Letter

A few weeks later, Cosette found a letter tucked under the garden gate. It had no name on it. She opened it and read it.

It was a love letter. It was Marius's letter. He had written it over three nights. He had torn up many drafts. He had written it in the simple, direct language of someone who has no talent for decoration and a great deal to say.

He wrote that he had been watching her for months, first in the park and then in the garden. He wrote that he knew this was wrong and that he was sorry. He wrote that he had never been in love before and that he had not known it would feel like this like walking in fog and then stepping suddenly into sunlight. He wrote that if she asked him to go away and never come back, he would go, and he would not bother her again. But if she did not ask him to go, he would be in the garden the next evening, as always.

Cosette read the letter twice. Then she sat very still in the garden for a long time.

She had received many lessons at the convent: Latin, music, sewing, scripture. No one had ever taught her what to do when a letter like this arrived.

She knew what she felt. She felt the same.

She went to the garden the next evening. Marius was there. She did not hand the letter back. She did not say it was wrong.

She said, "I read your letter."

"I know," Marius said. He was watching her face.

She did not say anything else for a moment. Then she said, "I felt the same. Since the park. Since the first day."

After that, there was nothing more to explain.

---

## Scene 16 The World Outside the Garden

While Marius and Cosette met in the evening garden, the world outside the Rue Plumet was growing louder.

Marius heard it at the Café Musain. The students talked about a soldier who had been hit by a fruit seller. They talked about the government reducing the rights of workers. They talked about a student called Lamarque who was ill General Lamarque, who was the last important man in France who still spoke openly about the rights of the poor. If Lamarque died, they said, things would break.

Enjolras was calm and certain, as always. He had been making plans for months. He spoke about the barricades as if they were already built. He spoke about the date as if he already knew it.

Marius listened to all of this. He believed in the cause. He had grown up with his father's letter in his pocket, and the letter had made him understand what it meant to fight for something true. But now there was also Cosette, and Cosette had changed the shape of everything.

He could not think about barricades and Cosette at the same time. The thoughts did not fit together.

He told himself there was still time. He told himself that things might not break. He told himself that the summer would continue for a while yet.

He was wrong. The summer was almost over.

---

## Scene 17 Valjean's Decision

Valjean made his decision quietly, as he made all decisions. He thought about it for several days. He looked at Cosette and thought about what her life needed. He thought about the young man in the garden, and about the things he did not know about this young man where he came from, what he wanted, whether he was honest.

He thought most of all about danger.

Someone had been watching the house. He was certain of it. He had noticed a shadow in the lane more than once. He had noticed that the back gate had been used there were marks in the grass. He was not frightened easily. But he was not careless.

One morning, he told Cosette that they were going to stay with an old friend for a while. He told her they would be gone for only a few days. He told her to pack lightly.

Cosette looked at him with quiet eyes. She said, "When are we going?"

"Tonight," he said. "After dark."

She packed carefully. She did not argue. She had learned, over the years, to trust his judgement about danger. But that evening, before they left, she went to the garden alone. She sat on the stone bench in the dark. She pressed her hand flat against the cold stone. She stayed for a long time.

Then she came inside and picked up her bag.

Valjean and Cosette left the Rue Plumet that night. They told no one. They left no note. They simply went.

---

## Scene 18 Marius Arrives

Marius came to the garden the next evening. He pushed the gate open. He walked to the stone bench. He sat down.

No one came.

He waited an hour. He walked the full length of the garden. He called her name softly, once. The garden was silent. The house was dark. The upstairs window, which had always been lit in the evenings, was black.

He went back the following evening. And the one after.

On the third evening, a neighbour came out of a door in the lane and looked at him. Marius asked about the old man and the young woman. The neighbour shrugged. "Gone," she said. "A few nights ago. In the dark. They took everything." She went back inside.

Marius stood alone in the lane.

He walked back through the city. He walked for a long time. He did not go to the café. He did not go home. He walked the streets of Paris in the dark, and the streets said nothing to him.

He thought about everything that had happened in the last few months. He thought about the Luxembourg Garden, the glance, the dropped glove, the two words she had spoken. He thought about the evenings in the garden, the low voices, the stars above the trees. He thought about her letter his letter and her words: *since the first day*.

He thought about the barricade.

The students at the café were right. Things were breaking. General Lamarque had died. The date was set. The word had gone out to all the members of the ABC Society. In three days, perhaps two, there would be barricades in the streets of Paris.

Marius had planned to stay away from the barricade. He had planned to be alive for Cosette. But now Cosette was gone, and he did not know where, and the garden was empty, and the house was dark, and Paris was loud with the sound of something about to break.

He thought: if she is gone, there is nothing left to lose.

He thought: the cause is true. My father fought for something true. I can fight for something true.

He thought: I will go.

---

## Scene 19 The Night Before

Marius went to see his grandfather.

Gillenormand was old now, and tired. He sat in his chair by the fire and looked up when Marius came in. He had not seen his grandson for years. His face showed something complicated pride and hurt and old anger and old love, all mixed together.

Marius stood in the middle of the room. He looked at the old man. He thought about all the years that had been lost because of silence and stubbornness on both sides.

"I came to say goodbye," Marius said.

Gillenormand stared at him. "Goodbye? Where are you going?"

Marius did not answer directly. He said, "I may not come back. I wanted you to know that I am grateful for everything you gave me when I was young. And I wanted you to know that my father was a good man. He was brave and honest. I am proud to be his son."

Gillenormand's face worked through several expressions. He opened his mouth. He closed it. He looked at his hands. He was a proud man, and old habits die slowly in proud men. He said nothing.

Marius waited a moment. Then he said, quietly, "Goodbye, Grandfather."

He turned and walked out. He heard, as he reached the door, a sound behind him. It might have been the old man's voice. But he could not be sure, and he did not turn back.

He walked through the dark streets to the Latin Quarter. He knocked on Courfeyrac's door. He told him that he was coming. Courfeyrac clasped his hand firmly. He did not try to talk him out of it.

That night, Marius sat in his small room for the last time. He looked at his father's letter. He read the words once more: *A man named Thénardier carried me to safety. He saved my life. If you ever meet him, help him.*

He folded the letter and put it carefully in his pocket.

He looked at his books. He did not take any of them. He looked at the window, where the early morning light was beginning to show.

He thought of Cosette. Her face. The garden. The bench. The way she said *since the first day*.

"I am sorry," he said, very quietly, to the empty room. "If you are somewhere safe, stay safe. I hope you are somewhere safe."

Then he put on his coat, picked up the pistol that Javert had left behind in an earlier scene of the story, and went out.

Paris was waiting for him.

## Grammar Focus

### Past Simple vs Past Continuous

The chapter uses **Past Simple** for completed actions and **Past Continuous** for background situations.

**Past Simple** a finished action:
- *Marius opened the letter.*
- *Éponine walked away through the gate.*
- *Cosette looked up from her book.*

**Past Continuous** an ongoing situation in the background:
- *She was sitting on a low bench, reading.*
- *He was watching her from behind his book.*
- *The students were talking about barricades.*

**Both together** one long action is interrupted by a short action:
- *She was reading when Marius came through the gate.*
- *He was walking home when he decided to turn back.*

### "Used to" for past habits

The chapter mentions things that happened regularly in the past:
- *He used to sit on a particular bench near the fountain.*
- *They used to come on Tuesdays and Thursdays.*
- *She used to leave the gate ajar in the evenings.*

Note: **used to + verb** (base form) = a repeated past action that no longer happens.

### Modal: "had to" for past obligation

- *He had to find a room quickly.* (He had no choice.)
- *She had to go inside.* (It was time.)
- *He had to copy legal documents for money.* (He needed money; he had no other option.)
