# Les Misérables
## Chapter 4 Fantine's Story
### Extended A2/B1 Reader | ~10,000 words

---

*Before this chapter: Jean Valjean spent nineteen years in prison. A bishop showed him kindness and changed his life. He became Monsieur Madeleine, built a factory in the northern town of Montreuil-sur-Mer, and became its mayor. He helped many poor people. But Inspector Javert arrived in the town and began watching him with suspicious eyes. At the end of the last chapter, Madeleine learned that an innocent man was about to go to prison in his place. He drove through the night toward the truth.*

*In this chapter, we leave Madeleine for a while. We travel back in time to Paris, and we learn the story of another person a young woman whose life crossed paths with his. Her name was Fantine.*

---

## Part One Paris in Spring

There is a part of Paris where young students live. It is not a rich part of the city. The buildings are old and the rooms are small and the streets are narrow. But in the spring, when the sun comes back after winter, these streets are full of noise and laughter and the smell of fresh bread from the bakers on the corner.

In one of these streets, in the spring of 1817, there was a group of young people who were happy.

They were eight in total. Four young men and four young women. They were students and seamstresses and shop girls. They had very little money, but they were young and healthy and the world seemed large and full of possibilities.

The four young men were students at the university. They studied law and philosophy and literature. They had strong opinions about everything. They argued about politics and art and the meaning of life. They could talk for hours about ideas. They were the kind of young men who believe they are going to change the world, and who are sometimes right.

Their names were Tholomyès, Listolier, Fameuil, and Blachevelle.

The four young women worked with their hands. Two were seamstresses they sewed fine clothes in small workshops. One worked in a flower shop, making decorations for rich people's tables and hats. One sold ribbons and buttons in a little haberdashery on the Rue Saint-Denis.

Their names were Zéphine, Dahlia, Favourite, and Fantine.

Fantine was the youngest of the four. She had long, golden hair and clear blue eyes. She was thin, not because she was sick, but because she ate very little not from sadness, but simply because she had never had very much food in her life and her body had grown used to it. She was gentle and quiet compared to the others. Favourite talked the loudest. Dahlia laughed the longest. Zéphine made the sharpest jokes.

But Fantine was the one people looked at. When she walked into a room, something in the room changed. Not because she tried to be noticed. She did not try. She was simply beautiful in the way that a clear morning is beautiful without effort, without decoration, without knowing it.

She had grown up alone. She did not know who her parents were. She had come to Paris as a young girl and had found work, and had made friends, and had built a small life for herself. She had a room the size of a large cupboard. She owned two dresses. She had a small collection of cheap glass beads that she kept in a box under her bed, and which she wore sometimes on Sundays.

She was happy. Simply and completely happy. She did not ask for much. She had enough to eat, she had friends, and she had the spring.

---

## Part Two Tholomyès

Of the four young men, the most confident was Tholomyès.

He was from the south of France. He was tall, with dark hair and a wide smile. He dressed better than the others and spoke more fluently. He was clever in a way that some people found impressive and others found exhausting. He always had an answer for everything. He always had a story to tell. He liked to be the centre of attention, and he usually was.

When he walked into a café, he chose the best table. When he spoke, the others listened. When he made a joke, everyone laughed not always because the joke was funny, but because Tholomyès was the kind of person people wanted to please.

He chose Fantine.

That was how it felt as if he had looked at the four women and made a selection. He turned his attention toward her the way a bright lamp turns toward a flower. And Fantine, who had never been loved, who had never had anyone choose her for anything, felt the warmth of this attention and moved toward it like a plant moves toward the sun.

He was charming. He brought her small gifts a bunch of violets, a paper of sugared almonds, a pretty printed handkerchief. He took her to the theatre. He held her hand when they walked. He spoke to her as if her opinions mattered, which was more than most men did.

Fantine loved him quickly and completely. She was nineteen years old. She had never been in love before. She had no experience to warn her. She had no mother to advise her. She had only the feeling in her chest when she saw him, which was so strong and so warm that it seemed to her like certainty.

They spent the summer together. They went on walks along the Seine. They had picnics in the Bois de Boulogne with the other couples. They sat in cafés and talked late into the evening. They went dancing on Saturday nights in the working-class dance halls where candles burned in coloured glass jars and the floors shook with the energy of the crowd.

Fantine learned to see the world through Tholomyès's eyes, which were the eyes of someone who found everything amusing. He thought poverty was charming. He thought simplicity was romantic. He thought the working-class neighbourhoods of Paris were picturesque. He visited them as a tourist visits an interesting foreign country with enthusiasm, and with the complete confidence that he could leave whenever he wanted.

Fantine did not know this. Or perhaps she knew it somewhere, but did not want to know it. When you are in love for the first time, you do not look for what is wrong. You look for what is wonderful. And there was plenty of wonderful.

The summer ended. The autumn came. And with the autumn came a change.

Fantine noticed that Tholomyès sometimes seemed impatient. Not with her, exactly, but with the situation. He had been a student in Paris for many years. He talked sometimes about going home to Toulouse, where his family was, where there was a comfortable house and a career waiting for him. He had obligations, he said. His father expected things of him.

Fantine listened and said nothing. She held his hand and listened, and she told herself that all of this was very far away.

---

## Part Three Cosette

The baby came in the winter.

Fantine had known for several months. She told Tholomyès first, alone, one quiet evening in her small room. She sat across the table from him with her hands in her lap and told him, and then she waited.

He was quiet for a long moment. She watched his face. She saw different things move across it surprise, discomfort, and then something that he covered very quickly with a smile.

"Well," he said. "That is something."

He did not say he was happy. He did not say he was unhappy. He said it was something, and then he changed the subject, and Fantine did not press him.

She told Favourite next, who told the others. Zéphine said that babies were wonderful and that everything would be fine. Dahlia said nothing but patted Fantine's hand. Favourite, who was the most practical of the four, said that Fantine needed to think about what she would do after the birth.

"Tholomyès will provide for you," Zéphine said confidently.

"Yes," said Fantine. "Of course."

She believed this because she needed to believe it. And because she loved him. And because she could not imagine any other possibility.

The baby was born in the late winter. It was a girl. Fantine held her daughter for the first time and felt something she had never felt before a love so immediate and so enormous that it seemed impossible her chest could contain it. The baby had dark eyes and very small hands. She made a sound like a small bird.

Fantine named her Cosette.

In the following months, Tholomyès continued to visit. He was affectionate with Fantine and polite about the baby. But Fantine could see she was not blind, even if love makes you want to be that the baby made him uncomfortable in a way he could not hide. He held Cosette awkwardly when offered, for the shortest time possible, and then handed her back.

He had started talking more about Toulouse. About his father's expectations. About the career in law that was waiting for him.

Fantine listened to all of this with a calm face and a frightened heart.

She did not ask him directly what his intentions were. She was afraid of the answer.

---

## Part Four The Letter

It was a Sunday in late summer, one year after the baby was born.

The eight friends had planned an excursion. They were going to spend the day at the village of Saint-Cloud, on the western edge of Paris, where there was a park and a waterfall and an outdoor café that served cold lemonade and grilled fish. It was the kind of summer day that seems, when you are living it, as if it will last forever.

They took a carriage. The women sat inside and the men sat on top, on the driver's box, which they thought was more exciting. The carriage went along the river road with the trees on one side and the water on the other, and everyone was in good spirits.

They had their lunch in the park. The men drank wine and the women drank lemonade. There was bread and cold meat and cheese, laid out on a cloth on the grass. Cosette sat between Fantine's knees and ate pieces of bread that Fantine tore into small shapes for her.

After lunch, the men said they were going to make a surprise.

"A surprise?" said Favourite. "What kind of surprise?"

"A real one," said Tholomyès. He had a happy light in his eyes that Fantine had always found charming. "A magnificent surprise. Wait here. We will be back very soon."

He and Listolier and Fameuil and Blachevelle walked away together, laughing about something among themselves. The four women watched them go.

"Where are they going?" asked Zéphine.

"I don't know," said Favourite. "But Tholomyès is excited about something, so it will probably be ridiculous."

They waited. They talked. They played with Cosette in the grass. They watched other families in the park fathers with small children on their shoulders, old couples walking slowly, young men on horseback showing off.

An hour passed. Then another.

The men did not come back.

After two hours, a boy appeared. He was perhaps twelve years old and was wearing an apron, which suggested he worked at the café nearby. He walked up to the women and looked at them.

"Are you the four ladies who were with the four gentlemen?" he asked.

"Yes," said Favourite.

The boy held out an envelope. "The gentlemen asked me to give you this."

Favourite took the envelope. The boy ran away. Favourite looked at the other women, and then she opened it.

She read it aloud.

The letter was written in Tholomyès's handwriting. It was meant to be funny. It used long words and elaborate phrases. It said that the four men were going home back to their families, their futures, their responsibilities. It thanked the women for the wonderful time. It said goodbye in a joking, literary way that was clearly designed to be clever.

It was not a farewell from a man who had respected them. It was a farewell from a man who had enjoyed himself and was now finished.

Favourite finished reading. She put the letter down in the grass.

There was a long silence.

Then Zéphine said, in a very small voice: "Oh."

Dahlia looked at the ground.

Favourite put her hands flat on the grass in front of her and looked out across the park. Her face was angry and controlled.

Fantine said nothing. She sat very still with Cosette in her lap. She looked at the space where the men had been standing when they walked away. There was nothing there now except a patch of sunlit grass and some pigeons.

She looked down at Cosette. Cosette looked back at her with those dark eyes that were entirely trusting. The baby reached up and touched Fantine's chin with one small hand.

Fantine held Cosette tighter. She did not cry. She sat very still and held her daughter and said nothing at all.

## Part Five Paris Without Love

The weeks after Saint-Cloud were difficult.

Fantine tried to continue as before. She went to work every morning. She brought Cosette to the woman in the next room who looked after her during the day in exchange for a small payment. She worked her hours at the sewing workshop. She collected her wages. She came home.

But the world had changed. Or rather, she had changed. Or rather she could now see the world more clearly, which sometimes feels like the same thing.

She saw how the other women at the workshop looked at her now. Some of them looked with pity. Some looked with judgement. Some turned away when she entered. She had heard words not directed at her, but spoken nearby, loud enough to be heard. Words like "abandoned" and "foolish" and "what did she expect."

She expected nothing. She expected nothing now.

In Paris in 1817, an unmarried woman with a child had a very specific place in society. It was not a comfortable place. The respectable women of the working class looked down on her. The respectable men of the middle class ignored her. The shopkeepers served her last. The landladies raised the rent or asked her to leave.

And she had a child to feed.

Cosette was one year old now. She was healthy and cheerful and she smiled at everything. She had a small laugh that Fantine thought was the most beautiful sound she had ever heard. Cosette did not know that her father had gone. She did not know that the world had a particular opinion of her mother. She knew only warmth and milk and her mother's face.

Fantine worked. She counted every coin. She ate less so that Cosette could eat more. She mended her dresses instead of buying new ones. She sold her glass bead collection the small box of cheap beads she had kept under her bed for years.

It was not enough.

The rent was due. The woman who looked after Cosette needed to be paid. There was a bill from the pharmacy for medicine Cosette had needed during a cold last month. There was the cost of coal for the small stove in the room, without which they would both freeze when winter came.

Fantine looked at these numbers the costs and the wages and she could not make them balance. She tried every way she could think of. She added and subtracted and rearranged. The numbers did not change.

She needed to find more money. Or she needed to find cheaper circumstances. Or both.

She thought about this for many nights, lying awake in her narrow bed with Cosette sleeping beside her.

She thought about other cities. She had heard that the north of France had factories larger than the workshops in Paris, better paid, more stable. A woman alone with no past and no scandal could arrive in a northern town and simply be a worker. She could start again.

But she could not take Cosette to a factory town and look for work. Nobody would hire a woman with a small child. The other workers would resent it. The supervisors would refuse. She had seen this happen to other women.

She needed to find someone to look after Cosette. Someone trustworthy. Someone kind. Someone who would treat Cosette well while Fantine was away earning money to send back.

She thought about this for many nights.

And then she packed her bag. She picked up her daughter. She began to walk.

---

## Part Six The Road North

It was a warm day in late spring when Fantine left Paris. She carried a small bag with her belongings and she carried Cosette in her arms. Cosette was two years old now, and she was getting heavy. But Fantine did not put her down.

They took the northern road out of the city. The road went through the flat countryside north of Paris, through fields of wheat and rye that were just beginning to grow. The sky was very blue. There were birds singing in the hedges beside the road.

Fantine talked to Cosette as they walked. She described everything they passed. She named the flowers in the hedgerows. She described the clouds. She sang songs simple children's songs that she made up as she went along, because she did not know many real ones.

Cosette listened and pointed at things and said words in her small voice.

Other travellers were on the road farmers with carts, merchants, a family on foot going the same direction. Fantine walked with them and apart from them. She was polite when spoken to but she did not seek conversation. She was thinking.

She was thinking about Cosette. About what it meant to leave her somewhere. About whether she could do it.

She loved Cosette more than she had ever loved anything. She had loved Tholomyès, but that love had been young and uninformed. She had not known what love was yet. Now she knew. Love was this this specific small person with dark eyes who weighed heavy in her arms and who said "Mama" in a voice that reached directly into Fantine's chest.

The idea of leaving her felt like taking out her own heart and placing it somewhere, in the care of strangers, and walking away.

But she had no choice. She had thought about this for weeks. She had tried every other option first. She could not stay in Paris and keep Cosette with her the money was not enough and the situation would only get worse. She had to go north. She had to find work. And to find work, she had to look like a woman with no complications.

She told herself it was temporary. Many women did this left their children with a relative or a neighbour while they worked, and came back when they had enough saved. She would send money. She would come back as soon as she could. Perhaps in a year. Perhaps in two years, but not longer.

She told herself this until it almost felt true.

They walked through villages. At midday they stopped at a fountain in a village square and Fantine let Cosette drink from her cupped hands and splash the water, which made her laugh. They ate bread that Fantine had brought from Paris. Cosette ate hers with complete satisfaction and then demanded more.

"That's all for now," said Fantine. "More later."

"More," said Cosette.

"Later," said Fantine. She kissed her daughter's forehead.

In the late afternoon, they came to the village of Montfermeil. It was a small village, quiet and unremarkable. A church, a square, a handful of shops, and an inn on the main street.

---

## Part Seven The Thénardier Inn

The inn was called the Sergeant of Waterloo. It had a painted sign above the door showing a soldier with a sword. The building was old and not very clean, but it had the look of a place that was functioning there were lights in the windows and the smell of cooking came from inside.

In front of the inn, on the step, sat a woman.

She was about thirty years old, large and broad-shouldered, with strong red hands and a sharp face. She was sewing something a child's dress, pink and pretty and she was doing it quickly, with an air of competence. Beside her on the step sat two little girls. They were perhaps three and four years old. They had pink cheeks and matching dresses and they were playing with a doll, passing it back and forth between them with the easy generosity of children who have everything they need.

Fantine stopped. She looked at the two little girls playing. She looked at the woman sewing on the step. She looked at the inn behind them.

And something in her chest loosened just a little. Just enough.

This looked right. This looked like a normal family. The children were clean and healthy and happy. The woman looked capable and solid. This was not a grand house, but it was a real one, with real children living real lives in it.

Cosette was watching the two little girls and their doll. Her dark eyes were wide and interested.

Fantine walked up to the woman.

"Good evening, madame," she said.

The woman looked up from her sewing. Her eyes moved quickly over Fantine her dress, her bag, the child in her arms. The eyes were calculating, but they were not unkind.

"Good evening," said the woman. "Are you looking for a room?"

"I am looking for something else," said Fantine. "I am looking for someone who could care for my daughter. I need to go north to find work, but I cannot take her with me. I would pay for her care every month. Regularly. Without fail."

The woman put down her sewing. She looked at Cosette.

Cosette looked back at her. Then Cosette looked at the two little girls and their doll. She reached out toward the doll with one hand.

The larger of the two girls held the doll out to her. "You can look at it," she said. This was generous of her. Cosette reached for it and touched it gently and looked at the girl with grateful eyes.

The woman on the step watched this and something in her face shifted.

"What is your daughter's name?" she asked.

"Cosette," said Fantine.

"And yours?"

"Fantine."

The woman looked at her. "How old is the child?"

"Two years and three months."

"And how much could you pay?"

Fantine said a number. The woman said it was not enough. Fantine said a higher number. The woman said perhaps. They talked for a while, the way you talk about money when both sides need the arrangement to work. They agreed on a number. Fantine agreed to pay for the first month in advance. She agreed to send money every month without fail.

The woman's name was Madame Thénardier. Her husband the innkeeper was inside, she said. He was a quiet man who left such arrangements to her.

They shook hands.

Fantine looked at Cosette. Cosette had sat down in the dirt beside the two Thénardier girls and was holding the doll carefully, looking at its painted face with complete attention.

---

## Part Eight Goodbye

Fantine spent the night at the inn. She had paid for a room. She wanted one more night with Cosette before she left.

Cosette slept next to her as always, pressed against her side, making small warm sounds in her sleep. Fantine lay awake and looked at the ceiling of the small room. The sounds of the inn came up from below voices, the clatter of dishes, a burst of laughter, then silence.

She thought about the decision she had made. She thought about it from every side. Was it the right thing? Was there another way she had not considered? Was Madame Thénardier really trustworthy?

The woman had seemed capable. The little girls had seemed happy. The inn, though not elegant, was a real working place. There was food here. There was warmth. There were children for Cosette to play with.

She would send money every month. She would write letters. She would come back as soon as she had saved enough. This was a temporary arrangement. Just for now. Just until things were better.

She told herself these things and watched Cosette sleeping.

Cosette's eyelashes were very long. Her cheeks were round. She slept with her mouth slightly open and both arms stretched out to the sides, the way small children sleep completely, without reservation, with their whole body.

Fantine watched her sleep until the room began to grow light.

In the morning, she dressed Cosette in her best dress. It was a white dress with blue flowers on it. It was slightly too small now, but it was still the prettiest one. She combed Cosette's dark hair. She put on her small shoes.

Then she took her down to the innyard where Madame Thénardier was waiting.

The Thénardier girls were already in the yard, playing with a ball. When Cosette appeared, the older girl called to her and the three children were immediately absorbed in a game, running back and forth across the yard. Cosette laughed her small bird-laugh.

Fantine watched for a moment.

Then she knelt down. She took Cosette's face in both hands. She kissed her forehead. She kissed both cheeks. She kissed her once more on the forehead.

Cosette was distracted by the game. "Mama, look!" she said, pointing at the ball.

"I see," said Fantine. "Beautiful."

She stood up. She picked up her bag. She said something to Madame Thénardier something about letters, about the money, about the arrangement. Madame Thénardier nodded and said yes, yes, she understood, everything would be fine.

Fantine walked out of the inn yard. She walked to the gate. She walked through the gate and onto the road.

She did not turn back. She had decided, before this morning, that she would not turn back. Because if she turned back and saw Cosette's face, she would not be able to keep walking. And she had to keep walking. She had no choice.

She walked. The road was straight and empty in the morning light. Her footsteps were quiet on the dry earth.

Behind her, in the yard of the inn, she could hear the sound of children playing.

She kept walking.

## Part Nine Letters and Money

Fantine arrived in Montreuil-sur-Mer on a Tuesday in late spring. She found a room in a workers' boarding house near the factory district. It was very small, but it had a window and a lock on the door and it was cheap. She put her bag on the bed and sat down for a moment.

Then she got up and went to look for work.

She found it at the black bead factory the factory that belonged to Monsieur Madeleine. She was hired the next day. The work was repetitive and required careful hands. She learned it quickly. She was a hard worker. She arrived early and left late. She did not complain.

Every two weeks she was paid. She counted the money carefully. She divided it into parts. One part for rent. One part for food. One part for coal in winter. And a part for Cosette folded into an envelope, addressed to Madame Thénardier in Montfermeil, put in the post at the end of the street.

And then the letters came back.

The first letter arrived six weeks after Fantine left Montfermeil. It was from Madame Thénardier. The handwriting was large and irregular. The letter said that Cosette was well and cheerful. It said that Cosette had had a small cold but was recovered. It said that the costs of caring for a child were higher than expected, and that Madame Thénardier hoped Fantine could send a little more money next month.

Fantine sent more.

The second letter came a month later. It said that Cosette was wonderful and growing. It said that she had formed a strong attachment to the Thénardier girls and was very happy. It also said that the doctor had visited because Cosette had a cough, and that the medicine cost money, and that the price of everything was going up, and that Madame Thénardier was doing her very best but it was difficult.

Fantine sent more.

The third letter said that Cosette needed new clothes for the cold weather, and that her shoes were too small, and that the coal was very expensive this year.

Fantine sold the ribbon she wore in her hair. She sent the money.

The fourth letter said Cosette was ill again. A doctor's bill was included. The number was large.

Fantine stopped buying meat. She ate bread and potatoes and water. She sent the money for the doctor.

The letters always said that Cosette was happy. They always said Cosette was growing well. They always ended with a request for more money.

Fantine worked and sent and ate less and worried.

She wrote her own letters. She asked for details. How tall was Cosette now? Was she talking well? What did she like to eat? Did she sleep through the night? Did she ask for her mother?

The replies were always short and general. "Cosette is fine. Cosette is a good girl. Cosette is very content here."

Fantine told herself this was enough. She told herself to trust. She sent money and worked and waited.

## Part Ten What Was Really Happening

In the inn at Montfermeil, Cosette was not fine.

She was not sick. She was not dying. But she was not fine.

Madame Thénardier had looked at Cosette and at the monthly money and made a calculation. The calculation was this: Cosette cost very little to keep. She ate simple food. She slept in a corner. She needed no expensive things. The money that came from Fantine was more than enough to cover these costs, with a great deal left over.

The left over went to the Thénardier family.

Éponine and Azelma the two Thénardier daughters wore good dresses and had shoes that fit and had dolls and ribbons and warm beds. They ate proper meals at the family table. They went to bed at a reasonable hour. They had what children deserve to have.

Cosette wore what was left over. When Éponine grew out of a dress, the dress went to Cosette. By the time it reached her, it was old and worn and often too large. Her shoes were wrong sizes. In winter, she had one thin blanket.

She ate after the family ate. She ate what was left on the plates. She drank water.

When she was old enough to be useful which Madame Thénardier decided was very young indeed she worked. She swept floors. She scrubbed pots. She carried water from the well. She cleaned the inn's public rooms before the customers arrived in the evening. She peeled vegetables. She carried wood.

She did all of this without complaining. She did not know that it was wrong. She did not know that the Thénardier daughters lived differently from her, in the same house. She knew it, in a factual sense she could see their dresses and their warm meals and their easy lives. But she did not know that this difference was wrong. She thought it was simply how the world was.

She was a very small child. Small children accept the world they are given.

There was one thing that comforted her. The Thénardier daughters sometimes let her play with the doll. Not always. Sometimes. When they were in a good mood and there was nothing more interesting to do. Cosette held the doll very carefully when she was allowed to, as if it might break, or as if it might be taken away which it often was.

She missed her mother. She did not have words for this yet. It was just a feeling in her chest, a reaching-for that found nothing. When the inn door opened in the evening, she sometimes looked up quickly with an expression of hope.

But Fantine never came through the door.

---

## Part Eleven Discovery

Two years passed.

In Montreuil-sur-Mer, Fantine continued to work at the factory. She worked steadily and quietly. She gave nobody any reason to notice her. She kept to herself. She did her work, collected her wages, sent money to Montfermeil, and went back to her room.

She was thinner than when she had arrived. She ate very little. The money she did not send to Montfermeil went to rent and coal and a minimum of food. She had stopped buying anything that was not absolutely necessary. She had sold most of what she owned.

But she was managing. The arrangement was hard, but it was working. She had a job. Cosette was safe. She would save a little more and then she could go back and bring Cosette to live with her. Not yet. But soon.

Then one afternoon, everything changed.

A woman named Madame Victurnien worked in the same part of the factory as Fantine. She was an older woman with sharp eyes and a strong interest in other people's business. She had noticed things about Fantine over the months the way she never talked about family, the letters she sent and received, the extra money she was always trying to save. She had noticed and wondered.

One day, Madame Victurnien spoke to a man who knew someone in Paris. She asked questions. She got answers.

She went to the floor supervisor with what she had found out.

The floor supervisor was a small, officious man who believed strongly in the moral character of the factory's workforce. He had been told he believed sincerely that the workers' behaviour outside the factory reflected on the factory itself. He had rules, and the rules were clear to him.

He called Fantine into his office.

He told her what he had discovered. He used careful, formal language. He said that her situation he paused on this word was incompatible with the standards of the establishment. He said he had no choice. He was letting her go. She could collect her final wages at the end of the week.

Fantine stood in front of his desk and listened to this.

She wanted to ask: could she not speak to Monsieur Madeleine? She had heard that the mayor was a fair man, a kind man, a man who listened. She wanted to explain. She wanted to say: I am a mother trying to provide for my child. Is that wrong? Is that incompatible?

But the supervisor had already turned back to his papers.

She was dismissed. The meeting was over.

She walked out of the office. She walked through the factory floor. The machines went on working. The beads went on moving along their tracks. Nobody looked up.

She went out into the street.

She stood on the pavement outside the factory with her hands at her sides. The afternoon was cold and grey. The street was busy with people going about their ordinary lives.

She counted what she had. She counted what she owed. She counted what Montfermeil expected next month.

The numbers did not balance. They did not come close to balancing.

She began to look for other work.

---

## Part Twelve The Fall

She tried everywhere.

She went to other workshops. She went to shops. She went to houses where they needed laundry done or floors scrubbed. She went to the farms on the edge of town and asked about seasonal work.

Everywhere she went, the answer was the same. No work. Or: there is work, but not for someone like you. Or simply: the door closed before she finished her sentence.

Word had spread. In a small town, word always spreads. The woman from the factory the one who was sent away she has a child somewhere, an illegitimate child, no father. The good women of the town pulled their coats a little tighter when she passed. The men of the town looked at her with a different kind of attention.

She had some savings. They lasted two months.

Then there was nothing.

She could not stop sending money to Montfermeil. She could not stop, because if she stopped, what would happen to Cosette? She received a letter from Madame Thénardier that said Cosette needed a wool dress for the winter. The price given was not small.

Fantine looked at the letter for a long time.

Then she went to the hairdresser.

The hairdresser was a man who had a sideline in buying hair. He paid well for long, healthy, naturally coloured hair, which he sold to wig-makers. Fantine had very long, golden hair. It reached below her waist. She had always been proud of it. It was the thing people complimented her on most.

She sat down in the chair. She asked the man to cut it all.

He cut it. He paid her. He held the long golden rope of her hair in his hands and measured it with a professional eye.

She looked at herself in the mirror afterwards. She barely recognised the face she saw. Her head looked too small now. Her neck looked too bare. She looked older and harder and more like what she had become.

She sent the money to Montfermeil.

A month passed. Then came another letter. Another crisis. Another demand.

This time she went to the dentist. There was a dentist who paid for teeth healthy, white teeth, which were also sold on, used in dentures for wealthy people who had lost their own. Fantine had two very fine front teeth. She had always had a beautiful smile.

She sat in the chair. She gripped the arms. She did not look away.

She sent the money.

---

The months that followed were the worst of her life. And her life had already had very bad months.

She lived in two rooms, then one room, then a corner of a room shared with other women in similar circumstances. She ate once a day, sometimes. She wore the same clothes until they were beyond repair. She was cold in winter and exhausted always.

She developed a cough. It was not a serious cough at first. Just a tickle in her throat that came back every morning. She ignored it. She could not afford to pay attention to a cough.

The cough got worse. She noticed, in the evenings, that she was more tired than she had been. She noticed that her face in other people's window glass looked pale in a specific way a grey-white that she did not like.

She told herself it was nothing. She was tired. She needed more food. Things would get better.

A Montreuil winter night. It was very cold. A group of young men was standing outside a café on the main street. They had been drinking. One of them a young man in a fine coat, the son of someone important in the town was bored and looking for amusement.

He saw Fantine passing. He looked at her in the specific way that men sometimes look at women who are alone on cold streets at night. He said something to his friends. They laughed. Then he said something directly to her.

What he said was cruel. It was the kind of thing that was designed to humiliate. It took everything about her situation her poverty, her appearance, her circumstances and made a joke of it, loudly, for his friends to appreciate.

Fantine stopped. Something broke in her. She had been patient for so long. She had endured so much the dismissal, the rejection, the cold, the hunger, the loneliness. She had endured all of it quietly because she was trying to survive, because she was trying to provide for Cosette, because she had no choice.

But she had a limit. Everyone has a limit.

She said something sharp back to him. He laughed. He stepped toward her. She was very small and he was large and his friends were there. She scratched him. She was not proud of this. She simply did it, because he came too close and she was frightened and exhausted and done with being quiet.

The police arrived quickly. Inspector Javert was there within minutes. He had been watching from across the street.

"Arrest her," said the young man. "She attacked me."

Javert looked at the scene. He looked at the young man's coat and at Fantine's dress. He made his assessment in less than a second.

He put his hand on Fantine's arm. "You are under arrest," he said. "Six months."

Six months. The words fell on her like stones. Six months. Who would send money for Cosette? Who would keep the arrangement with the Thénardiers? Who would bring Cosette home?

"Please," she said. She turned to Javert. Her voice was low and very controlled, because she was holding herself together by force. "Please. I have a daughter. She needs me. I cannot go to prison."

Javert looked at her without expression. He had heard this kind of thing many times. He had a rule: the law was the law. She had committed an offence. She would be punished. That was how the world worked.

He began to lead her away.

"Please," she said again. She was not crying. She was past crying. "Please."

A voice came from behind them.

"Release this woman."

Javert stopped. He turned around. A man was standing a few metres away. He was a large man, well dressed but without show. His face was calm and serious.

Javert recognised him. It was Mayor Madeleine.

"Monsieur le Maire," said Javert, with a slight stiffness. "This woman has committed "

"I heard what happened," said Madeleine. "There were witnesses. She was provoked. She did nothing wrong. Release her."

There was a very short, very tense silence. Javert and Madeleine looked at each other. Fantine stood between them, very still.

Javert released her arm.

Madeleine did not look at the young man in the fine coat. He looked at Fantine. He looked at her the way a doctor looks at a patient seeing not the surface, but what is underneath.

"Can you walk?" he asked her.

"Yes," she said.

"Come with me," he said. "We are going to the hospital."

She did not argue. She was too tired to argue. She walked with him through the cold night streets of Montreuil-sur-Mer, through the darkness, toward the building with warm lights in the windows.

## Part Thirteen The Hospital

The hospital was warm. There were beds with clean sheets. A nurse showed Fantine to a bed and told her to lie down.

Fantine lay down. She could not remember the last time she had been in a bed with clean sheets. Perhaps never. The pillow was soft under her head. The blanket was heavy and warm.

She closed her eyes for a moment. Just a moment.

When she opened them, Madeleine was sitting in a chair beside the bed. He had taken off his hat. He was looking at her with that same calm, direct attention.

"Tell me about your daughter," he said.

She told him. She told him about Cosette. About leaving Paris. About the road to Montfermeil. About the Thénardier inn. About the letters and the money and the hair and the teeth and the years.

She told him all of it. She was not sure why she told him all of it. Perhaps because he was the mayor and she was too tired to be careful. Perhaps because he was listening in a real way not the polite half-listening of most people, but actually receiving what she said, actually understanding it.

She told him everything.

When she finished, the room was quiet. The nurse moved in the corridor outside. Somewhere in the hospital, someone coughed.

"I want to see my daughter," Fantine said. Her voice was very quiet now. "She is small and she needs me. I want to bring her home."

Madeleine looked at her. He could see what the doctor had already seen. The grey-white face. The thinness. The cough that she tried to suppress but could not entirely hide.

He did not say what he was thinking. He thought it would not help her to hear it right now.

"I will go to Montfermeil and bring her to you," he said. "I promise."

Fantine looked at him. Her dark-circled eyes were very clear. "You promise?" she said.

"I promise," he said.

She closed her eyes. She pulled the blanket closer around herself.

"Thank you," she whispered.

Madeleine sat beside the bed for a while longer. The nurse came and brought a cup of warm broth. Fantine drank some of it. The warmth spread through her thin body.

Outside the window, the streets of Montreuil-sur-Mer were dark and quiet. Snow had begun to fall. Small, silent flakes touched the window glass and disappeared.

Fantine lay still in her clean bed and thought about Cosette. She saw her as she was the last morning in the white dress with blue flowers, running across the inn yard with the Thénardier girls. Cosette's small laugh. Her dark eyes. The way she had reached out to touch the doll so gently, as if she was afraid to break it.

She was coming home. Madeleine had promised. She just needed to hold on until she arrived.

She held on.
