# Les Misérables
## Chapter 6 Little Cosette
### Extended A2/B1 Reader | ~10,000 words

---

*Before this chapter: Jean Valjean stood up in a courtroom in Arras and said "I am Jean Valjean." He freed an innocent man. He was arrested. But he escaped again. He rode back to Montreuil-sur-Mer, sat with Fantine in her hospital bed, and promised to bring Cosette to her. Then Inspector Javert arrived and arrested him. Fantine died. Valjean escaped from the police post during the night and disappeared into the dark.*

*He had made a promise to a dying woman. He had not kept it yet. Cosette was still at the Thénardier inn in Montfermeil wearing old clothes, carrying water, sleeping in the cold.*

*This chapter takes place many months later. It is winter. It is almost Christmas.*

---

## Part One After the Escape

When Jean Valjean left Montreuil-sur-Mer that night, he had very little.

He had his bag, which contained a change of clothes, some money, and the two silver candlesticks wrapped in cloth. He had a horse, which he had borrowed from a farmer on the edge of town. He had the dark road ahead of him and the cold night around him.

He rode south at first, to confuse anyone who might follow him. Then he turned east. Then north again, through roads he did not know, through villages he had never seen. He rode until the horse was tired and then he walked, leading the animal by the reins.

He had no plan. He had a promise.

The promise was everything. It was the only clear thing in his head. He had promised Fantine that he would go to Montfermeil and bring Cosette to her. Fantine was dead now. He had not kept the promise in time. But the promise still existed. It was not the kind of promise that died with the person you made it to. It was the other kind.

He found a small village far from any main road. He spent three days there, resting, thinking, and letting the horse recover. He ate bread and drank water and slept on straw in a barn. The farmer who owned the barn did not ask questions. Valjean paid him generously and the farmer was satisfied.

On the third night, Valjean sat in the barn with a candle not one of the bishop's candlesticks, which he kept wrapped and would not use for ordinary light, but a cheap tallow candle from the village shop. He sat and thought.

He thought about his situation very carefully. He was now, officially, a fugitive. He had escaped from police custody twice. His face was known to the police of Arras. His face was known to Inspector Javert. The story of the mayor of Montreuil-sur-Mer who had turned out to be Jean Valjean would be travelling through the police communication system right now, from prefecture to prefecture, from inspector to inspector.

He could not go back to Montreuil-sur-Mer. He could not go anywhere he had been before.

He had money. Not a great deal, but more than most people in France had. Over his years as Madeleine, he had been careful. He had kept a private reserve not out of greed, but out of the deep instinct for survival that eight years in prison had given him. He had known, some part of him had always known, that there might come a day when he needed to run again.

He counted the money in the candlelight. It was enough. Enough to live on for several years, if he was careful. Enough to go somewhere new and start again. Enough to take a child and care for her.

He had no family. He had no friends who were still safe to contact. He had nobody in the world.

But there was a child in Montfermeil. A child with dark eyes and thin arms who had been left with strangers by a woman who loved her completely. A child who did not know that the woman who loved her was dead. A child who did not know that anyone was coming for her.

Valjean put the money back in his bag. He blew out the candle. He lay down on the straw.

In the morning, he would go to Montfermeil.

## Part Two The Village of Montfermeil

Montfermeil was a small village about fifteen kilometres east of Paris. It sat on a low hill in the middle of flat farmland. In summer, the fields around it were green and full of wheat. In winter, they were brown and empty and the wind moved across them without anything to stop it.

Valjean arrived in the village on the afternoon of Christmas Eve.

He had walked the last part of the journey on foot, leaving his horse at a farm outside the village. He did not want to arrive on horseback. A man on horseback attracted attention. A man on foot, with a bag on his back and old boots on his feet, was just a traveller. There were always travellers on the roads, especially at this time of year, when people moved from place to place to visit family or find work.

He walked into the village slowly and looked around.

Montfermeil was not a poor village, but it was not rich either. There were perhaps thirty or forty houses. A small church in the centre. A baker. A blacksmith. A general shop. And on the main street, a little way from the church, an inn.

The inn had a sign above the door. The sign showed a soldier and the words: THE SERGEANT OF WATERLOO. It was a common name for inns in France in those years. The Battle of Waterloo had happened many years ago, but people still talked about it, especially innkeepers who needed a name that sounded patriotic and memorable.

Valjean stood outside the inn for a moment and looked at it.

The building was old and not well maintained. The plaster on the front wall had cracks in it. The windows were small and the glass in them was uneven, the way old glass always is slightly wavy, slightly distorted. Inside, he could see firelight and the shapes of people sitting at tables.

He did not go in immediately. He walked around the village first. He wanted to see the place, to understand it, before he did anything.

He walked along the main street. He passed the baker, which was closed for the evening. He passed the church. He walked down a side street and found the back of the inn, and behind that, a courtyard with a well.

He remembered the well from the description if it could be called a description that he had pieced together from Fantine's letters and what little he knew of the situation. The Thénardiers had a child in their care. A little girl. Dark hair. Dark eyes. She was eight years old now.

He stood in the street and looked at the back of the inn for a moment.

Then he continued his walk around the village. He needed to think before he acted.

## Part Three The Life of the Inn

The Sergeant of Waterloo was a working inn. That meant it was never completely quiet.

During the day, there were deliveries bread from the baker, firewood from the woodcutter, barrels from the wine merchant. There were local men who came in the mornings to drink a cup of hot wine and talk before going to work. There were travellers who stopped for a meal or a room. There were neighbours who came in the evenings to sit by the fire and play cards.

The innkeeper, Thénardier, managed all of this with the particular energy of a man who is always slightly desperate for money. He was a medium-sized man with a thin face and sharp eyes. He had been a soldier, or so he said he mentioned Waterloo when it seemed useful and kept quiet about it when it did not. He had a talent for sensing what people wanted to hear and saying it. This made him a reasonable innkeeper, if not always an honest one.

His wife, Madame Thénardier, was a large, loud woman with strong arms and a sharp tongue. She ran the kitchen and the practical operations of the inn. She was not cruel in the dramatic way of stories she did not shout for pleasure or hit for the satisfaction of it. She was indifferent. And indifference, applied to a child for years, has its own particular cruelty.

Their daughters Éponine, who was now nine, and Azelma, who was eight were comfortable and slightly spoiled. They had warm rooms and plenty of food. They had dolls and ribbons and the general confidence of children who have never had to worry about anything.

And then there was Cosette.

## Part Four Cosette

Cosette was eight years old on that Christmas Eve. She was small for her age noticeably small, the kind of small that comes from not eating quite enough for several years. Her arms were thin. Her face was thin. But her eyes were not the eyes of a thin or diminished child. They were very large and very dark, and they looked at the world with an alertness that was both intelligent and somehow cautious. She had learned, over many years, to watch things carefully before she responded to them.

She had dark hair that was cut short and practical. There was no one to braid it or style it, and nobody thought about it, so it was simply cut when it became inconvenient and left alone the rest of the time.

Her clothes were what the Thénardier daughters had outgrown. This meant they were always slightly wrong too large here, too small there, worn at the elbows and the knees. She had a grey dress that had once belonged to Éponine and before that to someone else. She had a brown apron over it, for working. She had wooden shoes on her feet, the kind that all poor children wore, which made a clacking sound on the stone floor when she walked.

She worked.

This was the central fact of her life at the inn. She worked from early morning until late at night. There was always something to do always a floor to sweep, a pot to scrub, a table to wipe down, water to carry, wood to bring in from the courtyard. She did these things without being asked, because she had learned that being asked was uncomfortable and not being asked having everything done before anyone noticed it needed doing was safer.

She was not unhappy in the dramatic sense. She did not weep every day. She did not lie awake at night consumed by grief. Children are adaptable in a way that adults sometimes forget. Cosette had adapted to her life. This was her life. She knew no other.

But she knew something was missing. She felt it in a general, unspecific way that she did not have words for. She felt it when she looked at Éponine and Azelma sitting by the fire with their toys. She felt it when she saw a mother and daughter walking through the village holding hands. She felt it most sharply, most specifically when she thought about the word "Mama."

She thought about it sometimes. Not every day. But sometimes.

She did not have a clear memory of her mother's face. She had been very young when Fantine left. She had a feeling a kind of warmth in her chest when she thought about it that was not quite a memory and not quite an imagining. Just a feeling. A warm feeling in the shape of a person.

She held onto it carefully, the way you hold something small and delicate when you're afraid of breaking it.

## Part Five The Doll in the Window

On Christmas Eve, the village of Montfermeil had a small fair.

It was not a large or grand affair. It was the kind of fair that small French villages had in those years a few stalls in the square near the church, selling gingerbread and roasted chestnuts and small toys. There were candles everywhere, in the windows of the houses and on the stalls themselves. The air smelled of wood smoke and roasted nuts and cold.

The children of the village were allowed to walk through the fair with their parents or older siblings. Some of them had small coins to spend. They moved between the stalls with the particular happiness of children on a special occasion not loud or frantic, but glowing. Quietly, warmly glowing.

Cosette was not allowed to go to the fair.

She had asked. She had asked carefully and at the right moment, when Madame Thénardier was in a reasonable mood after a good lunch. She had asked if she might go and look, just look, at the stalls for a little while. She would come back quickly. She would not spend any money because she had none.

Madame Thénardier had not said yes or no. She had said: the kitchen floor needs scrubbing. Go and scrub it.

Cosette had scrubbed it.

Later, when the kitchen was clean and the evening preparations were done and Éponine and Azelma had gone with their father to look at the fair, Cosette stood at the kitchen window and looked out at the lights in the square. She could see the stalls from there, indistinctly, through the old wavy glass. She could see the light and the movement. She could hear, very faintly, the sound of voices and laughter.

She stood at the window for a while. Then she turned away and went back to her work.

---

There was a shop on the main street of Montfermeil, next to the baker. The shop sold general goods thread and ribbon, candles, small tools, household items. And in the window of this shop, for Christmas, the owner had placed a display.

The display was centred on a doll.

The doll was magnificent. It was large as large as a real baby with a porcelain face and real hair, brown and silky, arranged in careful ringlets. Its eyes were blue and opened and closed when you tilted it. It wore a dress of pink silk with a sash and little leather shoes on its feet. It had a small bonnet.

It was the most beautiful thing in Montfermeil. Perhaps in the world. At least, that was how it looked to the children who pressed their faces against the shop window to see it.

Éponine and Azelma had pressed their faces against that window many times in the weeks before Christmas. They had asked their father. Their father had looked at the price and said: perhaps. Their mother had looked at the price and said: we will see. This meant, for children who understood the language of their parents, that it was possible.

Cosette had looked at the doll exactly once.

She had been walking past the shop on an errand for the inn carrying a small parcel to the blacksmith's wife when she stopped and looked in the window. She stood there for a moment, perfectly still, looking at the doll.

It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen. She did not touch the glass. She did not press her nose against it. She just looked, carefully and completely, in the way that you look at something when you know you are only allowed to look once.

Then she picked up her parcel and continued to the blacksmith's house.

That had been three weeks ago.

She had not looked at the doll again. She had made a decision, without being conscious of making it, that once was enough.

## Part Six Christmas Eve at the Inn

The inn was busy on Christmas Eve. Several travellers had come through during the afternoon, asking for rooms. Thénardier had taken them in, at Christmas prices. The main room was full of people eating and drinking. There was noise and warmth and the smell of roasting meat.

Cosette worked through all of it.

She cleared tables and brought drinks and swept up the things that fell on the floor. She carried logs in from the courtyard for the fire and brought water up from the kitchen for the guests upstairs who needed to wash. She moved through the busy room quietly and efficiently, like a small grey ghost that everyone used but nobody particularly noticed.

The Thénardier daughters sat near the fire, on the good side of the room, playing with their toys. They had received some gifts already small things, ribbons and sweets. They were waiting for Christmas morning with the confident patience of children who have always received what they expected.

At some point in the early evening, when the main rush of the dinner service was over, Madame Thénardier found Cosette sitting on the kitchen step with her knees pulled up to her chest, looking at the fire through the kitchen doorway.

"Why are you sitting?" said Madame Thénardier.

Cosette looked up. "The tables are done," she said. "The floor is swept. The wood is in. I didn't know what else to do."

"The water barrel in the hall is half empty. Go and fill it."

Cosette got up. She took the bucket from its hook. She went to the door.

Outside, it was dark and very cold. Snow had been falling earlier in the evening and had stopped, leaving a thin white covering on everything. The courtyard behind the inn was quiet. The well was at the far end of it, next to the back gate that led to the forest path.

Cosette walked across the courtyard. Her wooden shoes made small prints in the thin snow. She could hear, from the front of the inn, the sounds of the fair still going on in the square voices, and the distant ringing of a bell from the church.

She reached the well. She attached the bucket to the rope and lowered it down. She could hear it splash as it hit the water far below. She waited for it to fill. Then she began to pull it up.

The bucket, when full of water, was very heavy. Cosette was small and thin and not strong in the way that well-fed children are strong. She pulled with both hands. The rope cut into her palms. The bucket came up slowly, swinging and splashing.

She got it over the edge of the well and set it down on the stone ledge. She rested for a moment, breathing hard. Her hands were cold. The metal handle of the bucket was very cold.

She picked up the bucket. She began to walk back across the courtyard.

The bucket was heavy. She walked slowly, leaning to one side to balance the weight. Water sloshed over the rim and hit the snow and her shoes. Her arms ached. She was about halfway across the courtyard when she stopped.

Not because she needed to rest though she did. She stopped because she heard a sound.

A footstep. On the other side of the back gate.

## Part Seven The Stranger

The gate opened.

A man came through it. He was tall very tall, taller than most men she had seen and he had white hair, though his face was not very old. He wore a dark travelling coat that was dusty from the road. He carried a bag on his back. He moved carefully and looked around the courtyard before he came all the way through the gate, the way a person looks when they are not entirely sure of their welcome.

He saw Cosette immediately. She was standing in the middle of the courtyard with the heavy bucket, looking at him.

They looked at each other for a moment.

"Good evening," said the man. His voice was low and quiet.

"Good evening, monsieur," said Cosette. She said it the way she said everything to adults carefully, without expression, in the polite tone that meant: I am being respectful, please don't be angry with me.

The man looked at the bucket. He looked at her thin arms and her small hands holding the handle. He looked at her grey dress and her wooden shoes, which were wet from the snow and the spilled water.

"Is that bucket very heavy?" he said.

"Yes, monsieur," said Cosette.

"Give it to me," he said. He walked across the courtyard toward her. "I will carry it."

Cosette did not move for a moment. Adults did not usually offer to carry things for her. Adults usually asked her to carry more things, and heavier things. She was not sure how to respond to this.

"It needs to go inside," she said carefully. "To the hall."

"I know," he said. He reached down and took the handle of the bucket from her hands. He lifted it easily, the way you lift something that is light, without effort, without changing his posture or his breathing.

He walked to the back door of the inn. He held it open and waited.

Cosette looked at him. She was not frightened of him, which was interesting. She did not know why she was not frightened. He was a large man and a stranger and it was dark. But his eyes, when he had looked at her, had not been the eyes of a person she needed to be frightened of. They were serious eyes. Tired eyes. But not dangerous ones.

She walked through the door. He followed her with the bucket and set it down in the hall, where the water barrel stood.

"Thank you, monsieur," she said.

"What is your name?" he said.

"Cosette," she said.

He was quiet for a moment. Just one second a very small pause. If you had not been watching carefully, you would not have noticed it.

"Are you staying here?" she said. She asked because she needed to know if he was a guest, she would need to tell her mistress.

"Yes," he said. "I would like a room."

"I will tell Madame," she said. She turned and walked down the hall toward the main room.

## Part Eight The Room

Thénardier gave Valjean a room without hesitation. A travelling man with money was always welcome, and Valjean had the look of a man with money not a rich man, but not a poor one either. He paid for a week in advance, which was unusual and therefore gratifying. Thénardier gave him the best room on the upper floor, the one with a window that looked onto the street.

Valjean put his bag on the bed. He took out his coat and hung it on the hook behind the door. He sat in the chair by the small window and looked down at the street below.

The fair was still going on in the square. He could see the lights from here. He could see people moving between the stalls. He watched for a while, not thinking about anything in particular, just watching the lights.

Then he thought about Cosette.

He had not planned to meet her in the courtyard. He had planned to arrive quietly, get a room, observe the situation for a day or two, and then approach Thénardier about taking the child. He had thought this through carefully during the long walk to Montfermeil. He had a plan.

But then he had come through the back gate because the front of the inn was too busy and too bright, and he had found a small girl in the dark courtyard carrying a bucket that was too heavy for her, in shoes that were wet through, in a dress that was too thin for the cold.

He sat in the chair and looked at the lights of the fair.

He thought about Fantine. He thought about the look on her face in the hospital when she asked: did you go for Cosette? He thought about her voice saying: please bring her to me. He thought about the way her face had looked in the last moment before Javert came into the room open and trusting and frightened all at once.

He had not been able to keep the promise to Fantine in the way he intended. But he could keep it in the only way that was still possible. He could take Cosette. He could bring her somewhere safe. He could look after her the way Fantine would have wanted.

He went to bed.

He slept better than he had slept in months.

## Part Nine Christmas Morning

He came downstairs early the next morning. It was Christmas Day.

The inn was quiet. Most of the previous night's guests were still asleep. The fire in the main room had died down to embers. The tables needed to be set for breakfast.

And at the far end of the main room, sitting alone on a low stool in the corner, was Cosette.

She had a small pile of firewood beside her that she was supposed to be sorting. But she was not sorting firewood. She was sitting very still, looking at something in her hands.

Valjean walked closer.

She heard him and looked up quickly, with a slight flinching movement the movement of someone who is used to being surprised by adults and has learned to react fast. But when she saw it was the man from the night before, the movement stopped and she just looked at him.

In her hands was a small piece of wood. A scrap, really. A leftover from the firewood pile. She had wrapped it in a piece of old cloth that she must have found somewhere. She was holding this bundle in her arms the way you hold a baby. Or the way you hold a doll.

Valjean understood immediately. He looked at the piece of wood wrapped in cloth and then at the little girl holding it.

"Is that your doll?" he said. He asked it simply, without mockery and without excessive gentleness. Just the question.

Cosette looked at the bundle in her hands. She seemed to consider the question seriously.

"Yes," she said, after a moment. "She is called Jacqueline."

"That is a good name," said Valjean.

He sat down in a chair near her not too close, not trying to be more familiar than she was comfortable with. He looked at the fire for a moment.

"Have you had Jacqueline for a long time?" he said.

"A little while," said Cosette. She held the bundle a little more carefully. "I made her myself."

"She looks well made," said Valjean.

Cosette looked at him with her large dark eyes. She was deciding something. He could see it in her face some kind of calculation that small children make, the kind that asks: is this person safe? Is this person real?

"The Mademoiselles Thénardier have a real doll," she said, after the calculation was done. "In the shop on the street. It is very beautiful. It has a pink dress and real hair." She paused. "But it belongs to them."

"Yes," said Valjean.

"Jacqueline is mine," said Cosette. She said it simply, without sadness and without defiance. Just as a fact. Jacqueline was hers. That was enough.

Valjean looked at the bundle in her arms. He looked at her wooden shoes, still damp from the night before. He looked at her thin wrists. He looked at her face intelligent, careful, waiting.

He made a decision.

## Part Ten The Doll

He put on his coat and went out into the village.

The Christmas morning was cold and clear. The thin snow from the previous night was still on the ground, though it was melting at the edges where the sun touched it. The square was quiet. The fair stalls were closed and covered up.

But the general shop was open. It opened early even on Christmas Day because the owner, a practical man, understood that holidays were days when people wanted things they had not thought to buy before.

Valjean went into the shop. The owner was behind the counter, arranging some items on a shelf. He turned when Valjean came in.

"Good morning," said Valjean. "I want to buy the doll in the window."

The owner looked at him. "The large doll?" he said. "The one in the pink dress?"

"Yes," said Valjean.

The owner came out from behind the counter and looked at the window display. He looked at the doll. He named a price.

It was not a small price. It was a price that assumed the buyer was wealthy or very determined. In the village of Montfermeil, almost no one could afford it. The owner had put it in the window more for display than with any real expectation of selling it.

Valjean paid it without negotiating. He paid in cash, from the money in his coat pocket.

The owner looked at the money in his hand with some surprise. Then he took the doll carefully from the window display. He wrapped it in paper. He tied it with a ribbon.

He handed it to Valjean.

Valjean walked back to the inn. He came through the front door and into the main room, where Cosette was now setting tables for breakfast. She had put Jacqueline the piece of firewood wrapped in cloth on the floor in the corner while she worked.

She saw him come in. She continued setting the table. She had learned not to stop working just because something was happening in the room.

Valjean walked to the corner where she was working. He set the wrapped parcel on the nearest table.

"This is for you," he said.

Cosette stopped. She looked at the parcel. She looked at him.

"For me?" she said.

"Yes," he said.

She put down the napkins she was holding. She came to the table slowly. She looked at the parcel again. She looked up at him. There was in her face a particular kind of confusion the confusion of someone who is offered something they have not expected and cannot quite believe is real.

She touched the paper wrapping with one finger.

"Open it," he said.

She pulled the ribbon. She folded back the paper, carefully, the way you open something when you want to make sure the opening itself does not damage what is inside.

She saw the pink silk. She saw the hair the real, silky, curled hair. She saw the porcelain face and the small leather shoes and the bonnet.

She did not move.

She stood with her hands on either side of the open parcel and looked at the doll. A long moment passed. And then, without making a sound, without crying, without any dramatic expression at all, she picked the doll up. She held it against her chest with both arms. She pressed her cheek against the porcelain face.

She stood there holding the doll and said nothing.

From the kitchen door, Madame Thénardier had seen this. She appeared in the room with her arms crossed and an expression of deep suspicion.

"What is this?" she said. She looked at Valjean. "Who gave her that?"

"I did," said Valjean.

Madame Thénardier looked at the doll. It was expensive she knew that doll, she had looked at the price herself. She looked at this quiet, white-haired man who had paid for a week's room in advance last night. She looked at Cosette, who was still holding the doll and had not looked up.

She made a calculation. There was a guest who was apparently wealthy and who was interested in the child. This was something to handle carefully.

"How kind," she said. Her voice was different now smooth and social. "Cosette, what do you say to the gentleman?"

Cosette looked up. She looked at Valjean.

"Thank you," she said. Her voice was very quiet. It was the most serious thank you he had ever heard.

## Part Eleven Three Days

Valjean spent three days at the Sergeant of Waterloo before he approached Thénardier about Cosette.

During those three days, he observed.

He watched how Cosette moved through the inn always working, always attentive to what needed to be done, never idle unless she had made certain that everything was finished. He watched how she ate quickly and with concentration, the way people eat when they are genuinely hungry and have not always had enough. He watched how she slept, one afternoon when the kitchen door was open and he could see her curled up on her thin straw mattress in the corner of the kitchen, her new doll pressed against her chest.

She carried the doll everywhere within the inn. Not in the way that spoiled children carry toys lazily, as if they might put it down at any moment but with continuous, careful attention. She put it on the shelf near her sleeping place when she needed both hands to work, and she looked at it while she worked, and she picked it up again the moment she was free.

Éponine and Azelma had noticed the doll, of course. They had come to look at it. Éponine had asked to hold it. Cosette had given it to her, very carefully, with the specific expression of someone who is giving away something precious and watching every second to make sure it comes back safely. Éponine had held it for two minutes and then given it back. She had her own toys. This one, she could see, belonged to Cosette in a way that made it less interesting to take.

Valjean also watched Thénardier. He watched how the innkeeper handled money and how he talked about money always with an eye to what he could get, never with satisfaction about what he had. He was the kind of man who saw every situation as a potential transaction. He was the kind of man who could be bargained with, if you approached it correctly.

On the third evening, after dinner, when the inn was quieter and most of the guests had gone to their rooms, Valjean came to sit at Thénardier's table.

"I want to talk to you about the child," he said.

Thénardier looked at him with careful eyes. "Which child?" he said. Though he knew perfectly well which child.

"The one called Cosette," said Valjean. "I understand she is in your care."

"She is like a daughter to us," said Thénardier immediately. He said it with an expression of warm feeling that was quite convincing. He had practised it.

"I want to take her," said Valjean.

Thénardier's expression did not change, but something behind his eyes sharpened. "Take her?" he said. "What do you mean?"

"Her mother is dead," said Valjean. He did not say how he knew this. "I am a friend of the family. I want to take Cosette and care for her. You will be compensated, of course, for your costs and for the loss of her company."

Thénardier was quiet for a moment. He looked at Valjean with the assessing gaze of a man calculating how much something is worth. He thought about the girl the work she did around the inn, the money that had come every month from her mother until recently. He thought about this quiet man who had paid for a week in advance and bought an expensive doll without negotiating the price.

"She is like a daughter," he said again. "We have raised her. We love her as our own. It would be very difficult "

"Name your price," said Valjean.

## Part Twelve The Negotiation

What followed was the kind of conversation that Thénardier enjoyed and that Valjean had very little patience for but understood was necessary.

Thénardier named a price. It was very high much higher than was reasonable. He spoke at length about how much he and his wife had done for Cosette. He mentioned the doctor's bills. He mentioned the cost of clothing. He mentioned the cost of food and care and education though in truth Cosette had received very little education at the inn, mostly whatever she had picked up from listening to the lessons that the village schoolmaster gave to the Thénardier daughters in the evenings.

Valjean listened to all of this without expression.

He negotiated. He brought the number down. Thénardier resisted. Valjean moved the number again. Thénardier said that he also needed a letter some kind of document, he said, to protect himself. A letter from someone with authority, saying that the child was being taken with permission.

Valjean thought about this. He said: I can give you a letter from the child's mother. The mother is dead, but the letter was written before her death, authorizing the transfer of the child to my care.

This was not exactly true. But Valjean had, in his bag, some letters from Fantine letters she had written to him when he was mayor, asking about Cosette, explaining the situation. He could use these, carefully. They were real documents, in Fantine's real handwriting.

Thénardier read the letter that Valjean showed him. He could not read very fluently, but he read enough to understand that it was real. He looked at the handwriting. He looked at Valjean.

He asked one more time for a higher price. Valjean paid it.

The negotiation was over. They shook hands. Thénardier's handshake was firm and professional, the handshake of a man who has just made a transaction he is satisfied with.

Valjean went upstairs to his room. He packed his bag. He put the bishop's candlesticks carefully at the bottom, wrapped in cloth.

He went to the kitchen.

---

## Part Thirteen Leaving

Cosette was in the kitchen, washing the last of the evening's pots. The kitchen was warm and smelled of soap and wood smoke. She was working carefully and quickly, the way she always worked.

She saw Valjean come in. She looked at him with that direct, watchful gaze of hers.

"Cosette," he said. "Come with me."

She looked at him. "Where?" she said.

"To a new place," he said. "A better place."

She put down the pot she was holding. She dried her hands on her apron. She looked at him for a long moment.

"Is it far?" she said.

"A little far," he said. "We will walk tonight and then take a carriage in the morning."

She looked at her apron. She looked at her wooden shoes. She looked at the kitchen around her the pots and the fire and the floor she had scrubbed hundreds of times.

"Can I bring Jacqueline?" she said.

"Of course," he said.

She went to the shelf and took down the new doll. She held it against her chest. She looked at Valjean.

"I am ready," she said.

They went through the kitchen door and into the hall. At the end of the hall, Madame Thénardier was standing. She looked at Cosette with her usual expression not warm, not cold, just indifferent. Cosette looked back at her. Neither of them said anything.

They went out through the front door.

---

The village was quiet. Most of the houses were dark. The square was empty, the fair stalls still closed. A dog barked somewhere, once, and then was silent.

They walked down the main street of Montfermeil. Cosette walked beside Valjean, one hand holding the doll and one hand free at her side. Her wooden shoes made a small sound on the cold cobblestones.

At the edge of the village, the road continued south into the forest. It was a proper road, wide enough for a carriage, but at night it felt like it was going into very deep darkness. The trees on either side were tall and leafless and their branches made complicated shapes against the sky, which was clear now and full of stars.

Cosette stopped at the edge of the forest. She looked at the dark road ahead.

Valjean stopped beside her.

"Are you afraid of the dark?" he said.

She thought about this honestly. "A little," she said.

"So am I," he said. "But we will walk together."

She looked up at him. He held out his hand.

She looked at his hand for a moment. Then she put her small hand in it. His hand was very large and very warm. She held it the way she held the doll carefully, with full attention, with the specific grip of someone who has learned that good things can be taken away and does not intend to let go.

They walked into the forest together.

---

## Part Fourteen The Forest Road

The road through the forest was long. It went downhill for a while and then uphill and then flat for a long stretch. There was no one else on it. The snow was on the ground here too, thin and white in the starlight, and their footsteps made small sounds in it.

Cosette walked beside Valjean and held his hand and said nothing for a long time. She was not a chatterbox. She had never had anyone to chatter to, really. She had learned to be quiet, and quiet had become her natural state.

But she was not anxious. She was not frightened. She did not ask where they were going or how long it would take or what the place would be like. She walked and held his hand and looked at the road ahead of her.

After a while, she spoke.

"Monsieur," she said.

"Yes," he said.

"What is your name?"

He thought for a moment. He needed a name. A name that was not Madeleine and not Valjean. A name that he could carry forward into the new life he was building.

"Fauchelevent," he said. He thought of the old man the cart accident, the man he had lifted. He did not know exactly why that name came to him in that moment. Perhaps because it was the name of someone he had genuinely helped. Perhaps because it felt like a connection to a better version of himself.

"Monsieur Fauchelevent," said Cosette. She tried the name carefully, like a new piece of clothing.

"Yes," he said.

They walked on.

The forest thinned after a while. The road came out into open country again flat fields and the distant lights of a farmhouse. The air was colder without the trees. Cosette pulled the doll more tightly against her with her free arm.

"Are you cold?" said Valjean.

"A little," she said.

He stopped. He put down his bag. He took out the travelling coat that was packed at the top of the bag. It was a large coat, much too large for her. But he wrapped it around her shoulders anyway, and she disappeared into it almost completely, only her face and the doll's face visible above the collar.

She looked up at him from inside the coat.

"Thank you," she said. The same serious, quiet thank you. The most genuine one he had heard in a long time.

They continued walking.

After another hour, they found a farmhouse that had a light on. Valjean knocked on the door. The farmer answered a large, sleepy man in his nightshirt, suspicious of strangers at this hour. But Valjean explained that he was travelling with his granddaughter, that the child was cold and tired, and that he would pay well for a warm corner and a cup of something hot.

The farmer looked at Cosette. She looked back at him from inside the enormous coat, holding her doll, with her large dark eyes and her serious face.

The farmer stepped back and let them in.

His wife heated milk on the fire. Cosette drank it slowly, sitting on a stool near the kitchen fire, with the doll on her lap. Valjean sat across from her and watched her. The firelight was warm and orange on her face. She was not falling asleep, though she was tired. She was looking at the fire with the concentrated attention of someone watching something interesting.

"What are you thinking about?" he said.

She looked at him. She thought about the question seriously, the way she thought about all questions.

"I am thinking that this is a warm kitchen," she said. "And that the milk is good."

He smiled. He could not help it. It was not a small or polite smile. It was a real one the kind that happens before you have time to decide whether to smile or not.

Cosette saw it. She looked at his face for a moment with curiosity. She had not seen many real smiles directed at her.

Then she looked back at the fire.

"Where will we sleep?" she said.

"The farmer will find us somewhere," said Valjean. "A warm place."

"Good," she said. She stroked the doll's hair once, very gently, with one finger. "Jacqueline is also tired."

"Then she should sleep too," he said.

"Yes," said Cosette. She settled the doll more comfortably in her lap. She looked at the fire. "She has never slept anywhere warm before," she said.

Valjean was quiet.

"Neither have I," said Cosette. She said it without self-pity, as a simple fact. The way she said everything.

---

## Part Fifteen The Morning After

They left the farmhouse early. The farmer's wife had made porridge for them hot and thick and sweet with a small spoon of honey on top. Cosette ate it with the same concentration she brought to all food, not hurrying but not leaving a single speck in the bowl.

The farmer had arranged a cart for them. He knew a man in the next village who was travelling to Paris that day and would take passengers for a small fee. Valjean thanked him and paid him generously. The farmer's wife gave Cosette a small piece of Christmas cake wrapped in cloth, for the journey.

Cosette held the cake in one hand and Jacqueline in the other arm as they climbed into the cart. The man driving the cart was a friendly, quiet farmer who was curious but polite enough not to ask many questions. He just drove his horse through the morning countryside and let his passengers be.

Cosette sat beside Valjean in the back of the cart and looked at the fields and the road and the sky, which was grey but not dark the grey of a winter morning that might become slightly brighter by midday.

After a while, she said: "Monsieur Fauchelevent?"

"Yes," he said.

"Are we going to live in Paris?"

"For a little while," he said. "We are going to find a place to stay."

"And after that?"

"We will see," he said.

She thought about this. "I have never been to Paris," she said.

"You have," he said. "When you were very young. But you don't remember it."

She thought about this too. She tried to remember Paris and could not. She had no clear memories from before the inn.

"Were you a friend of my mother's?" she said.

He did not answer immediately. He looked at the road ahead.

"Yes," he said, at last. "I knew her."

Cosette was quiet. She looked at the field passing beside the road. "She is not coming," she said. It was not a question. She had known this for a while, in the specific way that children know things they have not been told by the silence around a subject, by the absence of information where information should be.

"No," said Valjean. "She is not coming."

Cosette said nothing for a moment. She held Jacqueline a little tighter.

"Was she good?" she said.

He looked at her. Her profile was turned away from him, looking at the field. Her chin was up. She was not going to cry. She was asking a real question, the way she asked all real questions steadily, prepared for a real answer.

"Yes," he said. "She was very good. She worked very hard and she loved you completely. Everything she did, she did for you."

Cosette was quiet.

"She wanted you to be happy," he said. "That was the most important thing to her."

Cosette nodded, very slightly. She looked at the field for a while longer.

Then she looked down at Jacqueline and straightened the doll's bonnet.

"We are going to be all right," she said. She said it in a quiet, practical tone. She might have been talking to the doll. She might have been talking to herself. She might have been talking to him.

He looked at her small serious face and the doll in her arms and the Christmas cake still in her other hand, and he thought: yes. Yes. We are going to be all right.

He did not say it out loud. He was not yet the kind of man who said such things easily. But he thought it, and it was true, and that was enough for now.

---

The cart rolled on toward Paris. The grey morning brightened slightly, the way winter mornings sometimes do not to full sunshine, but to a pale, clear light that made everything look clean and definite. The fields on either side of the road. The bare trees. The distant farmhouses with their thin columns of smoke.

Cosette ate her piece of Christmas cake. She gave a small piece of it to Jacqueline, which involved holding it against the porcelain mouth for a moment with great seriousness. Valjean watched this from the corner of his eye and said nothing.

She offered him a piece.

"Thank you," he said. He took it.

She nodded. She settled back against the side of the cart. The movement of the cart was steady and comfortable. The horse's hooves made a regular sound on the road.

After a while, Cosette fell asleep. She leaned against Valjean's arm without quite knowing she was doing it, the way tired children lean against whoever is beside them. Her head tipped sideways. The doll was still clutched to her chest.

Valjean sat very still. He did not move his arm. He looked at the road ahead.

He thought about the bishop. He thought about everything that had happened since that morning in Digne, when he had walked away with silver in his bag and rage in his chest. He thought about all the different lives he had lived since then the wanderer, the factory man, the mayor, the fugitive, the man in the courtroom. He thought about Fantine. He thought about the promise.

The promise had been kept. Imperfectly, too late, in a way Fantine could not know about. But kept.

He looked down at the child sleeping against his arm. Eight years old. Thin and small and carrying, underneath her seriousness and her caution, something that had not been destroyed by the years at the Thénardier inn. Something that was still intact. Something that had survived.

He did not know what to call it. Resilience, perhaps. Or simply: herself.

She was still herself.

He would make sure she had the space to continue being herself. Whatever it cost. Whatever it took.

He looked at the road ahead. Paris was not far now. They would find somewhere to stay. He would think about the next step. He had always been good at the next step.

Beside him, Cosette breathed the slow, even breaths of a child who is warm and safe and finally, for the first time in a long time, has nothing to worry about.

The cart rolled on.
