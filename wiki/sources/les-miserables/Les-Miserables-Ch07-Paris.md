# Les Misérables
## Chapter 7 Paris
### Extended A2/B1 Reader | ~10,000 words

---

*Before this chapter: Jean Valjean went to the village of Montfermeil on Christmas Eve. He found Cosette thin, small, working hard, carrying a bucket of water in the dark. He gave her a doll. He negotiated with Thénardier. He paid a high price and took Cosette away from the inn. They walked through the forest together, hand in hand. A farmer's wife gave them warm milk. They slept in a dry barn. In the morning, they got on a cart going to Paris.*

*Now they are arriving in the city. They have each other. They have the bishop's silver candlesticks. They have some money. They have no plan beyond: find somewhere safe and be quiet.*

---

## Part One The City

Paris in those years was a city of contrasts.

In the west, there were wide, clean boulevards and tall white buildings and shops with expensive things in their windows. There were parks where rich people walked their dogs and mothers pushed baby carriages and old gentlemen sat on benches and read newspapers. There were cafés with marble tables and good coffee. There were theatres and concert halls and restaurants that served food on white tablecloths.

But this was not the whole city.

In the east and in the centre, there were other streets. Narrow streets, where the buildings were so close together that very little light reached the pavement. Streets where the buildings were old and leaning and sometimes falling apart. Streets where twenty or thirty families lived in a building that had been designed for five. Streets where the smell of poverty was always present a combination of damp and old cooking and too many people in too small a space.

This was the part of Paris where Jean Valjean brought Cosette.

He brought her there not because he wanted to live in poverty he had money, and he could have chosen a more comfortable neighbourhood. He brought her there because it was the part of the city where strangers were not noticed. In the narrow streets of the poor quarters of Paris, a man and a small girl could arrive one day and simply be there, and nobody would ask who they were or where they had come from. The neighbours had their own difficulties to think about. A new face was just a new face.

This was what he needed. Invisibility. Quiet. A place to disappear.

## Part Two The Gorbeau House

The building where they found a room was called, by the people who lived near it, the Gorbeau house. Nobody quite knew why. There had perhaps once been a man called Gorbeau who owned it, or perhaps not. The name had stuck, the way names stick to old buildings, for no reason that anyone remembered.

It was a large, old building at the end of a quiet street in the eleventh district. From the outside, it looked as if it had been respectable once long ago, when the street was newer and the neighbourhood was not so poor. The facade had mouldings and a decorative arch above the main door, both of which were now crumbling. The door itself was large and wooden and had a heavy iron handle that had turned green with age.

Inside, the building was divided into many small apartments some on the ground floor, some on the first and second floors, some in the attic. Each apartment was slightly different in shape, because the building had been modified and subdivided so many times over the years that the interior made very little architectural sense. There were small staircases that led to unexpected landings. There were doors that opened into walls. There were windows that looked not out to the street but into the building itself.

The landlady was a small woman of about sixty. Her name was Madame Burgon, though she had had several names over the years she had been married twice and had also used her first husband's name and her second husband's name and a third name that belonged to neither of them, for reasons she did not explain. She had a pointed face and quick eyes and a talent for knowing other people's business. But she was not essentially cruel, and she charged a fair rent for such a neighbourhood.

Valjean paid her two months in advance, as was his habit. He told her his name was Fauchelevent. He said the child was his granddaughter. He said he was a retired worker and that they lived quietly. He said all of this simply and without elaboration, and Madame Burgon, who recognised a private person when she saw one, asked no further questions.

She showed them the apartment. It was on the first floor, facing the courtyard rather than the street. It had two rooms a main room with a fireplace, a table, and two chairs, and a smaller room to one side with a bed in it. The walls were bare plaster. The floors were wooden and uneven. There were two small windows, both with glass in them, which was a good sign.

It was not comfortable. But it was clean. And it was theirs.

When Madame Burgon left and closed the door behind her, Valjean set his bag down on the table. He looked at the two rooms. He looked at Cosette, who was standing in the middle of the main room holding Jacqueline and looking around her with those large, attentive dark eyes.

"What do you think?" he said.

Cosette looked at the fireplace. She looked at the two windows. She looked at the smaller room.

"Is that my room?" she said.

"Yes," he said.

She walked to the door of the smaller room and looked in. The bed had a straw mattress and a blanket. It was not a grand bed. But it was hers.

"It is good," she said seriously.

## Part Three The First Days

The first thing Valjean did was make the apartment warmer.

He went out early the next morning and bought wood for the fireplace, and coal to keep the fire going through the night. He bought two more blankets. He bought a pillow not a grand one, but a proper pillow, the kind you can actually sleep on, not the hard flat thing that had been on the bed when they arrived.

He bought food. He bought bread and cheese and soup vegetables and a small piece of smoked meat. He bought milk a small jug of it, which Cosette drank with both hands around the cup, as if she was afraid it might move.

He bought Cosette clothes. He found a small shop on the main street of the neighbourhood that sold second-hand children's clothing. He described Cosette's size to the woman behind the counter thin, small for her age, eight years old. The woman brought out dresses, and Valjean looked at them carefully and chose the ones that were warmest and most practical. He bought a coat, dark blue, slightly too large but warm. He bought two pairs of wool stockings. He bought shoes real shoes, leather shoes, with laces, that actually fit.

He brought these things back to the apartment. He laid them out on the bed in the small room.

Cosette stood in the doorway and looked at the clothes. She did not rush forward. She looked at them carefully, from where she stood.

"These are for me?" she said.

"Yes," he said.

She walked to the bed. She touched the coat first stroked the fabric with one hand. Then she picked up one of the shoes. She looked at it. She put it down.

"They are good," she said.

She said this about many things in those first weeks. Food: good. The fire: good. The bed: good. She was not, Valjean noticed, ungrateful. It was something else. She was assessing things checking them against her experience, finding them satisfactory. She had not yet learned to be surprised by kindness, because for her it was all still new and she was not yet sure it would continue.

He understood this. He did not push her to be more expressive. He just made sure that the fire kept burning and the food kept coming and the shoes stayed on her feet.

## Part Four School

After two weeks, Valjean enrolled Cosette in school.

There was a small school for girls three streets away, run by two elderly sisters who had been teachers all their lives. It cost a small fee each month, which Valjean paid. The sisters were strict but not unkind. They taught reading, writing, numbers, and a little history. They expected the girls to sit quietly and pay attention and do their homework.

On the first morning, Valjean walked Cosette to the school. He brought her to the door and knocked. One of the sisters opened it. She looked at Cosette.

"This is the new girl?" she said.

"Yes," said Valjean. "Her name is Cosette Fauchelevent."

The sister looked at Cosette's clean dress and her leather shoes and her neatly combed hair. She looked at the child's face serious, watchful, meeting her gaze directly.

"Can you read?" said the sister.

"A little," said Cosette. She had learned some letters from listening to the Thénardier girls' lessons in the evenings, but she had never been formally taught.

"Can you write your name?"

"No," said Cosette.

"Can you count to twenty?"

"Yes," said Cosette. She counted: one, two, three all the way to twenty, without stopping and without mistakes.

The sister nodded. "She can come in," she said to Valjean.

Cosette looked at Valjean. He nodded.

She followed the sister through the door.

Valjean stood outside the school for a moment after the door closed. He looked at the door. He turned and walked back down the street.

He came back at midday to collect her. She came out of the school with two other girls she had apparently been talking to, and she was slightly different from how she had been in the morning not dramatically different, but slightly. Her posture was easier. Her face was less careful.

"How was it?" he said.

"There is a girl called Marie," she said. "She knew all the letters already. I only knew some of them. But the sister said that by the end of the week I will know them all."

"And will you?" he said.

Cosette thought about this seriously. "Yes," she said.

She was right. By the end of the week, she knew them all. By the end of the month, she could read simple sentences. By the end of two months, she was the fastest reader in her class.

## Part Five Valjean's Days

While Cosette was at school, Valjean was alone.

He was used to being alone. He had spent much of his life alone in the sense of being without close company, without family, without people who knew him well. Even in the busy years in Montreuil-sur-Mer, he had been fundamentally alone. Madeleine had been a public figure, but Valjean had always been private.

Here in Paris, he was completely private.

He did not go out much during the day. He had no work to go to he had enough money to live on for a long time, if they were careful. He spent the mornings in the apartment. He read. He kept the apartment tidy. He prepared food for the midday meal and for the evening. He had discovered that he liked to cook not grand meals, but simple, careful ones. Good soup. Good bread. A piece of good cheese. Simple things, done well.

He went out in the afternoons, when the streets were busy and a single man walking was less noticeable than he would be in the quiet of the morning. He walked through the neighbourhood, learning the streets. He identified the exits from the area the routes that led to other parts of the city, the streets that connected to the main roads, the shortcuts through courtyards and passages that only someone who had walked them many times would know.

He did this carefully and methodically, the way he had done everything practical in his life. He was not preparing for anything specific. He was being a man who had lived in danger for many years, and who knew that knowing your surroundings is not paranoia it is simply common sense.

He came home at the end of the afternoon. He made the fire brighter. He put the pot on.

At four o'clock, Cosette came home from school.

This was, he found, the part of the day he looked forward to most.

She came through the door and put her bag on the chair and sat down at the table. She always had things to report. The lesson on history today. The girl called Marie who had said something funny. The sister who had been strict about the handwriting. A problem in mathematics that she had solved correctly. A problem that she had not solved correctly and wanted to think about more.

She talked and he listened. He asked questions. She answered them and often asked him questions back. He was not accustomed to this to being asked questions by someone who genuinely wanted to know his opinion. He answered carefully and honestly. She listened with the same complete attention she brought to everything.

After dinner, she did her homework at the table while he read by the fire. The apartment was quiet except for the scratch of her pen and the occasional sound of the fire settling.

He lit the two silver candlesticks in the evening. He always lit them. He had done this every night since the bishop gave them to him, even when he had been on the road and had to make do with a flat stone as a surface. The candles were not expensive, but the candlesticks were, and he treated them with care. He set them on the shelf above the fireplace where they gave a warm, steady light.

Cosette had noticed them from the beginning.

"Those are beautiful," she had said, on the first evening.

"They were a gift," he said.

"From who?"

"From a good man," he said. "A man who changed my life."

She had looked at them for a moment longer and then gone back to her dinner.

But after that, she sometimes looked at them in the evenings when the candlelight was warm on the wall. He could see her looking. He said nothing. The candlesticks did not need explanation. They explained themselves, in the way that things do when they carry a real meaning.

## Part Six Spring

Winter ended slowly, as winters in Paris always do reluctantly, in small stages, with several false starts where it was suddenly warm for a day and then cold again for a week.

But eventually, spring came.

Cosette grew. Not dramatically she was still small, still slight but her face filled out a little and her arms became a little less thin and she moved with less caution, as if her body was beginning to trust that there would be enough food again tomorrow.

She had friends at school now. Not many she was not a very social child, in the sense of seeking out large groups of people but two or three particular friendships that were real. She brought home occasional information about these friends: what their families were like, what they were good at in school, what they thought about things.

She began to laugh more. This was the thing Valjean noticed most. At the Thénardier inn, Cosette had almost never laughed. She had been too careful for laughter, too watchful. But now, sometimes, something would happen something Marie said, or a problem with the homework that she had solved in a ridiculous way, or a cat that got into the courtyard and stared at them both with enormous dignity before being chased out by the landlady and Cosette would laugh. Genuinely, suddenly, surprised by it herself.

He noticed this each time. He did not comment on it. He just kept a note of it, in the part of his mind that was keeping track of how she was growing.

He was not her father. He had never pretended to be her father he had said he was her grandfather, for the purposes of the landlady and the school, and this was fine with Cosette, who did not press the question of who he was exactly. She called him Monsieur Fauchelevent to begin with, which was correct and formal. Then she began to shorten it just Monsieur. Then, one afternoon, without any particular occasion, she called him Grand-père.

Grandfather.

He had not prepared himself for this. He was standing at the stove with a wooden spoon in his hand and she said it Grand-père, can you help me with this problem? and he was so surprised that he stood still for a moment before he could answer.

He helped her with the problem.

That evening, after she had gone to bed, he sat by the fire for a long time.

He thought about what it meant to be responsible for a child. He had known it in a practical sense the food, the school, the clothes, the warmth. But Grand-père was something different. Grand-père meant that in her mind, in whatever way she understood it, he was family. He was the person who was there. He was the person she came home to.

He looked at the candlesticks on the shelf.

He had promised Fantine. He had not been able to bring Cosette to her mother. But he had brought something to Cosette safety, warmth, education, someone who came at four o'clock to listen. Whether this was enough, he did not know. He hoped it was moving in the right direction.

He hoped.

## Part Seven The Neighbours

The Gorbeau house had other tenants, and over the months Valjean became aware of them without becoming acquainted with them.

There was an old man on the ground floor called Monsieur Mabeuf. He was a church warden by profession and had an enormous collection of old books that filled every surface of his apartment. He was a gentle, absent-minded man who was clearly not very well off. He sometimes met Valjean on the stairs and said good morning with the polite brevity of someone who preferred books to people but had not forgotten his manners. Valjean liked him, in the quiet way that one can like a person one barely knows.

There was a family on the second floor whose name Valjean did not initially know. A man and a woman and two daughters and, eventually, a son. They were very poor. The man was loud and the woman was tired and the children were sometimes hungry, which Cosette noticed because she herself had been a hungry child and had a particular sensitivity to the signs of it. She sometimes left a small parcel of food on their landing bread, a piece of cheese, nothing dramatic and did not mention it to anyone.

Valjean noticed this and said nothing. He was not sure when she had started doing it. He added a little more to the weekly shopping.

There were other tenants he barely saw a seamstress who worked at night and slept during the day, a young man who was apparently a student and who came and went at odd hours, an elderly woman who never seemed to leave her apartment at all.

They were all living their lives in close proximity to each other, separated by thin walls, and carefully, mutually ignoring each other. This was how Valjean preferred it.

But the family on the second floor worried him, in a small, background way. The man had a voice that carried through the walls not always angry, but always loud, always slightly wrong. There was something about him that Valjean could not identify precisely. A quality of performance, perhaps. A sense that the loudness and the obvious poverty were slightly more deliberate than they appeared.

He said nothing. He kept his door closed. He reminded himself that not everything that made him uneasy was actually dangerous.

He tried to believe this.

## Part Eight The Luxembourg Garden

One of Valjean's habits, in the better weather, was to take Cosette to the Luxembourg Garden on Sunday afternoons.

The Luxembourg Garden was a large park in the sixth district, on the left bank of the Seine. It had a central pond where children sailed toy boats, and long gravel paths between rows of trees, and stone statues, and benches where people sat to read or talk. It was a democratic sort of park meaning that rich and poor and everyone in between used it, which made it one of the few places in Paris where very different kinds of people occupied the same space without too much friction.

On Sunday afternoons in spring and summer, the garden was particularly pleasant. The trees were full. The paths were warm with sunlight. There were always children running and pigeons walking with their particular self-important stride and old people sitting on the benches with newspapers.

Valjean and Cosette came every Sunday.

Cosette liked the pond. She liked to watch the toy boats sail across it the serious ones that belonged to children who had received them as gifts and handled them with care, and the improvised ones that were made from sticks and leaves and sailed badly but with great enthusiasm. She had been given a small boat once, by one of the school friends, and she had brought it to the garden and sailed it for several Sunday afternoons before it got waterlogged and sank.

She had not been particularly upset about this.

"It had a good life," she said.

"It sailed well," Valjean agreed.

"For a stick," she said.

They sat on their usual bench it was not formally their bench, of course, but they had sat on it so many Sundays that it had become theirs in the informal way that these things happen and watched the garden. Cosette sometimes did homework. Valjean sometimes read. Sometimes they just sat.

It was on these Sunday afternoons in the garden that Valjean was most conscious of what they had built together. Not happiness exactly he was too old and had seen too much to call it happiness without qualification. But something very close to it. A life. A quiet, ordered, genuine life.

He watched Cosette watching the boats on the pond and thought: this is what Fantine wanted. Not this specific garden, not this specific Sunday, but this a child safe and warm and occupied with small joys. This is what the money was for. This is what the years in the factory were for. This is what the promise was for.

The thought gave him, each time, a particular kind of peace.

## Part Nine The Young Man

It was in the Luxembourg Garden that Valjean first noticed the young man.

He was perhaps nineteen or twenty years old. He was thin, with dark hair and a serious face and the kind of clothes that are well made but have been worn for a long time not poor exactly, but careful. He was often in the garden on the same Sunday afternoons as Valjean and Cosette. He sat on a bench nearby with a book, but he did not always read it.

Valjean noticed him because the young man sometimes looked at Cosette.

Not in a way that was frightening or inappropriate. In a way that was simply obvious. He looked at her the way young people look at someone who has caught their attention. Quickly, then away. Then again. The kind of looking that the person doing it does not quite realise they are doing.

Cosette was fourteen now. She had grown into a quiet, serious, studious young woman with her mother's dark eyes and something of her mother's particular quality of grace that quality of being noticed without trying to be noticed. She was not vain. She paid very little attention to her own appearance. She wore her dark hair simply, without ornamentation, and she dressed practically.

But she was lovely. Valjean could see this objectively, as a fact about her, separate from his own feelings about her. She was lovely, and the young man in the garden could see it too.

The young man came back the next Sunday. And the Sunday after that.

Valjean began to pay attention.

He watched how the young man moved how he arranged himself on the bench, how he held his book, the angle at which he sat so that Cosette was visible in his peripheral vision. Young people in love are not subtle. They think they are subtle. They are not.

Valjean said nothing to Cosette.

He watched. He thought. He filed the information away.

One Sunday, he noticed that Cosette had noticed the young man.

He saw it in a small change in the way she was sitting a slight straightening, a slight extra attention to the world immediately around her that had not been there the week before. She did not look at the young man. She was too careful for that. But she knew he was there. Her awareness of his presence was visible in her posture, in the specific way she was not looking at him.

Valjean sat beside her on the bench and felt, for the first time in a long time, something uncomfortable. Something that was not quite fear but was related to it. A premonition.

He did not know the young man. He did not know his name or his character or his intentions. He knew only that Cosette was fourteen and had spent the last six years in a quiet, protected world that he had built very carefully, and that this world was about to become more complicated.

They went home that afternoon in their usual comfortable silence. Cosette did her homework. Valjean cooked dinner. The candlesticks burned on the shelf.

Nothing had happened. And yet everything was slightly different.

## Part Ten Javert

One evening in late autumn, Valjean was walking home through the neighbourhood after buying bread.

He turned a corner and stopped.

At the end of the street, standing under a lamp post, talking to a man Valjean did not recognise, was Inspector Javert.

Valjean's body knew before his mind did. He stepped back around the corner. He pressed himself against the wall. He was not thinking. He was reacting the deep, animal reaction of someone who has been hunted for most of their life and whose instincts are faster than their reasoning.

He stood against the wall and breathed.

He told himself: look again. Make sure. Do not react to a mistake.

He leaned very slightly around the corner and looked.

It was Javert. He was certain. He had known Javert's posture and Javert's coat and Javert's particular way of standing upright, still, like a man who is always slightly on duty for too many years to mistake them now.

Javert was not looking in his direction. He was talking to the man beside him a local shopkeeper, it appeared, gesturing at a shop window. This was not, Valjean recognised, the behaviour of a man who had found who he was looking for. This was routine. An inspector doing his rounds. Gathering information about a neighbourhood.

But Javert being in this neighbourhood was not a coincidence.

Valjean walked home by a different route. He walked quickly without appearing to hurry. He let himself into the building, went up to the apartment, and closed the door very quietly behind him.

Cosette was at the table, writing.

She looked up. "You were a long time," she said.

"I took a different way home," he said. He put the bread on the table. He sat down. He made his face and his voice normal calm, unhurried, ordinary.

"Are you all right?" she said. She was perceptive. She had always been perceptive.

"Yes," he said. "Are you hungry?"

She looked at him for a moment with those clear dark eyes. She was not entirely convinced. But she was also fourteen, and she understood by now that there were things about him that he did not explain, and she had learned somewhere between the inn and this apartment that some things are better held quietly.

"Yes," she said. "I am hungry."

He got up and made dinner. His hands were steady. He kept them steady on purpose.

After dinner, after Cosette had gone to bed, he sat by the dying fire for a very long time.

Javert was here. In this district. On these streets. He had found not Valjean himself, not yet, but the area. He was tightening the circle.

How had he found the district? Valjean thought about this carefully. The cart driver from Montfermeil he had brought them partway into Paris. Had he described his passengers? The landlady, Madame Burgon he had given her the name Fauchelevent, but she was a woman who knew things about her neighbourhood. Had she spoken to someone?

It did not matter now how Javert had found the district. What mattered was that he had found it. And Javert, once on a scent, did not stop.

He looked at the candlesticks. He thought about Cosette asleep in her room, in her real bed with her proper pillow and her warm blanket, with Jacqueline on the shelf beside her, which was where the doll had ended up watching over the room like a slightly severe guardian.

He would not let her go back to a life like the inn. He would not let her be taken, moved, disrupted. She had just started to be happy. She had just started to laugh.

He had to move them.

## Part Eleven The Night

He did not sleep.

He sat by the cold fireplace and planned. He thought about every option he could think of. He thought about other neighbourhoods but if Javert was looking in this district, moving to another part of Paris would only help temporarily. He thought about leaving Paris entirely but Paris was the best place to hide, precisely because it was so large and full of people.

He needed somewhere that was inside Paris but not accessible to Javert. Somewhere with its own rules. Somewhere where the police did not routinely enter.

He thought about this for a long time.

He thought about Fauchelevent. The old man from the cart accident at Montreuil-sur-Mer. He had saved the old man's life by lifting the cart. Later, when he and Cosette had come over the wall into the convent garden that had been four years ago, when they first came to Paris it had been Fauchelevent who recognised him and helped them. Fauchelevent now worked as a gardener at the Petit-Picpus convent, a religious house for nuns in the twelfth district.

The convent.

A convent was, by its nature, closed. It was a world apart from the regular city. The police did not go into a convent without serious reason. The nuns did not receive casual visitors. A man and a girl who lived inside the walls of a convent were, for all practical purposes, invisible to the city outside.

He had thought about this option before and set it aside, because it seemed extreme a convent was not a home, it was not a life, it was a hiding place, and he did not want Cosette to grow up in a hiding place.

But hiding was what they needed right now. Not forever. Just long enough for Javert to look elsewhere.

He made his decision just before dawn.

## Part Twelve The Chase

It happened the next evening.

Valjean had been watching from the apartment window during the day and had seen nothing. But at dusk, when he went out carefully, by a back route to check the street, he saw two men standing at the corner of the next street. They were not in uniform. But they had the particular quality of stillness that police officers have when they are watching something.

He went back inside. He went upstairs. He said nothing to Cosette.

He packed one bag. He packed it quickly and efficiently the things that mattered, nothing more. Clothes for Cosette. The money. The bishop's candlesticks, wrapped carefully. Some food for tonight.

He told Cosette: we need to go out. Bring your coat.

She looked at his face. She put on her coat. She did not ask why.

They went out through the building's back door, which opened onto a small alley. They moved through the alley quickly. Cosette kept pace with him without complaint she was quick and strong now, not the thin, cautious child she had been at the inn.

They moved through the back streets of the neighbourhood, going east. He had walked these streets many times. He knew which alleys connected to which courts. He knew which passages led through to the next street.

For a while, he thought they were clear.

Then he heard footsteps. Behind them, moving at the same pace.

He did not look back. He took Cosette's hand and walked faster.

The footsteps behind them walked faster.

They turned a corner. Then another. He was looking for something a specific street he had identified months ago as a potential route. He found it: a narrow passage between two buildings, so narrow you had to turn sideways to pass through it, that came out into a courtyard behind a row of houses.

They went through the passage.

On the other side, the courtyard was dark and empty. An old wall ran along the back of it high, perhaps four metres. Beyond the wall, Valjean knew, was a garden. Beyond the garden, a building.

He looked at the wall. He looked at Cosette. She was breathing hard from the fast walking but she was not frightened. She was watching him with clear, steady eyes.

"I am going to help you over this wall," he said.

She looked at the wall. She looked at him.

"All right," she said.

He found a rope there was one coiled on a hook near the courtyard wall, left there for some purpose he did not know. He tied it around Cosette's waist. He lifted her as high as he could, which was very high. She caught the top of the wall with both hands. She pulled herself up. She sat on top of the wall and looked down at him.

"Jump down on the other side," he said quietly. "There is garden. Land on the grass."

She disappeared over the wall. He heard a soft sound her feet landing. Then her voice: I'm all right.

He tied the rope around a stone and climbed the wall himself. He went over the top and dropped down into the garden.

It was dark. The garden was large larger than he had expected. There were trees and paths and the smell of winter grass. At the far end, visible through the trees, was the outline of a large building. Lights were on in some of the windows.

He took Cosette's hand again. They moved through the garden toward the building.

## Part Thirteen The Convent

The building was the Convent of the Petit-Picpus.

It was a religious house a place where nuns lived and prayed and kept themselves apart from the world outside. The garden they had come into was the convent's garden. The wall they had climbed was the convent's wall.

Valjean moved through the garden carefully, keeping to the shadows near the trees. He was looking for something. A specific door a small door at the side of the main building that led to the gardener's quarters.

He found it. He knocked. Softly one, two, three, pause, one, two. A pattern.

There was a silence. Then footsteps. Then the door opened.

An old man stood in the doorway. He was small and bent and his face was deeply lined. He was holding a lantern, and the light of it fell on Valjean's face.

The old man stared. His mouth opened slightly.

"Monsieur Madeleine," he whispered.

"Fauchelevent," said Valjean. "I need your help."

---

## Part Fourteen Fauchelevent

Fauchelevent was the gardener of the convent. He had been, once, the man whom Valjean had saved from under the fallen cart in Montreuil-sur-Mer. In the years after that, his leg had recovered enough for light work but not for heavy work, and Valjean when he was still Madeleine the mayor had arranged for him to take the position of gardener here, where the duties were manageable and the pay was enough to live on.

He had been here when Valjean and Cosette had first come over the wall, four years ago. He had hidden them then for a night, and then arrangements had been made complicated arrangements involving the community of nuns and the abbess who ran the convent and they had lived here for almost two years. Valjean had worked as Fauchelevent's assistant in the garden. Cosette had been a student at the small school that the nuns ran for the daughters of local families.

It had been safe. And it had been somewhat suffocating not the right word, but something close. A convent is not a prison, but it is enclosed. It has its own rhythms and its own rules, and the rules apply to everyone inside the walls, not just the nuns. Cosette had been patient about it. But she had been a child then.

She was not a child anymore.

Valjean had known, when they left the convent and moved to the Gorbeau house, that they were taking a risk. But he had also known that Cosette needed to see the world needed to go to a real school, make real friends, grow up in contact with ordinary life.

And she had. And it had been good.

But now Javert was in the neighbourhood.

Fauchelevent listened to all of this without interrupting. He was a simple man in many ways, but he was not a stupid one. He had a particular loyalty to Valjean that was the loyalty of someone whose life has been saved and who does not forget it.

"How long?" he said.

"I don't know," said Valjean. "Not forever. Long enough."

Fauchelevent thought. "The abbess," he said. "She would need to agree."

"I know."

"She is a fair woman," said Fauchelevent. "She has agreed to strange things before." He looked at Cosette, who was sitting on a wooden bench near the door, holding her bag and her doll she had brought Jacqueline, who was now six years old in doll-years and somewhat worn but still carefully maintained and looking at the stone floor with her usual composure.

"The child is good," said Fauchelevent. "I remember her from before. She caused no trouble."

"She never causes trouble," said Valjean.

Fauchelevent nodded. "I will speak to the abbess in the morning," he said. "Tonight you can stay in the garden shed. It is not comfortable but it is warm."

"It is enough," said Valjean.

They went to the garden shed. It was indeed not comfortable it was full of gardening tools and smelled of earth and old wood. But there was a small stove in the corner and Fauchelevent lit it, and after a few minutes the air was warmer.

Cosette sat down on a sack of seed and looked around the shed with her practical, assessing gaze.

"This is all right," she said.

"For one night," said Valjean.

"Yes," she said. "One night is not very long."

## Part Fifteen The Abbess

The abbess of the Convent of the Petit-Picpus was a woman of seventy. She had been in the convent for fifty years. She had entered as a young woman of twenty, and she had never left not once, not for a day in all that time.

She was not a soft woman. She was not unkind, but she was very clear about what she expected and what she would accept. She had rules, and her rules were the rules of her order, and the rules of her order were the rules of God, and she was very certain that she understood all three correctly.

She met with Valjean and Fauchelevent in the morning, in a small room with bare white walls and a wooden crucifix above the door.

She looked at Valjean with calm, dark eyes.

"Fauchelevent tells me you need sanctuary," she said.

"Yes," said Valjean.

"Why?"

He told her. He did not tell her his real name, or all the details of his history. But he told her the essential truth: he was a man who was being pursued, unjustly, by the police. He had a child in his care a child who had no one else. He needed a place where they could stay quietly, for a while, until the situation changed.

The abbess listened to all of this without expression.

"You are a man," she said, when he finished. "Men are not permitted to live in this convent. This is a rule of our order. It has not changed in two hundred years."

"I know," said Valjean. "I was here before four years ago. Fauchelevent can explain how it was arranged."

Fauchelevent explained. He had a way of explaining things slowly, with many small details, circling the point rather than going directly to it that somehow made the complicated seem simple.

The abbess listened.

"The child," she said. "She was a student here before?"

"Yes," said Valjean.

"She was a good student?"

"She was, Mother," said Fauchelevent. "Quiet, attentive, no difficulties."

The abbess was silent for a moment. She looked at the crucifix on the wall. She looked at Valjean.

"You say you are pursued unjustly," she said.

"I believe so," he said. "But I am not asking you to agree with my belief. I am asking for a place to stay. I will work in the garden, wherever I am useful. We will cause no difficulties."

She was quiet again. The room was very quiet.

"The child can return as a student," she said at last. "The school has a place. As for you  " she paused "Fauchelevent is getting old. He needs an assistant. A man who works in the garden and lives quietly in the gardener's quarters is not exactly living in the convent."

This was the arrangement that had worked before. Valjean understood.

"Thank you," he said.

She stood. The interview was over. At the door she stopped.

"This is a house of God," she said. "Whatever you have done and whatever has been done to you, that is between you and God. In this house, you are a gardener. That is all."

She left.

Fauchelevent exhaled. Valjean looked at the crucifix.

"She is a fair woman," said Fauchelevent.

"Yes," said Valjean. "She is."

## Part Sixteen Inside the Walls

And so they came to live in the convent for the second time.

Cosette resumed her studies. The nuns received her back without comment one of the sisters remembered her, and greeted her with a nod that was warm in its way, though not effusive. Cosette sat down in the classroom with a straight back and her notebook open and her pen ready, and the sister began the lesson, and that was that.

After school each day, Cosette spent some time in the garden. There was a particular corner near the old wall where a pear tree grew, and she liked to sit beneath it in the late afternoon when the light came through the branches. She brought her books there. She read. Sometimes she looked up at the branches and thought about things she did not describe to anyone.

Valjean worked in the garden beside Fauchelevent. He learned the garden its beds and paths and the particular needs of each section. He dug and planted and pruned and carried. The physical work was good for him. It was honest and simple and he could feel himself getting stronger, which was not something he had felt for several years.

In the evenings, they ate together in the gardener's quarters a simple room with a table and two chairs and the small stove. Cosette told him about her day. He told her about the garden. They sat together by the lamp he had brought the candlesticks, of course, and they sat on the table as they always had and read or talked or were quiet together, which was a thing they had learned to do very well.

Outside the walls, Paris continued. Javert continued. The city turned through its seasons. People lived and worked and suffered and hoped.

Inside the walls, it was quiet. It was safe. It was, for now, enough.

Cosette grew another centimetre. She finished a book she had been reading for two months and immediately asked for the next one. She learned a new word every day she kept a list of them in the back of her notebook. She wrote a letter, one afternoon, that she addressed to nobody and sealed and put in the box where she kept her most private things. Valjean did not ask what was in it.

He respected her privacy. He always had. It was one of the most important things he could give her the understanding that she was her own person, with her own inner life, and that he was not going to demand entry to it.

She was fifteen now. She had grown up in a way that was both gradual and, looking back, sudden. He could remember clearly the child in the grey dress with the bucket in the courtyard, and then he looked at the young person across the table from him with her book and her notebook and her careful handwriting, and the distance between those two things seemed enormous and also very small.

She looked up from her book.

"Grand-père," she said, "what are you thinking about?"

"You," he said. It was true, so he said it.

She looked at him for a moment. Then she looked back at her book.

"I am thinking about the next chapter," she said. "I think something bad is going to happen to the main character."

"Does something bad always happen?"

"In good books," she said, "something always happens. Otherwise it is not interesting."

He thought about this. "That is true," he said.

"Yes," she said. She turned a page. "I am going to keep reading."

He watched her read. The candlelight was warm on her face. The convent was quiet around them. Outside the walls, somewhere, the city moved through its night.

He thought: we are safe here. For now.

He thought: for now is enough.
