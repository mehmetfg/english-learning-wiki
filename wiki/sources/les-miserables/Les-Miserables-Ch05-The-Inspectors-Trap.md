# Les Misérables
## Chapter 5 The Inspector's Trap
### Extended A2/B1 Reader | ~10,000 words

---

*Before this chapter: Jean Valjean became Monsieur Madeleine, mayor of Montreuil-sur-Mer. He built a factory, helped many poor people, and tried to live honestly. Inspector Javert arrived in the town and watched him with suspicious eyes. Meanwhile, a young woman named Fantine lost everything her job, her health, her dignity. Madeleine found her on the street after Javert arrested her. He brought her to the hospital and promised to bring her daughter Cosette to her. But before he could keep that promise, Javert came to his office with shocking news: a man had been arrested in another town and was going to trial accused of being Jean Valjean.*

---

## Part One The Night Before

Madeleine sat at his desk for a long time after Javert left.

The room was quiet. Outside, the town was going to sleep. He could hear the clock on the church tower strike nine, then ten. The streets became quieter with each hour.

The two silver candlesticks were burning on the shelf. Their light was small and steady. Madeleine looked at them.

He thought about what Javert had told him.

A man had been arrested in the region of Auvergne, to the south. His name was Champmathieu. He was a poor man, a simple labourer with no fixed address. He had been caught stealing apples from an orchard. A small crime. The kind of crime that, in normal times, would result in a brief sentence and nothing more.

But then someone at the local prison had recognised him. A guard who had worked at Toulon many years before. The guard said: that man is not Champmathieu. That man is Jean Valjean. I know him. I saw him at Toulon.

More witnesses were found. Two other former convicts from Toulon, now serving new sentences. They were brought to look at the man called Champmathieu. They both confirmed it. Yes, that is Valjean. I recognise the face. I recognise the way he stands. That is the man.

The trial was tomorrow. In Arras. The judges, the witnesses, and the accused man were all gathered. The verdict seemed certain before the trial had even begun.

Champmathieu himself said he was not Jean Valjean. He said this repeatedly and with great frustration. He did not know who Jean Valjean was. He had never been to Toulon. He was Champmathieu. He had always been Champmathieu.

Nobody believed him. The witnesses were sure. The evidence seemed solid. The judge was prepared to condemn.

Madeleine looked at his candlesticks and thought about all of this.

An innocent man.

The words sat in his chest like a stone. An innocent man was going to prison tomorrow morning. An innocent man was going to lose years of his life perhaps the rest of his life because three men from Toulon thought they recognised a face. Because a guard had a good memory. Because the real Jean Valjean had the good fortune to be sitting in a comfortable office three hundred kilometres away, pretending to be someone he was not.

If Madeleine stayed silent, Champmathieu would be condemned. The man would spend years in prison for being Jean Valjean, while Jean Valjean sat in Montreuil-sur-Mer and continued to be the mayor.

And nobody would ever know.

He could continue. The factory would continue. The hospital he had expanded would continue. The school would continue. The poor people of Montreuil-sur-Mer would continue to have work and help and someone in power who actually cared about them. And Fantine he had promised Fantine. He had promised to bring Cosette. If he was arrested, who would keep that promise?

He could do more good as Madeleine than as Valjean. This was true. This was factually true. As Madeleine, he could go on helping people for years. As Valjean, he would be put in chains and taken to a prison from which he would probably never emerge.

He told himself all of these things. He turned them over. He arranged them in the most convincing order.

And still the stone sat in his chest: an innocent man.

He got up from his desk. He walked to the window and looked out at the dark street below. A cat crossed the street, moving silently along the edge of the buildings. A light burned in a window across the way someone reading late, or a sick child being watched. The ordinary details of an ordinary night in a small town.

He thought about the bishop. He thought about what the bishop had said: I have bought your soul. I give it to God.

What did it mean to have a soul that could be bought and given to God? He was not a religious man. He did not fully understand these ideas. But he understood the bishop's meaning, even if he could not explain it precisely. It meant: you are responsible for what you do with your life. You are responsible for choosing, each time, between the easy thing and the right thing.

He had made that choice once before. On the road after the bishop. He had made it with his whole self.

Could he make it again now?

He stood at the window for a long time. Then he went to his wardrobe. He took out his travelling coat. He put it on.

He went to his writing table and wrote two letters. One to his factory manager, with instructions for the next week's work. One to the hospital, asking them to continue caring for Fantine and assuring them that he would return soon and would explain everything.

He sealed the letters. He put them on the desk.

He picked up the two silver candlesticks. He wrapped them carefully in a cloth and put them in his travelling bag.

He went downstairs. He woke the coachman who lived in the small building beside the yard. He said: I need to travel to Arras tonight. Have the carriage ready in twenty minutes.

The coachman looked at him at his face, his coat, his bag and said nothing. He went to harness the horses.

Twenty minutes later, Madeleine climbed into the carriage.

The driver shook the reins. The horses began to move.

The carriage went through the dark streets of Montreuil-sur-Mer, past the factory, past the church, past the square where old Bernard swept the pavement every morning. It turned onto the main road going south and east.

Madeleine leaned back against the carriage seat. He looked out the small window at the darkness going past.

He did not know exactly what he would do when he got to Arras. He had not made a complete plan. He had only made the first decision: he was going. Everything else would come from that.

---

## Part Two The Carriage in the Night

The road to Arras was not good in those years. It was wide enough for a carriage, but the surface was uneven and full of holes. The carriage moved at the speed of the horses, which was not fast in the dark. They could not push the animals too hard when the road was difficult to see.

Madeleine did not sleep. He sat in the dark carriage and thought.

He was fifty-two years old. He had now been Monsieur Madeleine for more than eight years. Eight years. In that time, he had built a factory, employed hundreds of people, become mayor, expanded the hospital, supported the school, given away a large portion of everything he earned. He had worked every day. He had helped every person he could reach.

Was all of that going to end tomorrow?

Yes, he thought. Yes. If he walked into that courtroom and said what he needed to say, it would all end. Everything he had built. The factory might survive without him his manager was capable but it would be different. The mayor's position would go to someone else. The hospital, the school, all the projects he was managing they would either continue differently or not continue at all.

He thought about the workers in his factory. He thought about their faces the women who had come to him after their husbands died, the old men nobody else would hire, the young people starting out with nothing. They had jobs now. They had wages. They had dignity.

If he was taken to prison, would they keep their jobs? Probably yes the factory was established, it could run without him. But maybe some would not. And all the future people he could have helped the next Fantine, the next Fauchelevent they would have no one.

He pressed these thoughts down hard. He had already considered them. They were real, but they were not the point.

The point was simple and ugly and clear: a man who was not Jean Valjean was about to be condemned as Jean Valjean. And only one person in France could stop this. That person was sitting in a dark carriage on the road to Arras.

The carriage stopped twice during the night. The horses needed water. The driver needed to rest his eyes. Each time they stopped, Madeleine got out and stood in the dark road and breathed the cold night air. The fields around the road were flat and black. The stars above were very bright.

He thought about being in prison again. He thought about Toulon. The sounds of it metal on stone, the calls of the guards, the silence that was somehow louder than the noise. The smell of it. The loss of everything private, everything personal. The way years went by without any sense of purpose.

He was afraid. He was completely and sincerely afraid.

But fear, he had learned, was not a reason. Fear was just a feeling. It told you that something was dangerous or difficult. It did not tell you what to do about it.

He got back in the carriage. The driver shook the reins. They continued.

Dawn came slowly. The sky went from black to dark blue to a pale, cold grey. The flat fields around the road became visible brown and empty at this time of year, waiting for spring planting. A few farmhouses appeared, their windows dark.

The carriage moved on through the early morning.

---

## Part Three Arras

The city of Arras was small and old. It had a famous town square with arcaded buildings around it long stone arches, like a cloister. The buildings were elegant in a cold, formal way. The streets were clean and orderly.

Madeleine arrived in the late morning. He told the driver to stop near the centre of town. He got out. He paid the driver and told him to wait at the inn. He picked up his bag.

He found a hotel near the town hall. He took a room. He washed his face. He changed his coat. He stood at the window of the hotel room and looked down at the street below.

The Palace of Justice the courthouse was visible from where he stood. It was a large stone building with tall windows. Carriages were arriving and leaving. Men in formal black coats and lawyers' robes were going in and out. People of the town were gathered outside, as they always gather when something important is happening nearby.

Madeleine watched from his window.

He had not eaten since the previous evening. He was not hungry. His stomach felt tight and wrong.

He thought: I should go in. I should go now, before the trial begins in earnest. I should speak to someone the judge, or the prosecutor and explain the situation.

He picked up his hat. He put it down again.

He sat on the edge of the hotel bed.

The honest truth and he was trying to be honest with himself, completely and without comfort was that a part of him was hoping for something. He was hoping that when he walked into that courtroom and saw the man called Champmathieu, he would see a face that was so different from his own that the witnesses' identification was clearly wrong. He was hoping that someone else would notice this. He was hoping that the trial would collapse on its own and he would not need to do anything.

He knew this was cowardly. He knew it. He said it clearly in his own mind: this hope is cowardly.

But he was human. And even the most committed human being, on the morning of a great sacrifice, looks for a reason not to make it.

He stood up. He put on his hat.

He went downstairs and walked to the Palace of Justice.

---

## Part Four The Courtroom

The room was large and full. Every seat in the public gallery was taken. People stood along the walls. There was a particular atmosphere in the room tense and alert, the way rooms feel when something important is happening.

At the front of the room sat three judges in red robes. They sat behind a long raised table. Their faces were serious and composed.

To one side sat the prosecutor a man in black, with papers in front of him and a sharp, careful expression.

To the other side sat the defence lawyer. He was younger and less confident. He kept looking at his papers.

And in the middle, in the dock the place where accused people stand during a trial was Champmathieu.

Madeleine saw him immediately.

Champmathieu was a man of about sixty. He was short and thick-bodied. He had a rough, weathered face the face of a man who had worked outside all his life, in all conditions. His hair was grey and untidy. His clothes were poor but not ragged. His hands were large and still on the rail in front of him.

His expression was extraordinary. He looked confused and frustrated and tired, all at once. He looked like a man who has been saying the same thing for a long time and cannot understand why nobody is listening.

Madeleine looked at this man carefully.

He did not look like Jean Valjean. He did not look like the man Madeleine saw in the mirror each morning. He was shorter. His face was a different shape. His hands were different.

But then did Jean Valjean look like Jean Valjean, after eight years? People change. Time and circumstances change a face, a posture, a way of moving. The witnesses from Toulon had not seen Valjean in many years. Memory is not perfect. A face remembered from prison, seen now in a courtroom could it not be confused?

This was what Madeleine was thinking when one of the judges spoke, and the trial resumed.

---

## Part Five The Witnesses

The prosecutor stood and began his summary of the case.

He spoke clearly and efficiently. He had a good voice controlled and persuasive. He described the crime the stolen apples, the arrest. He described the process by which Champmathieu had been identified as Jean Valjean. He described the witnesses.

The first witness was called.

He was a small man of about fifty. He walked to the front of the room with the particular way of a man who has spent time in prison careful, controlled, always aware of where the exits are. He looked at the judges, not at Champmathieu.

The judge asked him to look at the man in the dock.

The witness turned. He looked at Champmathieu for a long moment.

"That is Jean Valjean," he said. His voice was flat and certain. "I knew him at Toulon. He was a strong man very strong. I saw him lift heavy loads that other men could not move. His face is older now, but I know that face."

The defence lawyer asked some questions. The witness was not disturbed. He repeated what he had said. He was certain.

Madeleine sat in the public gallery and watched.

The second witness was brought in. Another former convict. He too looked at Champmathieu.

"Yes," he said. "That is Valjean. He does not use that name now, but that is the man. I recognise the eyes."

The third witness was the prison guard from Auvergne the man who had first identified Champmathieu. He was calm and professional. He described the moment of recognition. He said it had been immediate. He said there was no doubt in his mind.

Throughout all of this, Champmathieu stood in the dock and shook his head slowly, back and forth, like a man watching a mistake being made that he has no power to correct.

"I am not Valjean," he said, when the judge gave him the opportunity to speak. "I am Champmathieu. I have always been Champmathieu. I do not know these men. I have never been to Toulon."

"Can you explain," said the judge, "why three separate witnesses all identify you as Jean Valjean?"

"No," said Champmathieu simply. "I cannot explain it. I only know that they are wrong."

The judge made a note. The prosecutor smiled slightly.

The defence lawyer tried. He argued that eyewitness memory is unreliable. He argued that the passage of time changes faces. He argued that the witnesses had a shared prejudice they had already been told they were looking for Valjean, and so they saw what they expected to see. He argued as well as he could.

It was not enough. He could see that it was not enough. The judge's face was settled and decided.

Madeleine sat in the gallery and watched this and understood: unless something changed, Champmathieu would be condemned today.

---

## Part Six The Fight Inside

The session paused for a short break. The judges left the room briefly. People in the gallery stood and stretched and talked quietly to each other.

Madeleine sat still.

His mind was moving very fast now. Not in circles, as it had been during the long carriage ride. In a very direct, very clear line.

He had come here. He had seen the trial. He had seen the witnesses. He had seen Champmathieu's face that face of honest, exhausted confusion. He had seen the defence lawyer's inability to stop what was happening.

Nothing had changed. There was no miracle. Nobody was going to realise the mistake without being told.

There was only him.

He thought: if I stand up and say what I know, I will be arrested before I leave this room. They will send for chains. They will take me away. Everything I am now the factory, the hospital, the work, the promise to Fantine all of it will end within the hour.

He thought: Fantine. He had promised Fantine. She was in the hospital, sick, waiting. She needed him to go to Montfermeil and bring Cosette back. If he was arrested here, today, Cosette would stay at the Thénardier inn. Fantine would wait and not understand why he had not come. She would wait until she was too sick to wait anymore.

He sat with this thought for a long time. It was the hardest part. The factory, the mayor's position, his own freedom these were large things, but they were his things. Fantine's situation was someone else's suffering. Someone who had done nothing wrong. Someone who had trusted him.

But Champmathieu had also done nothing wrong. And Champmathieu had nobody.

He thought: there are two people who will suffer if I stay silent. And one person who will suffer if I speak. And that one person is me.

This was the arithmetic of the decision. It was very simple. Simple arithmetic was sometimes the cruelest kind.

The judges came back. The session resumed.

The prosecutor made his closing argument. He spoke for twenty minutes. He was skilled and thorough. He tied together the threads of evidence the arrest, the identification, the witnesses into a picture that seemed complete and solid. He asked the judges to condemn Jean Valjean for breaking his parole, for returning to criminal activity, for living under a false identity.

He sat down.

The judge turned to Champmathieu. "Do you have anything final to say before we consider our verdict?"

Champmathieu stood up. He looked at the judges. He was a simple man and he did not have the words for a speech. He just said what was true.

"I am Champmathieu," he said. "I am telling you the truth. I do not know why those men say I am someone else. I am telling you: I am not. My name is Champmathieu. I have always been Champmathieu. That is all I can say."

He sat down.

The judge nodded. The judges began to confer quietly among themselves.

Madeleine stood up.

---

## Part Seven The Truth

He stood up in the public gallery. He was tall and his standing was immediately noticeable. Several people near him looked up. A murmur went through the nearest rows.

One of the judges looked up at the movement.

"Order in the gallery," he said.

Madeleine stepped out of his row and walked to the front of the public area. He stood at the low railing that separated the public from the main floor of the courtroom.

"Monsieur le Président," he said.

His voice was clear and steady. It surprised him. He had not known if his voice would work properly.

The room went quiet. The judges looked at him. The prosecutor looked at him. The defence lawyer looked at him. Champmathieu looked at him. Everyone in the room looked at him.

"Monsieur le Président," he said again, "I need to speak. I have information that is relevant to this case."

The presiding judge the president of the court frowned. "Who are you?" he said. "You are not a registered witness in this case."

"No," said Madeleine. "But what I have to say concerns the identity of the accused man."

The judge looked at him. The courtroom was completely silent. Even the sounds from outside seemed to have stopped.

"You may speak," said the judge. "Briefly."

Madeleine put his hands on the railing in front of him. He looked at the judges. Then he looked at Champmathieu in the dock.

"This man is not Jean Valjean," he said.

A sound went through the room not a shout, not a gasp, but a collective intake of breath, as if the room itself was surprised.

"I know this because I am Jean Valjean," said Madeleine.

The silence that followed this was extraordinary. It was not simply the absence of sound. It was a kind of stillness that only comes when something very unexpected happens when the world tips slightly and everyone in the room needs a moment to understand what they just heard.

Then the room came back to life. People began to talk. The judges conferred urgently. The prosecutor rose to his feet. Two guards moved toward the railing where Madeleine stood.

Madeleine kept looking at the judges.

"My name is Jean Valjean," he said, clearly and loudly, over the noise. "I was sentenced at Toulon. I escaped. I have been living in Montreuil-sur-Mer for eight years under the name Madeleine. I am the mayor of that town."

One of the judges leaned forward. "This is a serious statement to make. Do you understand the consequences?"

"Yes," said Madeleine.

"Can you prove your identity?"

"I have marks on my arm from the prison at Toulon," he said. He pushed up his sleeve. He held out his arm. A judge nodded to a guard, who came forward and looked.

"Also," said Madeleine, "I can describe conditions at Toulon that only a man who was there would know. I can name specific guards. I can name specific inmates. I can name the precise place where I slept for eleven years."

He said these things. He spoke for several minutes. He described Toulon in the kind of detail that cannot be invented. The guards whose names he gave were names that the court could check. The details he described were not the kind of details that newspapers reported.

The room listened.

When he finished, the courtroom was not quiet anymore. It was full of urgent voices. The three judges were deep in conversation. The prosecutor looked shaken for the first time.

Champmathieu, in the dock, had been watching Madeleine throughout all of this. He looked confused and stunned, like a man in a dream he cannot understand.

One of the judges held up his hand for quiet. The room settled.

"In light of this testimony," the judge said carefully, "the court is suspending this session to conduct additional inquiries. The accused, Champmathieu, is released on a temporary basis pending further investigation."

He looked at Madeleine.

"As for you," he said, "you will remain in the custody of the court while your identity is verified. Guards, please."

Two guards came to the railing. Madeleine put his hands at his sides. He did not resist. He let the guards lead him out of the room through a side door.

As he passed through the door, he turned his head and looked back at Champmathieu. The old man was being helped to stand by the defence lawyer. He looked around the room in bewilderment, like someone who has just been woken from a bad dream and cannot yet believe the morning has come.

Madeleine turned back. He walked through the door. It closed behind him.

## Part Eight The Return

The verification took two days.

Letters were sent. Documents were checked. A former guard from Toulon was brought by fast post-carriage to identify the man in custody. The guard arrived, looked at Madeleine, and said immediately: yes. That is Jean Valjean. I am certain.

Madeleine did not argue. He confirmed everything. He answered every question put to him clearly and completely. He did not try to minimise or explain away his past. He told the truth, simply and fully.

At the end of the second day, the authorities formally confirmed his identity. Jean Valjean. Escaped convict. Wanted under the terms of his sentence.

They prepared to transfer him to the prison at Toulon.

But during the night before the transfer, a guard fell asleep. And the door of the holding room was not as secure as it should have been. And Jean Valjean who had, after all, escaped from prison before walked out into the dark street.

He did not go far. He did not try to flee the region. He had not finished.

He hired a horse. He rode north through the night. He rode fast, because he knew they would discover his absence by morning and send men after him.

He needed to get back to Montreuil-sur-Mer. He needed to keep his promise.

---

## Part Nine The Hospital Room

He arrived at the hospital in Montreuil-sur-Mer in the early morning. The streets were just beginning to wake. A baker was opening his shutters. A cat was sitting on a doorstep.

He tied his horse at the side of the hospital building. He went in through a side door.

He knew the hospital well. He had been there many times as mayor, as the man who had expanded it and funded it. The nurses recognised him. One of them, a young woman who had been there from the beginning, looked at his face and understood that something serious had happened, but she did not ask. She simply showed him to Fantine's room.

He stood in the doorway.

Fantine was lying in the bed. She was very still. She looked much worse than when he had last seen her. In just a few days, the change was remarkable. Her face was very pale that specific pale of someone who is seriously ill. Her hair, which had been cut when she sold it, lay flat against her head. Her breathing was shallow.

But her eyes were open. And when she heard his footstep, she turned her head toward the door.

Her face changed when she saw him. A light came into her eyes.

"Monsieur Madeleine," she whispered.

He came in. He sat down in the chair beside her bed. He took her hand. Her hand was very thin and very cold.

"I came back," he said.

"You were gone a long time," she said. There was no accusation in her voice. She was simply noting a fact.

"I know," he said. "I am sorry."

"Did you go to Montfermeil?" she said. Her eyes searched his face. "Did you go for Cosette?"

He was quiet for a moment.

"Not yet," he said. "I need to go soon."

She nodded slowly. She seemed to accept this. Or perhaps she was simply too tired to press.

"Tell me about her," said Fantine. "Tell me is she well? Have you had news?"

"She is well," he said. "She is growing. She is healthy."

He did not know these things for certain. But he said them because Fantine needed to hear them, and because he intended to make them true.

Fantine closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them again, they were wet.

"I have been thinking about her so much," she said. "Every day. Every hour. I see her face when I close my eyes." She paused. She needed to rest between sentences now. "She had such a way of looking at you. As if she trusted you completely. As if you were the whole world."

"I know," said Madeleine softly.

"I have missed so much," said Fantine. "So many of her years. I tell myself it was necessary. I had no choice. But at night I think did I have a choice? Was there another way that I could not see?"

He held her hand a little tighter.

"You did what you could," he said. "With what you had. Nobody can do more than that."

She looked at him. Her eyes were intelligent and direct, even in her illness.

"You are kind," she said. "You are the kindest man I have met in many years."

He said nothing.

"Will you go tomorrow?" she said. "To Montfermeil?"

"Yes," he said. "I will go tomorrow."

She relaxed. The tension went out of her face. She closed her eyes again.

He sat beside her and held her hand and listened to her breathe.

---

## Part Ten Javert

The door opened.

Madeleine looked up.

Javert stood in the doorway.

He was in his inspector's coat. He had his hat in his hands. His face was composed as it always was but there was something around his eyes that was different from usual. Not anger exactly. Something more complex.

Madeleine did not move.

Fantine opened her eyes. She looked at Javert. She looked at Madeleine. She did not understand what was happening.

Javert walked into the room. He stood at the end of the bed.

"Jean Valjean," he said.

His voice was quiet. Almost formal. He was being careful, Madeleine realised. He was being careful not to disturb the sick woman.

"Yes," said Madeleine.

Fantine made a sound a small, confused sound. She turned her head toward Madeleine. "What did he say?" she whispered. "What does he mean? Jean Valjean what "

"It is all right," said Madeleine. He did not look at her. He kept looking at Javert.

"It is not all right," said Javert, but still quietly. "I am arresting you."

"I know," said Madeleine.

"You escaped from custody in Arras," said Javert. "That is a serious additional offence."

"I know."

Fantine was looking between them. The confusion on her face was deepening. She was piecing together what she was hearing. She was slow, because she was ill, but she was not stupid.

"Monsieur Madeleine," she said. Her voice was thin but clear. "Who is Jean Valjean?"

He turned to look at her then. He looked at her directly and honestly, the way you look at someone when you are about to tell them something true that you wish you did not have to say.

"It is my name," he said. "My real name."

She stared at him. The light in her eyes changed. She was working through it all of it, fast. The name. The inspector. The arrest. What it meant.

"An escaped prisoner?" she said. Just the words, quiet and flat.

"Yes."

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "And Cosette?"

"I promised," he said. "I will still "

"You are being arrested," she said.

"Yes. But there are other ways. I will find a way. I "

Javert moved. He came around to the side of the bed. He put his hand on Madeleine's arm.

"Now," said Javert.

Madeleine stood up. He looked down at Fantine. She was looking up at him. Her face was he could not name what was in it exactly. It was not anger. It was not betrayal. It was something more desolate than both. It was the face of a person who has been running toward a door for a long time, and has now arrived to find the door locked.

"Cosette," she said. Just the name.

"I will find a way," he said again.

Fantine's eyes moved to Javert. Her face changed.

"Please," she said to Javert. Her voice was very quiet but very steady. "Please. He is the only one who can bring my daughter to me. My daughter is alone. She is with strangers. Please if you take him now "

Javert looked at her. His face was unreadable.

"I am sorry, madame," he said. "I am doing my duty."

"Your duty," she said. She said the word very gently, not with contempt but with a kind of exhausted wonder. As if the word had become unfamiliar to her. As if she could no longer understand what it meant or why it mattered.

She turned her face away.

Her body went still. Her eyes closed.

Javert tightened his grip on Madeleine's arm. They moved toward the door.

Madeleine turned back once. He looked at Fantine lying still in the bed. Her chest moved she was breathing, she was still alive but she looked as if she had gone somewhere far away inside herself.

He thought: I should have gone to Montfermeil first. I should have brought Cosette before going to Arras.

But he had not known this was possible. He had thought he had more time.

Javert led him out of the room.

## Part Eleven Fantine

The nurse found Fantine a few minutes after they left.

She was still lying in the same position. Her face was very pale. The nurse came to the bed and checked. She put her hand on Fantine's hand.

Fantine opened her eyes.

"Is he gone?" she asked.

"Yes," said the nurse.

Fantine was quiet for a moment.

"Cosette," she said.

"Rest," said the nurse gently. "You need to rest."

"Cosette," said Fantine again. As if the name was a thread she was holding. As if as long as she said it, the connection remained.

The nurse sat down beside her. She brought some warm broth, but Fantine did not drink it. She lay very still and looked at the ceiling.

After a while, she spoke again.

"He was a good man," she said. "Whatever his name was. He was good."

The nurse said nothing. She sat and held Fantine's hand.

Outside the hospital window, the morning was continuing. People were walking to work. A cart went past with a delivery of coal. A child ran down the street, laughing at something. The ordinary world, going about its ordinary business, completely unaware of the room and the woman in the bed and the name that she kept saying quietly, like a prayer, like the only word she had left.

Cosette. Cosette.

The nurse did not leave her side.

Toward midday, Fantine's breathing changed. It became more shallow. More irregular. The nurse called the doctor. He came quickly and looked and said nothing that the nurse had not already understood.

Later that afternoon, a priest came. He sat beside the bed and spoke quietly.

Fantine did not open her eyes again. But her lips moved, just slightly, as if she was saying something that only she could hear.

The room was very quiet. The light in the window changed as the afternoon moved forward. First bright, then softer, then the grey of early evening.

Fantine died as the evening came. She died quietly, without drama, in the clean hospital bed that someone had provided for her. She was twenty-five years old.

She died not knowing if anyone would keep the promise that had been made to her.

She died saying her daughter's name.

## Part Twelve What Remained

Javert took Madeleine to the local police post. He secured him in a holding room. He sent dispatches to Arras and to Paris. He wrote his report with the careful, precise handwriting that he always used, noting every detail, every time, every fact.

He was thorough. He was professional. He did his duty.

But that evening, in his own room, sitting at his own desk with his own papers in front of him, he sat for a long time without writing.

He thought about the woman in the hospital bed. He thought about her face when she looked at him. He thought about the word she had used duty and the way she had said it, as if it was a word from a foreign language she could not quite translate.

He thought about Madeleine about Jean Valjean, he corrected himself in the courtroom in Arras. Standing up in the public gallery. Saying: I am Jean Valjean. Knowing what would happen next. Saying it anyway.

This bothered Javert. It bothered him in a way that he could not organise into his usual categories.

An escaped convict. That was clear. A criminal who had broken his parole, who had lived under a false identity, who had deceived the entire population of a town for eight years. This was clear. This was wrong. This was what the law said.

But then: the factory. The workers. The hospital. The school. The boots for old Bernard. The food at the widow's door. The cart lifted off old Fauchelevent. A man who had used every day of eight years to do good for other people. A man who had walked into a courtroom and condemned himself rather than let an innocent man suffer.

What category did that go in?

Javert did not have a category for it. He searched his mind and found no drawer to put it in. The world, as he understood it, had good people on one side and bad people on the other. Good people followed the law. Bad people broke it. This was the structure.

Valjean had broken the law. He was therefore on the wrong side. This was clear.

But then why did it feel wrong? Why, sitting in his room with his report in front of him, did something in his chest feel  not wrong, exactly uncertain? Unsettled?

He pushed these thoughts away. He picked up his pen. He continued his report.

He was an inspector. He had done his job. The criminal was in custody. The case was clear.

He wrote. The candle burned beside him.

He tried not to think about the woman's face.

## Part Thirteen The Escape

During the night, Jean Valjean escaped again.

This is the simple fact. The details do not matter much. He was experienced at finding weaknesses in walls, in locks, in the attention of guards. He had spent nineteen years learning how prisons worked. He had a precise and practical knowledge of the difference between a door that is truly secure and a door that only appears to be.

He left the holding room. He left the police post. He moved through the dark streets of Montreuil-sur-Mer for the last time.

He passed the factory. He looked at the building the lights off now, the machines silent, the yard empty. He could see the window of his own apartment above, dark. The room where he had lived for eight years, with his books and his table and the two silver candlesticks. He had taken the candlesticks with him when he left for Arras. They were in his bag.

He passed the church. He passed the square where old Bernard swept every morning. He passed the hospital. He stopped outside the hospital for a moment. He stood in the dark street and looked at the lit windows.

He did not know which window was Fantine's. He did not know what was happening inside. He told himself she was still there, still waiting, still holding on.

He told himself: I will find Cosette. I will find a way to bring her to safety even if I cannot bring her to Fantine. I will keep the promise even if I cannot keep it in the way I intended.

He picked up his bag. He walked away from the town, toward the south road.

By morning, he was gone.

The town woke up to find its mayor gone, its factory without direction, and its police inspector filing reports with a controlled, professional efficiency that gave no indication of whatever it was that had kept him sitting at his desk until very late the night before.

In the hospital, the nurses prepared Fantine's body carefully and with respect. They arranged her hands and smoothed her hair and placed her in the attitude of peace. They sent word to the local church. They noted, in the hospital record, the time and the date.

In the town of Montfermeil, in the Thénardier inn, a small dark-haired girl swept the floor of the kitchen and listened to the sounds of the inn above her and did not know that anything had changed. She did not know about the woman in the hospital. She did not know about the man who had escaped into the night. She did not know that these things had anything to do with her.

She swept the floor and listened to the sounds above her and waited, in the way that children wait without knowing what they are waiting for, but knowing that they are.
