# Rich Dad Poor Dad
## Chapter 1 Two Fathers
### Extended A2/B1 Reader

---

*About this book: Rich Dad Poor Dad was written by Robert T. Kiyosaki and published in 1997. It is one of the most popular books about money ever written. The book tells the story of two men who taught Robert very different lessons about money, work, and life. One man was Robert's real father. The other was his best friend's father. By watching these two men live their lives, Robert learned the most important lesson he ever learned: the way you think about money changes everything.*

---

## Part 1 A Boy with Two Teachers

Robert Toru Kiyosaki grew up in Hawaii, a group of beautiful islands in the middle of the Pacific Ocean. Hawaii is a warm and sunny place with green mountains, white beaches, and clear blue water. It is also a place where many different kinds of people live together people from Japan, China, the Philippines, Portugal, and the mainland United States. Robert was Japanese-American. His family had lived in Hawaii for several generations.

Robert's father was an educated man. He had studied very hard at university and had earned a high degree. He worked for the government of Hawaii as a teacher and, later, as the head of education for the whole state. He was intelligent, well-spoken, and respected by many people. He believed deeply in education. He believed that if you studied hard, got good grades, went to a good university, and found a good job, you would have a good life. This was his philosophy. This was the advice he gave to Robert and to his other children.

Robert loved his father. He admired him. But when Robert was nine years old, he began to notice something that confused him. His father worked very hard. He studied, he taught, he managed, he planned. And yet, despite all this effort, the family never seemed to have very much money. They lived a comfortable life, but there was always a feeling of worry about money. There were always conversations about what they could and could not afford. Robert's father sometimes talked about money with a kind of quiet sadness as if money were something outside his control, something that happened to other people, something that the family would always struggle with no matter how hard they worked.

Robert also had a best friend. His name was Mike. Mike's father was very different from Robert's father. Mike's father had not gone to university. He had not earned any kind of advanced degree. He had started working when he was young and had built his own business from almost nothing. When Robert first met Mike's father, the family was not yet rich. But over the years through very specific ways of thinking about money and making decisions Mike's father became wealthy. He owned businesses, properties, and investments. He eventually became one of the richest men in Hawaii.

Robert called these two men his "poor dad" and his "rich dad." He did not use these words to be cruel or disrespectful. He used them to describe the most important difference he noticed: the way each man thought and talked and acted around the subject of money.

Both men were strong. Both men were hard-working. Both men cared deeply about their families. Both men wanted Robert to succeed in life. But they gave him completely different advice. And slowly, over many years, Robert realised that the advice they gave him was not just different it was opposite. In almost every important area of money and finance, his two fathers believed in completely different things.

This book is the story of those differences. It is the story of what Robert learned from each man. And it is an invitation an invitation to the reader to look at their own beliefs about money and ask: which father taught me? And is that the right education?

## Part 2 Two Very Different Men

To understand this book, you need to understand the two fathers clearly. Robert spent many years watching both men. He listened to them. He worked with them. He saw how they made decisions and how those decisions shaped their lives. The differences were not small. They were large and deep and very important.

Robert's poor dad his biological father was, in many ways, a very successful man by the standards of society. He had a doctorate, the highest university degree available. He was the head of education for the state of Hawaii. He earned a good salary. He had a secure, stable job with the government. Many people in Hawaii knew his name and respected him. By most measurements that society uses to judge success, he was doing well.

But Robert's poor dad had a very specific relationship with money. He saw money as something that came from working. You work, you get paid. That was how money came to you. If you wanted more money, you worked harder or got a better job. Money was the reward for your time and your effort. And because of this belief, money was always connected to working. If you stopped working if you were sick, or retired, or lost your job the money would stop too. Money, in this view, was fragile. It depended on you continuing to show up.

His poor dad also had certain phrases that he used often. Robert remembers them very well. When there was something the family could not buy, his poor dad would say: *"We can't afford it."* This sentence, Robert noticed, shut down all thinking. It ended the conversation. It made money sound like a wall a wall that the family could not get past. You could not climb it. You could not go around it. You just had to accept that it was there.

His poor dad also said things like: *"I'm not interested in money,"* or *"Money doesn't matter that much,"* or *"Rich people are greedy."* These were not things he thought about carefully they were automatic phrases, things he had been taught to say, things he believed without questioning. And because he believed them, money remained something outside his control, something that other people had, something slightly dangerous or immoral.

His poor dad also feared debt. Debt meant borrowing money, and borrowing money felt dangerous and shameful. He avoided debt whenever he could. He believed in saving. He believed in living within your means spending less than you earn, putting the rest in a bank account, and hoping that nothing bad would happen.

Now consider Robert's rich dad Mike's father. His education was very different. He had left school early. He had no university degree. By the standards of society, he was not an "educated" man. And yet, by the time Robert was in his twenties, his rich dad was worth millions of dollars.

Rich dad's relationship with money was completely different. He did not see money as the reward for working. He saw money as a tool something you use to create more money. In this view, money was not something that came to you when you worked hard. Money was something you sent out into the world to work for you. The question was not "how do I earn more?" The question was "how do I make my money earn for me?"

His rich dad also had different phrases. When there was something he wanted but could not yet afford, he never said "I can't afford it." He said: *"How can I afford it?"* Notice the difference. The first sentence is a statement. It closes the mind. The second sentence is a question. It opens the mind. It invites the brain to think, to look for solutions, to find a way.

Rich dad also had a different relationship with debt. He understood that there were two kinds of debt: bad debt and good debt. Bad debt was borrowing money to buy things that lost value a new television, a holiday, an expensive car. Good debt was borrowing money to buy things that made more money a business, a rental property, an investment. He was not afraid of good debt. He used it carefully and intelligently to build his wealth.

These two men these two very different ways of thinking were the foundation of everything Robert learned about money.

## Part 3 The Day Everything Changed

Robert and Mike were nine years old when they made a decision that would change Robert's life. They were tired of being poor. Not extremely poor they had food and clothes and a comfortable home. But compared to some of their classmates, they felt that they did not have much. Some of the children at their school came from wealthy families. They had nicer clothes, more expensive toys, and could talk about things that Robert and Mike could not afford.

Robert and Mike decided that they wanted to be rich. They were nine years old, and they had made a serious decision: they were going to make money.

At first, they tried to make money by making fake coins. They melted lead from toothpaste tubes and poured it into plaster moulds to create their own coins. This, of course, did not work. Fake coins are not real money, and using them would be illegal. The experiment was a complete failure.

Robert went to his poor dad and told him what they had been trying to do. His poor dad laughed not cruelly, but with some amusement. He said that they were creative, but that making fake coins was not the right path. Robert asked his father what they should do instead. His father told him to go and learn from Mike's father. "Mike's dad knows about money," he said. "Go and ask him."

This was an unusual suggestion. Most parents would have given their own advice. But Robert's poor dad, to his great credit, recognised that when it came to money and business, Mike's father knew things that he did not. He was honest about this. And so he sent his son to learn from another man a man who had built real wealth through real experience.

Robert went to Mike's father. He did not know what to expect. He had seen Mike's father only a few times. The man was busy he ran several businesses and was almost always working. Robert was nervous. He was nine years old, and he was going to ask a businessman to teach him about money.

Mike had already arranged the meeting. His father agreed to see them, but he was direct and serious. He did not speak to them like children. He did not use simple, comforting words. He looked at them both and said, in a calm voice: "I will teach you. But I will not teach you in a classroom. I will not give you lectures or homework. I will teach you the way life teaches through experience. And the first thing you are going to do is work for me."

Robert was surprised. He had not expected this. He had expected to sit down and listen. Instead, rich dad offered him a job. The pay was very small only ten cents per hour, for three hours on Saturday mornings. For a nine-year-old boy who wanted to be rich, this seemed like a very strange beginning.

But Robert and Mike said yes. They came to work the following Saturday.

## Part 4 Working for Almost Nothing

Robert and Mike worked at one of rich dad's businesses a small retail shop that sold goods to local people. Their job was simple. They came in on Saturday mornings, took cans of goods off the shelves, cleaned the shelves, and put the cans back again. Three hours of work. Then they received their pay: thirty cents each. Ten cents per hour.

This continued for three weeks. Robert became more and more frustrated. Thirty cents was almost nothing. He could not buy anything meaningful with thirty cents. He had left his Saturday mornings for this his time to play, to be free, to enjoy himself and in return he was getting almost nothing. He felt used. He felt that this was not what he had expected when he agreed to learn from rich dad.

By the third week, Robert had had enough. He decided he needed to talk to rich dad. He went to the office and asked to see him. Mike's father agreed to meet him, but first he made Robert wait outside his office for a long time. Robert sat on a bench and waited. And waited. He was angry. He felt disrespected. He had come to have an important conversation, and instead he was being made to sit and wait like someone who did not matter.

When rich dad finally let him in, Robert was ready to say what he felt. He sat down and said bravely, for a nine-year-old that he was not happy. He said he was working hard, getting almost no pay, and learning nothing. He said he thought rich dad was treating him unfairly. He said he might stop coming.

Rich dad looked at him. There was no anger in his face. But there was no apology either. He was calm and steady. He listened to everything Robert said. Then he said something that Robert never forgot.

He said: "Good. Now you are starting to think like a poor person."

Robert was confused. He had come to complain, and rich dad was telling him that complaining was a mistake. That did not make sense. Robert asked what he meant.

Rich dad explained. He said that most people in the world react to money in exactly the same way Robert had just reacted. When they feel they are not earning enough, they get angry. They feel the world is unfair. They go to their boss and ask for a raise. Or they leave one job and find another that pays a little bit more. And for the rest of their lives, they continue doing this getting angry about money, looking for a slightly better salary, never really changing their situation. This, rich dad said, was the pattern of the poor and the middle class. It was a trap. It was called working for money and almost everyone did it.

Then rich dad asked Robert a question. He said: "What do you want me to teach you? Do you want me to teach you to work for money or do you want me to teach you to make money work for you?"

Robert did not fully understand the question yet. But something in it was important. He felt it, even if he could not explain it. He said he wanted to understand. He wanted to learn. He asked rich dad to explain.

Rich dad smiled slightly. He said the lesson was already happening. By paying Robert almost nothing, he had created a feeling the feeling of frustration, of anger, of wanting more. That feeling, rich dad said, was very important. Most people spend their whole lives running from that feeling. They take jobs because those jobs reduce the feeling of financial fear. They accept salaries that feel safe. They do not take risks because risks make the fear worse. And so they never change. They stay in the same place earning enough to feel safe, but never enough to feel free.

Rich dad said: "The poor and the middle class work for money. The rich have money work for them." This was the central lesson. And it could not be learned from a book or a classroom. It had to be felt.

Robert asked rich dad to raise his pay. Rich dad said no. He said he was going to lower it or he was going to raise it to a dollar an hour. The choice, he told Robert, was a lesson. Robert chose to wait and see what would happen next.

## Part 5 The Trap of the Paycheck

Rich dad continued to meet with Robert and Mike regularly. He did not simply give them information. He gave them experiences, and then explained what those experiences meant. This was very different from how school taught things. In school, you learned the theory first and then, maybe, you saw an example. With rich dad, you had the experience first, and the explanation came after.

One of the most important things rich dad taught Robert was about the paycheck trap. A paycheck is the money you receive from your employer your boss after working for them. For most people in the world, the paycheck is the most important financial event in their lives. They work. They wait. They receive their paycheck. They spend it. They wait again. Then they work again.

Rich dad called this "the rat race." The image of a rat race comes from rats on an exercise wheel in a cage. The rat runs and runs, but it never gets anywhere. It is always in the same place. This is exactly what most working people do, rich dad said. They work hard. They receive their money. They pay their bills electricity, water, food, rent or mortgage, insurance, taxes. At the end of the month, there is little or nothing left. Then the new month begins, and they work hard again. They run the wheel. They are busy and tired, but they do not move forward.

The problem, rich dad explained, is not that people are lazy or stupid. The problem is that they have two strong emotions driving them, and these emotions never let them stop and think. The first emotion is fear. The second emotion is desire or, as rich dad sometimes called it, greed.

Fear comes first. People are afraid of not having money. They are afraid of not being able to pay their rent. They are afraid of losing their home, their car, their comfortable life. This fear is very powerful. It makes people get up early in the morning and go to work. It makes them stay at jobs they do not enjoy because the job provides safety. They do not dare to leave, because leaving means uncertainty and uncertainty feels like the edge of a cliff. So they stay. And they work. And the fear keeps them in the same place.

Then comes desire. After people receive their paycheck, the fear reduces for a moment. They feel relief. They feel a brief happiness. And then desire begins to grow. They see things they want a bigger television, a newer car, nicer clothes, a larger home. They spend their money on these things. The desire is satisfied temporarily. But then the money is gone, and the fear returns. The fear and desire cycle this cycle never ends for most people. It is the engine of the rat race.

Rich dad explained that this is not an accident. This is how society is designed. The wealthy, the corporations, and the government all benefit from people working steadily, spending regularly, and not asking too many questions about the system. If people stopped working for fear and started thinking about how money really works how the wealthy use money, how corporations use taxes, how assets create income then the system would change. But most people never stop long enough to think. The fear and the desire keep them moving.

Robert was nine years old, sitting in rich dad's office, listening to this. He did not understand every word. But he understood the feeling. He remembered how he had felt those three Saturdays the frustration, the anger, the wanting more. And he understood for the first time that those feelings were not unique to him. Almost every adult in the world felt those same feelings. Almost every adult was running the same wheel.

The question was: how do you get off?

## Part 6 Fear, Desire, and Why People Stay Poor

Over many months, Robert continued to meet with rich dad and continued to work in the shop. His pay rose slowly over time first to twenty-five cents an hour, then to fifty cents. Each time his pay rose, rich dad used it as a teaching opportunity. He asked Robert to notice what happened inside him when the pay went up. Did it change anything? Did it change the way he felt about the job?

Robert noticed something interesting. When his pay rose to twenty-five cents, he felt happy for a short time. But very quickly, the desire for more returned. He wanted fifty cents. When it went to fifty cents, he felt happy for a short time again. But then he wanted a dollar. This was exactly the pattern rich dad had described. The desire never stopped. It only changed its target.

Rich dad also spent a long time talking about fear because he believed that fear was the most important force keeping people poor. Not lack of intelligence. Not lack of opportunity. Fear.

He described it this way. When a person is young a child or a teenager they have very few responsibilities. They can take risks. If something goes wrong, it is not the end of the world. They can try again. But as people grow older, their responsibilities grow. They get married. They have children. They buy a car. They buy a house or they take out a mortgage. They build a life that requires a certain amount of money to maintain. And as their responsibilities grow, their fear grows with them. They cannot afford to lose their job, because losing their job would mean losing the car, the house, the comfortable life. So they become very careful. They do not take risks. They protect what they have.

Rich dad called this "the golden handcuffs." Handcuffs are the metal rings that police use to lock a person's wrists. The golden handcuffs are the comfortable life the car, the house, the salary that locks a person in place just as firmly as real handcuffs. The life feels good. It feels safe. But it is also a prison. You cannot leave. You cannot take risks. You cannot try something new. You are locked in.

And so, as the years pass, the fear becomes stronger and the courage to change becomes weaker. The person continues in the same job, earning the same kind of money, spending it in the same way, and telling themselves that this is responsible, sensible behaviour. They congratulate themselves for being careful with money. They save a small amount each month. They believe that one day, when they retire, they will finally have enough to be comfortable. But rich dad said this belief was dangerous, because it assumed that the system would work that the job would last, that the pension would be sufficient, that prices would not rise too fast. And none of these things were guaranteed.

Robert listened to all of this and felt something shift inside him. He had grown up watching his poor dad live exactly this way carefully, responsibly, always saving a little, always worrying a little. And now he was beginning to see it from a different angle. What if careful and responsible was not the right way? What if the very behaviour that his father believed in getting a good job, working hard, saving money was exactly what kept people stuck?

Rich dad was careful to explain that he was not saying that poor dad was bad or wrong as a person. He was a good man, a kind man, an intelligent man. But he had learned certain lessons about money from his own parents, and those parents had learned them from their parents, and none of those lessons had ever been questioned. The lessons were simply passed on, generation after generation, like old furniture useful once, but perhaps no longer fitting the world you actually live in.

The key question, rich dad said, was this: are your feelings about money serving you or are they controlling you?

## Part 7 Assets, Liabilities, and the Rat Race

As Robert grew older from nine, to eleven, to thirteen his lessons with rich dad became more detailed. Rich dad began to teach him specific things about how money works, not just how people feel about it. One of the most important lessons he ever taught came when Robert was about twelve years old.

Rich dad sat Robert and Mike down and drew a simple picture. It was a diagram with two columns. On one side, he wrote the word "Assets." On the other side, he wrote the word "Liabilities." Then he explained the difference.

An asset, rich dad said, is something that puts money into your pocket. It is something that produces income money that comes to you regularly without you having to work for it every time. Examples of assets: a rental property that tenants pay you to live in each month; shares in a company that pay you dividends a portion of the company's profits; a business that runs without you needing to be there every day; a book you wrote that earns royalties money paid to you each time someone buys it.

A liability, rich dad said, is the opposite. A liability is something that takes money out of your pocket. It is something that costs you money, either regularly or when you buy it. Examples of liabilities: a car, because you must pay for insurance, petrol, maintenance, and parking every month; a credit card balance with high interest; consumer goods things you buy for pleasure that decrease in value over time.

Then rich dad said the most important thing about this diagram. He said: "The rich buy assets. The poor only have expenses. The middle class buy liabilities and think they are assets."

Robert did not fully understand the last part. He asked about it. Rich dad explained with one specific example.

He talked about houses. In society, most people are told that buying a home is the best investment you can make. They are told that a home is an asset. Parents tell their children this. Banks tell their customers this. The entire culture supports this idea. "Buy a house," people say. "It is your biggest asset. It is the key to financial security."

But rich dad said this was wrong. A house, for most people, is not an asset it is a liability. Consider what happens when you buy a house. You take out a large mortgage. Every month, you must pay the mortgage a large payment to the bank. You must pay property taxes money to the local government. You must pay for maintenance repairs when things break. You must pay for insurance. You must pay for water, electricity, and other utilities. Every month, the house takes money out of your pocket.

An asset, rich dad reminded them, puts money into your pocket. A house takes money out. Therefore, for most people, a house is a liability.

Now, a house can become an asset under certain conditions. If you rent it to someone else and the rent they pay you is more than all the costs of maintaining the house, then the house puts money in your pocket and it is an asset. But the house you live in yourself? That is not an asset. That is a liability. And calling it an asset is one of the most common and most costly financial mistakes that people make.

Robert absorbed this idea slowly. It went against everything he had heard from the adults around him. He thought about his poor dad's house the family home. His father had always spoken of it as his most valuable possession, his security, his asset. And now rich dad was saying it was actually a liability. This was a difficult thing to accept. But the logic was clear. The money flowed out every month, not in. By definition, it was a liability.

Rich dad then explained the rat race in terms of assets and liabilities. Most people, he said, work for money. They get a paycheck. They use most of it to pay for liabilities their car, their house, their bills, their lifestyle. They have no assets producing income for them. So they need to keep working because if they stop, the money stops, and they cannot pay for the liabilities. They are on the wheel. They are the rat.

The rich, however, spend their time building assets. Every time they earn money, they do not rush to spend it on liabilities. They invest it in a rental property, in shares, in a business. These assets then produce income. Over time, the income from their assets grows. Eventually, the income from their assets is more than enough to pay for all their living expenses. At that point, they are free. They do not need to work for money any more. Their money works for them. They have escaped the rat race.

This was the difference. This was the secret. Not intelligence. Not education. Not luck. The difference was whether you built assets or accumulated liabilities.

## Part 8 Two Paths Forward

Robert was still a young man when he understood these lessons. He understood them not just with his mind, but with his body he had felt them. He had felt the frustration of working for almost nothing. He had felt the desire for more money when his pay rose. He had sat quietly and listened as rich dad explained how fear and desire trap people in jobs they do not want. He had drawn the diagram of assets and liabilities and understood really understood why most people never become free.

But understanding is not the same as doing. Rich dad knew this. And so he gave Robert a very specific instruction about the future. He said: "When you are earning money, always pay yourself first." Most people, he explained, receive their paycheck and then pay their bills the mortgage, the electricity, the phone, the insurance. Whatever is left, if there is anything, they save. Rich dad said this was backwards.

Paying yourself first means this: before you pay anyone else, take a portion of your money and invest it put it toward buying an asset. Then pay your bills with what remains. This creates pressure because sometimes you do not have enough for the bills. But that pressure, rich dad said, is good. It forces your brain to find a way. It forces you to think like a rich person how can I earn more? How can I create another source of income? How can I solve this problem?

The poor and the middle class pay everyone else first and themselves last, or not at all. The rich pay themselves first, every time, no matter what. This is not selfishness. This is discipline. And over time, this discipline creates assets, and those assets create freedom.

Rich dad also warned Robert about something else about the most common mistake that people make when they start to understand money. Many people learn that they should invest. They learn about stocks and real estate and business. They get excited. And then they make a mistake: they invest with their emotions, not with their minds. They see a stock rising and they buy it because they feel excitement, because they feel the desire for fast profits. They see a house for sale and they buy it because they feel the fear of missing a good opportunity.

Investing with emotions almost always leads to poor results. The stock you buy when you are excited is often the worst time to buy. The house you buy in a rush, because you are afraid someone else will take it, is often overpriced. Rich people, rich dad said, do not make decisions with their emotions. They make decisions with knowledge, with analysis, with patience.

He told Robert: "The most powerful thing you can invest in is your own mind." Learn about how money works. Learn about taxes. Learn about accounting the language of money. Learn about how businesses operate. Learn about how markets move. The more you understand, the better your decisions will be, and the faster your assets will grow.

Robert left those early conversations with rich dad carrying a great weight of new ideas. Some of these ideas were exciting. Some were frightening. Some went against everything he had been taught by his poor dad, by his school, by his culture. He did not know yet how to act on all of them. He did not know yet how the story of his life would unfold. He only knew that he had learned something true something that most people never learn and that this truth had the power to change everything.

He had two paths in front of him. The first path was the one his poor dad had walked education, a good job, a secure salary, a comfortable house, careful saving, a retirement plan. This was a safe and respectable path. Many people walked it. It was not a bad path. But it was also the rat race the wheel that never stopped.

The second path was the one rich dad had pointed toward financial education, assets, investment, patience, and the long journey toward the kind of freedom where your money works for you and you work only when you choose to. This was a harder path. It required learning things that school never taught. It required making decisions that felt uncomfortable. It required patience, when all around you, people were rushing.

Robert chose the second path.

This book is about that choice and everything that came after it.

## Chapter Summary

**What you learned in Chapter 1:**

Chapter 1 introduces the most important idea in the entire book: the difference between the way rich people and poor people think about money. Robert Kiyosaki grew up with two very different father figures his educated, hard-working biological father (poor dad) and his best friend's self-made, businessminded father (rich dad). These two men gave him completely opposite lessons about money.

Poor dad believed in the power of education, a good job, and a secure salary. He worked hard, earned a reasonable income, and managed his money carefully. But he always worked for money, and money always held power over him. His beliefs "we can't afford it," "rich people are greedy," "save your money and be careful" were inherited from generations before him and were never questioned.

Rich dad believed in making money work for you instead of working for money. He taught Robert to ask "How can I afford it?" instead of "I can't afford it." He taught him the difference between assets (things that put money in your pocket) and liabilities (things that take money out). He showed Robert the rat race the cycle of fear and desire that keeps most people earning and spending without ever becoming free. And he pointed toward a different way: financial education, asset building, and the discipline to invest before spending.

Robert chose to follow rich dad's path. The rest of the book follows what he learned along that journey.

**Key ideas from Chapter 1:**
- Poor dad: work for money, fear debt, value education and job security
- Rich dad: make money work for you, use good debt wisely, value financial education
- The paycheck trap: earn → spend → fear → repeat (the rat race)
- Fear and desire: the two emotions that keep people stuck
- Assets vs. Liabilities: the central diagram of the whole book
- "The rich buy assets. The poor only have expenses. The middle class buy liabilities and think they are assets."
- Pay yourself first: invest before paying your bills
- The most powerful investment is in your own financial education

## 📐 Grammar Notes Chapter 1

### 1. Present Simple Facts, Habits, and Repeated Truths
Rich Dad Poor Dad uses the present simple tense constantly to state ideas that are general truths things that are true for most people most of the time.

- *"The poor and the middle class work for money."*
- *"An asset is something that puts money into your pocket."*
- *"A liability takes money out of your pocket."*
- *"The rich pay themselves first."*

These are not descriptions of one person on one day. They are general patterns. Present simple is the right tense for patterns.

---

### 2. Used To Past Habits and Situations
The chapter often describes beliefs and behaviours that existed in the past especially in Robert's childhood.

- *"His poor dad used to say, 'We can't afford it.'"*
- *"Robert used to feel frustrated when his pay was very low."*
- *"Most adults used to believe that a house was always an asset."*

**Used to + base verb** describes something that was true or happened regularly in the past but may not be true now.

---

### 3. Contrast Connectors But, However, Instead, On the Other Hand
The chapter is built on contrasts poor dad vs. rich dad, assets vs. liabilities, working for money vs. making money work for you. Contrast connectors carry these comparisons.

- *"He was intelligent and well-spoken. But the family never had much money."*
- *"He did not believe in big changes. Instead, he believed in small, specific actions."*
- *"Paying your bills first feels responsible. However, it keeps you trapped."*

---

### 4. Conditional Sentences If + Present Simple → Will
The book explains cause and effect through first conditional sentences what happens if you follow one pattern or another.

- *"If you pay yourself first, your brain will find a way to pay the bills."*
- *"If you invest with your emotions, you will make poor decisions."*
- *"If your habits stay the same, your financial situation will stay the same."*

Pattern: **If + subject + present simple, subject + will + base verb**

---

### 5. Comparison Rich vs. Poor, Assets vs. Liabilities
The chapter uses comparison structures to contrast two very different worlds.

**Comparative adjectives:**
- *"Rich dad's beliefs were completely different from poor dad's beliefs."*
- *"A rental property is more useful than a television."*

**More + adjective / Less + adjective:**
- *"Financial education is more important than a university degree."*
- *"The fear becomes stronger and the courage to change becomes weaker."*

**Whereas / While (parallel contrast):**
- *"Poor dad said 'I can't afford it,' whereas rich dad said 'How can I afford it?'"*
