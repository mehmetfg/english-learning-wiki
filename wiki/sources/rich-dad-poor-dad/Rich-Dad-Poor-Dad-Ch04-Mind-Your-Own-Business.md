# Rich Dad Poor Dad
## Chapter 4 Mind Your Own Business
### Extended A2/B1 Reader

---

*What happened so far: In Chapter 1, Robert met his two father figures and learned the central difference in their philosophies about money. In Chapter 2, rich dad showed that most people are driven by fear and desire, and that the key is to build systems, not trade time for money. In Chapter 3, rich dad introduced the language of money assets and liabilities and showed through three cash flow diagrams how the rich, the middle class, and the poor each manage money very differently. He explained why a family home is a liability for most people, and why financial intelligence is a learnable skill. Chapter 4 introduces the third great lesson: mind your own business and what that really means.*

---

## Part 1 The Man Behind the Hamburger

One day, when Robert was studying at university, a professor arranged a special visit. A well-known businessman came to speak to the students. He was not famous for being a writer or a politician or a scientist. He was famous for something much more ordinary-sounding: he had built one of the largest fast-food companies in the world.

His name was Ray Kroc, and the company was McDonald's.

The students gathered in the auditorium. Kroc stood at the front, comfortable and confident, the kind of man who had spoken to thousands of audiences and felt at home in front of any crowd. He told the story of McDonald's how he had discovered the original small restaurant in California run by two brothers named McDonald, how he had seen immediately that their system was something extraordinary, and how he had eventually bought the rights to expand the business across the country and then around the world.

After the talk, Kroc asked the students if they had any questions. Several students raised their hands. They asked about management, about the food, about competition, about international expansion. Kroc answered each question patiently and clearly. He seemed to enjoy the questions. But then one student asked a question that changed the atmosphere in the room.

The student asked: "Mr Kroc, what business are you in?"

The room went quiet. It seemed like a strange question. Everyone in the room knew what business Ray Kroc was in. He had just spent an hour explaining it. He was in the hamburger business. He sold millions of hamburgers every day in thousands of restaurants across dozens of countries.

Kroc smiled. He said: "I am not in the hamburger business."

The room was silent. The students looked at each other. Then Kroc said: "I am in the real estate business."

He let that sit for a moment. Then he explained. McDonald's, he said, was not primarily a restaurant company. Yes, it sold hamburgers. Yes, the food was the reason customers came. But the real asset the thing that actually built Kroc's wealth and the wealth of the company was not the food. It was the land. Every time a new McDonald's restaurant opened, it opened on a piece of land. And in the vast majority of cases, McDonald's owned that land. The restaurant franchise the right to sell McDonald's food was given to an individual operator. But McDonald's kept the land underneath the building. Over decades, as cities grew and land values increased, McDonald's became one of the largest landowners in the world. The land was the asset. The hamburgers were the reason people showed up. But the land was where the real money was.

Robert never forgot this story. It illustrated, more vividly than any diagram could, the difference between your profession what you do every day, how you earn your money and your business. Your business is not necessarily what you do at work. Your business is what you are building for yourself in the asset column.

## Part 2 Your Profession and Your Business

Robert brought the McDonald's story back to rich dad and told him about it. Rich dad was not surprised he had known for a long time that the most successful businesses in the world were often in a completely different business from the one they appeared to be in on the surface. But he was pleased that Robert had heard it from someone so well-known, and from an experience he would not forget.

Rich dad then connected the McDonald's story to the third great lesson: mind your own business.

He said: "Most people confuse their profession with their business. They think the two are the same thing. They are not."

He asked Robert to think about a doctor. A doctor goes to school for many years undergraduate degree, medical school, residency, perhaps additional specialisation. This training takes a very long time and costs a great deal of money. At the end of it, the doctor begins to practise medicine. They work long hours. They help patients. They earn a very good income one of the better incomes in most countries. People respect them. Society needs them.

But the doctor's profession medicine is not the same as the doctor's business. The doctor's profession is what they do every day to earn money. Their business if they build one is their personal asset column: the investments they make, the properties they own, the retirement accounts they fund, the shares they hold.

Most doctors, rich dad said, never truly build their own business. They are so absorbed in their profession the long hours, the demanding work, the continuous learning required to stay current in medicine that they never step back and ask: "What am I building for myself? What will produce income when I stop working? What assets do I own?"

And so many doctors like many lawyers, engineers, teachers, and other professionals end up in a painful situation in their later years. They have had a long and respected career. They have helped many people. They have earned significant income. But they have spent most of it on a beautiful home, on private school for their children, on a comfortable lifestyle, on taxes. When they retire, they depend on a pension or on personal savings that may or may not be enough. Their professional income stops, and there is very little in the asset column to replace it.

Rich dad said: "The word 'professional' means you are very good at your job. But financial success is not the same as professional success. A person can be one of the best doctors in the country and still be financially vulnerable still dependent on a salary, still one medical emergency away from financial difficulty. To be financially free, you need a business. Not just a job, no matter how good that job is."

He then said the phrase that became one of the most quoted lines from the book: "Mind your own business." He was not telling Robert to be rude or to ignore other people's affairs. He meant something very specific: keep your eye on your own asset column. Build it. Protect it. Do not wait for someone else to build it for you.

## Part 3 What Is Your Business?

Rich dad said that the third lesson had two parts. The first part was the idea itself understanding the difference between your profession and your business, and committing to building your own asset column. The second part was more practical: what exactly should go into that asset column? What counts as a real asset?

He drew a simple box on a piece of paper and wrote the words "Your Asset Column" above it. Then he asked Robert: "What would you put in here?"

Robert thought about it. He said: "A rental property that produces income?"

Rich dad nodded. "Yes. What else?"

"Shares in a company that pays dividends?"

"Yes. What else?"

Robert thought harder. He said: "A business that runs without you being there every day?"

"Yes," rich dad said. "Very important. What else?"

Robert was less certain now. He mentioned savings. Rich dad accepted this, but said that savings earning a low rate of interest especially below the rate of inflation were a very weak asset. He mentioned gold and silver. Rich dad accepted these as stores of value, though not always income-producing. Robert mentioned a car and rich dad firmly said no. A car depreciates it loses value over time and costs money every month. A car is a liability.

Then rich dad gave his own complete list.

He said the assets worth building were:

**Businesses that do not require your presence.** You own them, you manage them, but the day-to-day operations run without you. Perhaps you hire a manager. Perhaps the business model itself does not require constant attention. The point is that the business produces income whether you are there or not. Rich dad said this was the most powerful category because a well-run business could produce large amounts of income and grow in value over time. However, it was also the most difficult to build it required a great deal of work, knowledge, and patience at the beginning.

**Stocks.** Shares in companies. When you own stocks, you own a small piece of a business without having to run it yourself. If the company is well-run, its value grows over time, and it may pay you dividends a portion of its profits. Rich dad was careful to add that buying stocks requires education you need to understand what you are buying, why, and at what price. Buying stocks without knowledge is gambling, not investing.

**Bonds.** These are loans that you make to a company or a government. In return, they pay you a regular amount of interest. Bonds are generally less risky than stocks but also produce less growth. They are useful for stability for the part of your asset column that you want to protect more carefully.

**Income-generating real estate.** Properties that you rent to tenants. The rent comes in every month. If you manage the property well and choose the right location, the rent not only covers the costs mortgage, taxes, maintenance but also produces extra income. Over the long term, the property itself may also increase in value, providing a capital gain when you sell.

**Notes IOUs.** This is a more unusual one. A note, or IOU, is a formal promise to repay a debt. If you lend money to someone a business, an individual and they sign a formal agreement to repay it with interest, you hold an asset. The interest they pay you is income. This is how banks make money: they hold millions of notes loans they have given and collect interest on each one.

**Royalties from intellectual property.** If you create something a book, a piece of music, a patent for an invention, a piece of software and other people use it or buy it, they pay you royalties. The creation takes time and effort. But once it is created, it can produce income for many years without further effort.

**Anything else that has value, produces income, has a ready market, and grows in value.** This is the broadest category. Some people collect rare art or rare coins that increase in value. Some people buy vintage cars that become more valuable over time. The key requirement is that the item genuinely increases in value it is not simply something you enjoy that you tell yourself is an investment.

## Part 4 The Important Difference: Keep Your Job, Build Your Business

One of the most practical and useful parts of rich dad's teaching was something that many people missed when they first heard the phrase "mind your own business." They assumed it meant: quit your job and start a company. Leave your salary behind, take the risk, go out on your own.

Rich dad said this was not what he meant. Not yet. Not for most people. Not at the beginning.

He said: "Keep your job. Keep your salary. You need it. Use it. But while you are working for your employer while someone else is paying you a salary use a portion of that salary to build your own asset column. Do both at the same time."

This was a very important distinction. The message was not "leave the system." The message was "participate in the system but also build outside it." Work for your employer during the day, and earn the income you need to live. But do not spend all of that income on liabilities. Do not let desire take every dollar the moment it arrives. Take a portion even a small portion and invest it. Put it in your asset column. Let it grow there.

Rich dad told Robert that the goal, in the early stages, was modest and clear. Not to become rich immediately. Not to quit work within a year. The goal was simply to begin. To start putting real assets in the column. To make the column something that existed and was growing, rather than something empty.

He described a typical progression. A young person gets their first proper job. They begin earning. At first, perhaps they invest five percent of their income not a large amount, but real. They put it into a low-cost index fund a type of investment that spreads your money across many different companies and they leave it there. They do not touch it. They contribute every month. Over the years, the compound growth of that investment becomes significant.

Then, as their financial education grows, they begin to look at other opportunities. Perhaps they save enough to make a small down payment on a rental property a modest flat in an area they know well. The rent from the flat adds to their income. They use some of that income to invest further. The asset column grows another branch. Then another. And slowly not in months, but in years the asset column begins to produce income that is significant relative to their salary. Perhaps it reaches twenty percent of what they earn at their job. Then thirty percent. Then fifty.

At some point and this is the key moment the income from the asset column is enough to cover their living expenses. At that point, they are financially free. They may choose to continue working, because they enjoy it. Or they may choose to stop, because they no longer need the salary. But it is now a choice, not a necessity. And that choice that freedom to decide is what the whole process has been building toward.

Rich dad said: "Financial freedom is not about having no work to do. It is about having the choice of whether to work. When your money works for you, you choose how to spend your time. When you work for money, someone else chooses for you."

## Part 5 What Rich Dad Owned and Why

To make this less abstract, Robert asked rich dad to describe his own asset column what he actually owned, and how he had built it over the years.

Rich dad was thoughtful for a moment, and then he described it in broad terms. He did not share specific numbers he was a private man about the details of his wealth but he described the structure clearly.

He said his asset column had started very small. When he was a young man just a few years older than Robert during those first Saturday lessons he had very little money. He was working in his first business, earning just enough to live on. But he had made a decision, very early, to keep his expenses low and to put every dollar he could into assets. Not big, dramatic assets. Modest ones.

His first real asset was a piece of land. It was not in a desirable area. It was not expensive. He had bought it with a small amount of saved money and an even smaller loan. He had bought it because he believed after research, after asking questions, after observing how that part of the city was growing that the land would be worth more in ten years. He was right, though not for the exact reasons he expected. The city grew in a slightly different direction than he had predicted, but it grew, and the land rose in value with it.

With the profit from selling that land, he made a slightly larger investment. And then with the profit from that, a larger one still. He did not do this quickly. He did not become rich in five years. He made mistakes. He bought things that did not increase in value. He made investments that lost money. But he learned from each one. And he kept going. He did not stop. He did not decide, after a few failures, that investing was too risky or too complicated. He decided that each failure was a lesson an expensive lesson, sometimes, but a lesson nonetheless.

By the time Robert knew him well, rich dad owned multiple businesses in Hawaii. He owned rental properties small ones and large ones. He owned stocks in several companies that he had studied carefully before purchasing. He had cash and bonds as a stable base. He had built the kind of diversified asset column that produced income from several different directions. If one area produced less in a particular year if the real estate market slowed, or if one business had a difficult period the other areas continued to produce. The column was wide, not just tall.

Rich dad called this "not putting all your eggs in one basket" a common English expression that means not depending on one single thing for your financial security. If you own only one type of asset, and something goes wrong with that type, your entire financial life is at risk. But if your assets are diversified spread across different types a problem in one area does not destroy everything.

He also said something that Robert found very striking. He said: "I am still building. Even now. There is no point at which a rich person says: 'That is enough. I am finished.' You keep learning. You keep looking for opportunities. You keep adding to the column. Not out of greed not because you always want more. But because the habit of building, the skill of investing, the practice of reading opportunities these are things you must exercise regularly, or they get weaker."

Financial intelligence, rich dad said, was like physical fitness. If you stop using it, it diminishes. You must keep practising, keep learning, keep questioning. The rich stay rich not just because they accumulated assets, but because they never stopped building the habits that created those assets in the first place.

## Part 6 The Enemy of the Asset Column

Rich dad spent one particularly serious meeting talking about what he called "the greatest enemy of the asset column." Robert expected him to name something like bad investments, or market crashes, or rising interest rates. These were the kinds of answers that financial journalists and economic textbooks gave. But rich dad's answer was different. He said the greatest enemy of the asset column was the human desire for comfort and more specifically, the habit of lifestyle inflation.

Lifestyle inflation, he explained, is what happens when your income rises and your spending rises with it automatically, without thought, without a deliberate decision. Most people experience this without being aware of it. When a young person gets their first good job, they buy a better flat. When they get their first raise, they buy a newer car. When they get promoted, they take a more expensive holiday. Each step upward in income is matched, almost immediately, by a step upward in spending. And because every new level of spending quickly feels normal quickly feels like the baseline, the minimum acceptable standard there is never any surplus to invest.

Rich dad called this "the lifestyle trap," and he distinguished it carefully from what he considered acceptable. He was not telling Robert to live like a monk to eat nothing, to own nothing, to enjoy nothing. He was not saying that spending money on good food, on travel, on beautiful things was wrong. He was saying that the timing and the sequence mattered enormously.

The question was: what comes first in your financial life?

For most people, the answer is: the lifestyle comes first. They earn money, and the first thing that happens is the lifestyle takes it. The mortgage payment, the car payment, the restaurant meals, the new clothes, the holidays all of this happens first. And then, if there is anything left which there usually is not they think about investing. The asset column is always last. It receives the leftovers.

Rich dad said the sequence should be the opposite. The asset column should come first. Before the mortgage payment goes out or at least at the same time a portion of the income should go into the asset column. Before the desire has a chance to spend the money, the investment is already made. The lifestyle receives what is left. This is what "paying yourself first" really means in practice: not that you are selfish, but that you are disciplined enough to make the asset column the first priority, not the last.

He gave Robert a very practical piece of advice. He said: "Every time your income rises every time you get a raise, every time your investments produce extra income make a rule for yourself. Half of the increase goes into the asset column. The other half you can enjoy. This way, as your income grows, your asset column grows at the same speed. And the lifestyle improves too just more slowly, and always supported by actual assets rather than by debt."

Robert found this advice almost unbearably practical. It did not require genius. It did not require a special talent. It required only a rule a simple, consistent rule applied every time the situation arose. The power was not in the rule itself. The power was in applying it without exception.

## Part 7 Why Most People Never Start

Rich dad asked Robert a question that Robert found difficult to answer: "If building an asset column is so important if it is, as I have told you, the most important financial habit a person can develop then why do most people never do it? Why is the middle class so large, and the group of people who are genuinely financially free so small?"

Robert thought about it. He offered several answers. He said maybe people did not know they had not been taught. Rich dad agreed that this was partly true. Financial education was largely absent from schools and from most family environments. But he said it was not the complete answer. Information about investing about stocks, about real estate, about building businesses was not secret. It was available in libraries, in bookshops, in newspapers, in conversations with people who had done it. The information was not hidden. And yet most people did not act on it.

Robert suggested that perhaps people were afraid afraid of losing money, afraid of making mistakes, afraid of looking foolish if something went wrong. Rich dad agreed that this was a significant factor. Fear of financial loss was one of the most powerful forces in human behaviour. He had already discussed this in the earlier lessons about fear and desire. The prospect of losing money felt, to most people, much worse than the prospect of staying exactly where they were. And because building an asset column required risk real, genuine risk most people preferred the safer path of the salary. The salary was certain, predictable, comfortable. The investment might fail.

But then rich dad said something that took Robert by surprise. He said: "The biggest reason most people never start is not ignorance and not fear. The biggest reason is that they are too busy."

Robert was not sure he had heard correctly. He asked rich dad to explain.

Rich dad said: "Think about the people you know who are in the middle class. Are they lazy? No. They are some of the hardest-working people you will ever meet. They wake up early. They work long hours. They come home tired. Then they have family responsibilities dinner, homework with the children, household tasks, social commitments. And when all of that is done, it is late, and they are exhausted, and the idea of sitting down to study a financial statement or research a property investment feels impossible. They collapse into the sofa and they watch television, because the television asks nothing of them, and everything else does."

He continued: "So they intend to start. They plan to start. They tell themselves that when things are less busy when the project at work is finished, when the children are a little older, when they have a bit more time they will sit down and sort out their finances. But the busy time never ends. Life fills every space that is available. And so the intention stays an intention, and the asset column stays empty, and the years pass."

Rich dad said that the solution to this was not to wait for free time. Free time would never arrive in sufficient amounts. The solution was to make financial education a non-negotiable part of life as regular and as important as eating or sleeping. Not hours every day. Not anything dramatic. Even thirty minutes a week, spent reading about finance, looking at investment opportunities, or studying a balance sheet, was thirty minutes more than most people spent. Over a year, that added up to twenty-six hours of financial education. Over a decade, two hundred and sixty hours. The knowledge accumulated. The opportunities became visible. The habit of seeing assets and liabilities everywhere became natural.

And then one day after years of small, consistent attention the first real opportunity appeared, clearly and unmistakably. And the person who had been paying attention was ready to act on it. While everyone else was still too busy.

## Part 8 Beginning to See the World Differently

By the time Robert finished university, something had shifted permanently in the way he saw the world around him. He did not see it as most of his classmates and colleagues saw it. When they looked at a city, they saw buildings, roads, shops, and restaurants. When Robert looked at a city, he saw assets and liabilities. He saw cash flows moving between places. He saw which buildings produced income and which consumed it. He saw which businesses were selling their time and which were selling systems.

He began to notice things that he had been blind to before. He noticed that the small grocery store on the corner had been owned by the same family for thirty years, and that behind the shop, the family quietly owned the building next door and the building across the street. The shop was the business the thing they were known for, the job they did every day. But the buildings were the asset column, growing quietly in value while the family worked.

He noticed that two of his neighbours had similar salaries both working in offices, both earning well. But one of them spent every holiday talking about renovating his home, adding an extension, improving the kitchen. Every raise was funnelled back into the house. The other neighbour spent her evenings reading financial reports and had, over the past several years, purchased two small rental flats in another part of the city. She did not talk about them much. They were not glamorous. But they produced income every month, and that income had funded her third flat.

Robert began to understand that financial freedom was not a secret kept by a small club of insiders. It was not luck, and it was not exclusively for people who were born into wealthy families or who had exceptional talents. It was a way of seeing and a set of habits that followed from that way of seeing.

The way of seeing was this: every financial decision produces a cash flow. Money either moves toward you into your pocket, into your asset column or it moves away from you out of your pocket, into someone else's asset column. Most people go through their entire lives without ever noticing this movement clearly. They make decisions based on what feels comfortable, what seems normal, what their friends and neighbours are doing, what society tells them represents success. And all the while, money moves out of their pockets and into the asset columns of the people who have learned to see.

Rich dad had given Robert something that most schools, most parents, most financial advisors, and most cultures never give to young people: a way of seeing. Not a formula for getting rich quickly. Not a guaranteed investment strategy. But a lens a way of looking at the world in which assets and liabilities were visible, in which cash flow was understandable, in which the difference between building for yourself and building for someone else was clear.

He had been told, many times over many years, to mind his own business. He finally understood what that meant. It did not mean to be selfish or to ignore the world. It meant to pay attention careful, consistent, lifelong attention to the one thing that nobody else would build for him: his own asset column.

This chapter in Robert's education was not the end. It was, in many ways, the beginning. He had the lens. Now he needed to use it.

## Chapter Summary

**What you learned in Chapter 4:**

Chapter 4 introduces the third great lesson: mind your own business. This phrase does not mean be rude or indifferent. It means: pay constant, deliberate attention to your own asset column because no one else will build it for you.

The chapter opens with Ray Kroc's famous statement that he was not in the hamburger business but in the real estate business. McDonald's story illustrates perfectly the difference between your profession what you do every day to earn money and your business what you are building for yourself in the asset column. A doctor is in the medical profession, but their business, if they build one, is their personal asset column: the rental properties they own, the investments they hold, the income streams they have created.

Rich dad explains in detail what belongs in a real asset column: businesses that run without you, stocks, bonds, income-generating real estate, notes (loans that pay you interest), royalties from intellectual property, and any item that genuinely grows in value. He is clear about what does not belong: cars, most consumer goods, and most importantly a family home that consumes money every month.

The chapter gives practical advice: keep your job and your salary, but use a portion of that salary to begin building the asset column simultaneously. The goal in the early stages is not to get rich quickly it is simply to begin. Even a small start, consistently maintained over years, produces remarkable results through compound growth.

Rich dad also names the greatest enemy of the asset column: lifestyle inflation. When income rises and spending rises automatically with it, there is never any surplus to invest. The solution is to put the asset column first to invest before lifestyle spending takes the income.

The chapter ends with a reflection on why most people never start: not because of ignorance or fear alone, but because they are too busy. Life fills every available space. The solution is to make financial education non-negotiable a small, regular commitment, maintained for years, that slowly changes the way you see the world.

**Key ideas from Chapter 4:**
- Ray Kroc was not in the hamburger business he was in the real estate business
- Profession (what you do for a paycheck) vs. business (what you build for yourself)
- The asset column: businesses without you, stocks, bonds, rental real estate, notes, royalties
- Keep your job AND build your asset column at the same time do not wait
- Lifestyle inflation is the greatest enemy of the asset column
- "Pay yourself first" means the asset column comes before lifestyle spending
- Most people never start not because of ignorance or fear but because they are always too busy
- Financial freedom is a way of seeing a lens that, once learned, changes everything

## 📐 Grammar Notes Chapter 4

### 1. Gerunds as Subjects and Objects
Chapter 4 uses gerunds verb + -ing forms frequently, both as the subject of a sentence and as the object of a verb. This is a common feature of non-fiction English.

**Gerund as subject:**
- ***Building** an asset column takes time.*
- ***Investing** requires education.*
- ***Paying** yourself first means the asset column comes before lifestyle.*

**Gerund as object (after certain verbs):**
- *He enjoyed **explaining** the system.*
- *Rich dad kept **learning** throughout his life.*
- *Most people avoid **thinking** carefully about their finances.*

Common verbs followed by gerunds: enjoy, avoid, keep, finish, consider, suggest, recommend, practise, imagine.

---

### 2. The Passive Voice Describing Systems and Structures
When the chapter describes how money, society, or financial systems work in general, the passive voice appears frequently. The agent (who does the action) is not important the action itself is what matters.

- *"Financial education **was never taught** in schools."*
- *"The franchise **was given** to an individual operator."*
- *"The land **was kept** by McDonald's."*
- *"This lesson **is often misunderstood** by people who hear it for the first time."*

Pattern: **was/were + past participle** (for past passive); **is/are + past participle** (for present passive).

---

### 3. Reported Speech Sharing What Someone Said
Chapter 4 frequently reports what rich dad said not using his exact words, but describing them. This is reported speech, also called indirect speech.

**Direct speech (exact words):**
- *Rich dad said: "I am in the real estate business."*

**Reported speech (paraphrased, tense shifted back):**
- *Rich dad said that he **was** in the real estate business.*
- *Rich dad explained that McDonald's **kept** the land beneath every restaurant.*
- *He told Robert that the timing and sequence **mattered** enormously.*

Pattern: **said/told + that + subject + past tense verb**

---

### 4. Phrasal Verbs
Chapter 4 is rich in phrasal verbs verbs combined with a preposition or adverb that create a new meaning.

| Phrasal verb | Meaning | Example from chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **end up** | to arrive at a result or situation | *They end up financially vulnerable.* |
| **step back** | to stop and consider something from a distance | *Doctors never step back to ask what they are building.* |
| **build up** | to increase gradually | *The asset column builds up over years.* |
| **sort out** | to deal with and resolve a problem | *They plan to sort out their finances when things are less busy.* |
| **act on** | to do something based on an idea or plan | *The person who paid attention was ready to act on the opportunity.* |

---

### 5. Contrast and Concession Although, Even Though, Despite
Chapter 4 frequently acknowledges that something is true on one side before pointing to a more important truth on the other side.

- ***Although** many people earn good salaries, most do not build real asset columns.*
- ***Even though** financial information is freely available, most people do not act on it.*
- ***Despite** working very hard, middle-class professionals often retire with little financial security.*

**Although / Even though** → introduce a contrast clause (can come at the beginning or middle).
**Despite** → followed by a noun phrase or gerund, not a full clause.
- *Despite **working hard**, they never built an asset column.*
- *Despite **the effort**, the lifestyle consumed all the income.*
