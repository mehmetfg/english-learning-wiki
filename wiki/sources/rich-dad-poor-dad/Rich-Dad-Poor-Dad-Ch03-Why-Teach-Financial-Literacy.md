## Part 1 The Language Nobody Teaches

When Robert was in his teenage years, he paid attention to something that most students ignored completely. He noticed that school taught many things. It taught history and science, literature and mathematics, geography and foreign languages. It taught him how to read a map, how to solve an equation, how to write an essay, how to understand the past. These were all useful things. He was grateful for them.

But school taught him nothing about money. Nothing at all. There was no class called "How Money Works." There was no lesson on reading a financial statement the document that shows how much money an individual or a company earns, spends, saves, and owes. There was no explanation of how taxes worked in real terms, how a mortgage functioned over thirty years, or what actually happened when you invested in shares. These things things that almost every adult would need to deal with every day of their lives were simply absent from the curriculum.

Robert asked his poor dad about this once. His poor dad said that these things were the responsibility of families, not schools. Parents were supposed to teach children about money. But when Robert looked at his family, he saw that his poor dad educated, intelligent, well-read did not really know these things either. He knew how to earn money. He knew how to save a little. He knew how to pay his bills. But he did not know how to make money grow. He did not know how to read a financial statement. He did not know the difference between an asset that produced income and a liability that consumed it.

Rich dad had a different view. He said: "The most important education you will ever receive is not in school. It is the education you give yourself. And the most important subject you can teach yourself is the language of money."

He called it a language deliberately. He meant that money has its own vocabulary, its own grammar, its own rules. If you do not speak this language, you cannot understand what is happening in the financial world around you. You cannot read the signs. You cannot see the opportunities. You cannot protect yourself from the traps. You are, in the most important financial sense, illiterate unable to read.

And the most dangerous thing about this illiteracy, rich dad said, is that most people do not know they have it. They think they understand money because they earn it and spend it every day. But earning and spending money is not the same as understanding money. A person can live in a country for their whole life without ever being able to read the books written in its language. And a person can handle money every day for their whole life without ever understanding how it really works.

The goal of this lesson, rich dad told Robert, was to give him the basic grammar the fundamental rules of the language of money. Not to make him an accountant or a financial expert, but to give him enough to read the world around him with open eyes.

## Part 2 Two Simple Words

Rich dad believed that the entire foundation of financial intelligence could be built on two simple words. He used these two words constantly, in almost every financial conversation. He drew them on paper, on whiteboards, on napkins, on the backs of envelopes anywhere he happened to be when the subject came up. The two words were: **assets** and **liabilities**.

Robert had first encountered these words in Chapter 1, when rich dad drew his diagram of assets putting money in your pocket and liabilities taking it out. But now, in these teenage years, rich dad went deeper. He wanted Robert to understand not just what the words meant, but how to see them in the real world how to look at any financial situation and recognise which category things belonged to.

Rich dad said: "The rich acquire assets. The poor and middle class acquire liabilities, but they think they are assets."

This sentence sounds simple. But rich dad insisted that almost nobody truly understood it. He said that of all the financial mistakes that people make and there are many this is the most common and the most costly. People work hard, earn money, and then spend it on things they believe will make them financially secure. But many of those things are not assets at all. They are liabilities dressed up as assets. They look like security. They feel like success. But mathematically, they take money out they do not put it in.

To understand this fully, you need a clear definition of each word.

An **asset** is anything that puts money into your pocket on a regular basis. Not once, not as a one-time payment, but regularly month after month, year after year. A rental property is an asset if the tenants pay you rent that is more than the cost of maintaining the property. Shares in a company are an asset if they pay you dividends regularly. A business you own is an asset if it generates profit that comes to you even when you are not there working. A book you wrote is an asset if people continue to buy it and send you royalties. The common thread in all assets is this: they produce. They generate. They work. They make money for you while you sleep, while you are on holiday, while you are doing other things.

A **liability** is anything that takes money out of your pocket on a regular basis. A car is a liability: you pay for it, and then you continue paying insurance every month, fuel every week, maintenance when it needs repairs, parking when you go to the city. These costs do not stop. A liability is the opposite of an asset. Instead of producing money, it consumes it. Instead of filling your pocket, it empties it.

Now here is the key insight that most people miss. The names of things do not tell you whether they are assets or liabilities. The behaviour of things tells you. Something is an asset or a liability based on what it does to your financial situation not based on what people call it or how it makes you feel.

This is why rich dad was so insistent about understanding cash flow the movement of money in and out of your financial life. Cash flow is the proof. If money flows in, it is an asset. If money flows out, it is a liability. Forget the names. Look at the flow.

## Part 3 Three Pictures of Money

Rich dad did not like to explain things only with words. He believed that pictures were more powerful than descriptions. "Show me the diagram," he would say, "and I will remember it. Tell me the words, and I will forget them." And so, when he taught Robert and Mike about assets and liabilities, he drew pictures. He drew three pictures three diagrams showing how money moved through the lives of three different kinds of people.

The first picture was the cash flow of a poor person.

At the top of the diagram, rich dad drew a box and wrote the word "Income." Below that, he drew another box and wrote "Expenses." Then he drew an arrow from the Income box straight down to the Expenses box all the money that came in went straight out again to pay for expenses. There was nothing left. There were no assets. There were no investments. Every dollar that arrived was already promised to a bill or a purchase. This was the financial life of a poor person: income in, expenses out, nothing remaining.

The second picture was the cash flow of a middle-class person.

This diagram was more complex. At the top was the Income box a salary, usually larger than the poor person's. Below that was a column on the left for Assets and a column on the right for Liabilities. But here was the important thing: the middle-class person had many liabilities a mortgage on a house, car payments, credit card debt, perhaps a personal loan. These liabilities created expenses monthly payments that went out regularly. And the income the salary went directly to paying these expenses. Just like the poor person's diagram, almost everything that came in went straight out. The difference was only that the middle-class person's life was more expensive, more complicated, and involved more debt. They had more income, but they also had more obligations. The amount going out grew to match the amount coming in.

Rich dad paused and pointed to the liabilities column in the middle-class diagram. He said: "Notice that the middle-class person believes they have assets. They say: 'I own a house. I have a car. I have a nice lifestyle.' But look at the diagram. These things are in the liabilities column, not the assets column. Why? Because they take money out every month. The house costs: mortgage payment, property tax, maintenance, insurance. The car costs: payment, fuel, insurance. These are not assets. These are expenses dressed up as achievements."

The third picture was the cash flow of a rich person.

This diagram was different in a fundamental way. The rich person also had an income column and an expenses column. But between them sitting in the middle was a large assets column. The rich person's income came mainly not from a salary, but from the assets. The rental properties sent in rent each month. The business investments sent in profits. The shares sent in dividends. This money from assets went into the income column. And from the income column, some of it went to expenses the ordinary costs of life but much of it went back into the assets column, buying more assets, growing the column further. The rich person's financial life was not a straight line from income to expenses. It was a cycle a growing cycle in which assets produced income, income bought more assets, and assets produced more income still.

Rich dad tapped the third diagram and said: "This is the machine. Once you build this machine, it runs. It produces. You do not have to push it every day. Your job is to build it and then to keep it running and growing. The more assets you add to the column, the stronger the machine becomes."

Robert stared at the three diagrams for a long time. He thought about his poor dad's financial life, and he could see, clearly, that it matched the second diagram middle class, not poor, but with a large mortgage and car payments and living expenses that consumed almost everything his salary produced. His poor dad was not irresponsible. He was not stupid. He worked very hard. But his financial structure was the middle-class structure income going directly to expenses, with very little building in the assets column.

Rich dad's diagram was different. And the difference, Robert could see, was not about earning more. It was about where you put what you earned.

## Part 4 Why Your House Is Not an Asset

Of all the ideas in Rich Dad Poor Dad, the one that caused the most surprise and the most disagreement was this: for most people, their house is not an asset. It is a liability.

This goes against one of the most commonly held beliefs in modern society. Almost everywhere in the world, people are told that buying a home is the best financial decision they can make. Parents tell their children. Banks advertise it. Governments create programmes to encourage it. Financial advisors recommend it. "Your home is your greatest asset" is something that millions of people believe without questioning, as naturally as they believe that the sun rises in the morning.

So when rich dad said that a house is a liability, most people's first reaction was disbelief. Or anger. Or both.

Rich dad was patient about this. He said he understood why people believed what they believed. He did not say that buying a house was always wrong, or that nobody should own a home. He said something much more precise. He said: "Let us look at what actually happens when someone buys a house, and let us follow the money."

Here is what happens. A family decides to buy a house. They go to the bank and take out a mortgage a very large loan, often the largest they will ever take in their lives. They begin making monthly payments to the bank. These payments continue for twenty-five or thirty years. During all those years, each monthly payment includes two parts: one part reduces the debt, and one part is interest money paid to the bank as the price of borrowing. In the early years of a mortgage, most of the monthly payment is interest. Very little of it actually reduces the debt. The bank earns a great deal of money from this arrangement.

The family also pays property taxes every year money to the local government simply for owning the land and building. They pay home insurance protection against fires, floods, and other disasters. They pay for maintenance the things that break down and need to be fixed. A roof has a limited life and eventually needs to be replaced. A heating system ages and fails. Pipes, windows, doors, flooring all of these things need attention over thirty years of ownership.

Add all of these costs together: mortgage payments, interest, property taxes, insurance, maintenance. The total cost of owning a home over thirty years is almost always much larger than the original purchase price. The house that seemed to cost two hundred thousand dollars may actually cost, in total, three hundred and fifty thousand or more by the time every payment is made.

But, people say: the house increases in value. This is true in many places, over long periods, property values do rise. If you bought a house for two hundred thousand dollars and sold it twenty years later for three hundred thousand, you made a hundred thousand dollars in capital gain the increase in value. This sounds like a significant profit.

But rich dad asked a simple question: during those twenty years, while the value was rising, what was the house doing to your monthly cash flow? Every month, money was going out. The mortgage payment, the taxes, the insurance, the maintenance. Every month, the house was taking money from your pocket, not putting money in. According to the cash flow definition money in versus money out the house was a liability during all of those years, even as its value was rising.

Compare this to a rental property. If you buy a property and rent it to tenants, the tenants pay you rent every month. If the rent is more than all your costs mortgage, taxes, insurance, maintenance then money comes into your pocket every month. The property is an asset. It is doing what assets do: producing income.

Rich dad's point was not that houses are bad. He owned a beautiful home himself. His point was that the family home should not be counted in the assets column when thinking about financial freedom. It sits in the liabilities column a large, expensive, long-term liability even when it is a wonderful and comfortable place to live. Confusing it with an asset is one of the main reasons why middle-class families feel financially stuck for decades. They count their home as their biggest asset, feel financially secure, and stop building real assets in the meantime.

## Part 5 The Middle-Class Trap

Rich dad devoted a great deal of time to explaining the specific financial trap that catches most middle-class people because he believed that understanding the trap was the first step toward escaping it.

The trap works like this. A person finishes their education and gets a good job. This is a success a real success. They worked hard for it, and it is the result of genuine effort and ability. The salary is good. Life feels positive. There is a sense of possibility in the air.

Then things begin to happen not because of bad choices, but because of the way life is organised and because of what society teaches people to want. The person gets married. They need a home. They buy a house. The house requires a mortgage a large debt. The mortgage payment is significant, but they earn enough to manage it. They take out the mortgage and feel good. They are homeowners. This is what adults do. This is success.

Then they need a car or perhaps a second car, because they live in a place where the public transport is not good. They take out a car loan. Another payment every month. But they can manage it. They are responsible adults with good jobs. They can handle it.

Then they have children. Children bring joy, and they also bring costs. Childcare costs money. Clothes and food cost more now that there are more people in the family. They want to send their children to a good school. They want to live in a good neighbourhood with good schools and safe streets. This means the house they bought may not be large enough, or it may not be in the right area. They decide to sell and buy a bigger house in a better area.

The bigger house means a bigger mortgage. The bigger mortgage means bigger payments. To manage these payments comfortably, they need more income. One of them works longer hours. Perhaps they take on extra responsibilities at work, hoping for a promotion. The promotion comes, and with it, a raise. The raise is welcome. But it also comes with a new tax reality the raise pushes them into a higher tax bracket, and they keep less of each additional dollar they earn than they did before.

And then something interesting happens. The raise arrives, and the lifestyle expands to meet it. The new salary feels good, but somehow life still feels the same as before still a little tight at the end of the month, still a little worried about large unexpected expenses. They buy slightly nicer things, go on a slightly better holiday, eat at slightly better restaurants. The desire that they thought the raise would satisfy simply moves to a higher level and waits for them there.

They are now solidly middle class. They earn good money. They live in a nice house. They drive a good car. Their children go to a good school. By almost every external measurement, they are successful. They have what most people want.

And yet they are trapped. They cannot stop working. The mortgage requires them to show up every month. The car payment requires them to show up every month. The school fees, the insurance, the groceries all of it requires them to keep earning. If they lost their job, the entire structure would collapse quickly. They have very little saved. Their assets column if they have one is thin, filled with the house that takes money out rather than putting it in.

Rich dad called this being "trapped by success." The lifestyle beautiful and real as it is has become a machine that requires constant feeding. And the fuel the machine runs on is the salary. Stop the salary, and the machine stops. The freedom that the salary seemed to offer has been quietly consumed by the obligations it created.

The exit from this trap, rich dad said, is not to earn more. More salary, as we have seen, simply creates more desire and more obligations. The exit is to start building the assets column slowly, carefully, one piece at a time while keeping the liabilities column from growing any faster than necessary. This is difficult in practice, because it requires saying no to things that feel good and responsible and normal. It requires thinking differently from the people around you. But it is possible. And it is the only genuine way out.

## Part 6 The Pattern of the Rich

Having shown Robert what poor and middle-class cash flow looked like, rich dad turned to the question Robert most wanted answered: what do the rich actually do? What is the pattern on the other side?

The answer, rich dad said, was both simple and difficult. Simple, because the principle could be stated in one sentence. Difficult, because following it required discipline at exactly the moments when most people lack discipline when they have money in their hands and desire in their hearts.

The principle was this: before you pay anyone else, buy an asset.

Rich dad called this "paying yourself first." He had mentioned it in passing in earlier lessons, but now he explained it fully. When money arrives from a salary, from a business, from any source the first thing you should do is set aside a portion for investment. Not a large portion necessarily. The size matters less than the habit. Even ten percent of everything you earn, set aside consistently and invested wisely, will produce remarkable results over twenty or thirty years. But the important thing is that this happens first before the mortgage payment, before the electricity bill, before the supermarket run, before anything.

Most people do the opposite. They receive their money and immediately pay their obligations. Mortgage first. Car payment next. Electricity, food, insurance, telephone. Then, if there is anything left which there usually is not they think about saving. And because there is usually nothing left, they save nothing. The assets column stays empty.

Rich dad said: "If you pay yourself last, you will always have nothing left for yourself. If you pay yourself first, you will always find a way to pay everyone else because you will have to. Necessity is a powerful motivator. When you have already put money aside, the pressure of paying your bills will push your brain to find solutions. You will work a little harder, you will find a small extra income, you will cut a small expense somewhere. But you will manage. And the assets column will grow."

He also said something important about patience. Building an assets column takes time real time, years and sometimes decades. In the beginning, the amounts seem small. You invest a hundred dollars and it grows slowly. You invest a thousand dollars and it still seems like a modest beginning. But here is what happens over time: the assets begin to generate their own income. And if you invest that income rather than spending it if you buy more assets with the money your assets produce the growth accelerates. This is the same compounding process that James Clear described with the one-percent habit: small things, repeated consistently, produce extraordinary results over time.

Rich dad had another piece of advice about what to do with the income from assets. He said: "Do not spend it on liabilities. Do not increase your lifestyle every time your asset income grows. When your rental property produces extra profit, buy another rental property. When your investments produce dividends, reinvest them. Keep the money moving into the assets column, and do not let desire redirect it into the liabilities column." This requires significant self-control. The human desire for comfort and display for a bigger house, a newer car, a more impressive lifestyle is always present. Rich dad acknowledged this. He did not say it was easy to resist. He said it was necessary.

The rich, he explained, live below their means for as long as it takes to build a meaningful asset column. Then, when the asset income is large enough to cover their living expenses comfortably, they can afford to spend more not because they earned a higher salary, but because their assets are producing enough. At that point, financial freedom has arrived. They can choose to work or not work. They can choose where they live, how they spend their time, and what they do with their days. The assets are running the machine. They are free.

## Part 7 When Prices Rise and Salaries Stay the Same

Rich dad introduced another concept that most people did not think about clearly: inflation. Inflation is the gradual increase in prices over time. It is not dramatic. It does not happen all at once. But it is constant and cumulative it builds up year after year, and over decades it can have a very significant effect on a person's financial situation.

Here is how inflation works in practice. Imagine that in 1980, a basic weekly food shop costs twenty dollars. In 1990, it costs twenty-eight dollars. In 2000, it costs thirty-eight dollars. In 2010, it costs fifty dollars. The food itself has not changed greatly. But the price has more than doubled over thirty years. The dollar of 2010 buys much less than the dollar of 1980. This erosion of the value of money over time is what inflation means.

Now, most working people receive salary increases over time pay rises that are supposed to keep up with inflation. If inflation is three percent per year, a salary rise of three percent keeps the worker in approximately the same position. They earn more dollars, but those dollars buy roughly the same amount as before. They have not moved forward financially. They have only stayed in the same place.

And sometimes, salary rises do not keep up with inflation at all. The cost of housing, of healthcare, of education for children in many countries and in many periods, these costs have risen much faster than ordinary salaries. This means that many working people are actually moving backward in real terms, even while their salaries are rising in nominal terms. A nominal figure is the number written on the paper the salary in dollars. A real figure takes inflation into account and measures actual purchasing power how much the money can actually buy.

Rich dad said: "Most people think about money in nominal terms. They see their salary going up and they feel better off. But they have not asked whether the purchasing power has actually increased. They have not asked whether the things they need to buy are more expensive than before. They have not asked whether they are actually richer, or just using bigger numbers."

He used a very specific and powerful example. He talked about the middle-class family that buys a house for two hundred thousand dollars and sells it thirty years later for four hundred thousand. They celebrate. They have doubled their money! But then rich dad asked: what was the inflation rate during those thirty years? If inflation averaged three percent per year, then something that cost two hundred thousand dollars in 1980 would cost approximately four hundred and eighty thousand dollars in 2010 just because of inflation not because of any real increase in value. The family that sold for four hundred thousand has not actually gained anything in real terms. They have, in fact, lost ground.

This is one of the reasons that simply saving money in a bank is not always the right strategy. A savings account pays interest, but if the interest rate is lower than the inflation rate which it often is the value of the savings is being slowly eaten away. The number in the account grows, but its purchasing power shrinks. The saver feels disciplined and responsible, but mathematically they are moving backward.

The answer, rich dad said, is to own assets whose value grows faster than inflation. Good rental properties in desirable areas tend to increase in value over time and also produce income. Shares in well-run companies tend to grow in value over the long term and produce dividends. A well-built business creates value that grows with the economy and often faster. These assets protect against inflation because their income and their value grow, rather than staying fixed.

This was why, rich dad said, financial education was not a luxury it was a survival skill. In a world where prices rise every year, where taxes take a portion of everything you earn, and where pensions may not keep up with the cost of living, understanding how money works is not optional. It is necessary for anyone who wants to maintain their quality of life over a lifetime.

## Part 8 The Foundation of Financial Intelligence

By the time Robert finished his teenage years, he had received an education that almost no school in the world would have provided. He had learned the language of money not perfectly, not completely, but enough to see the world differently from most of the people around him.

He had learned to read cash flow. He knew that the question to ask about any financial decision was not "Is this a good thing to have?" but "Does this put money into my pocket or take money out?" He knew that the answer to that question not the name of the thing, not the feeling it produced, not the status it suggested was the only honest answer to whether something was an asset or a liability.

He had learned why the middle class struggles despite earning good money. Not because they are irresponsible or lazy. But because they buy liabilities and call them assets. Because they measure their success by the things they own rather than by the income those things produce. Because they increase their lifestyle every time their salary rises, and so the obligations always grow to consume the new income. Because they never build a strong assets column or they build one so slowly, and redirect so much of the income from it into new liabilities, that it never becomes large enough to provide freedom.

He had learned about inflation about how the world does not stand still, and how a strategy that works in theory but ignores the rising cost of everything can actually leave a person poorer in real terms even while they feel more comfortable in nominal ones.

And from all of this, he had arrived at a conclusion that rich dad had been pointing toward from the very beginning: the foundation of financial success is not income. It is not salary. It is not even effort. The foundation is financial intelligence the ability to understand money, to read its language, to see through the names and feelings to the underlying cash flow, and to make decisions based on that clear understanding rather than on fear, desire, or the opinions of people who have never questioned the system they live inside.

Rich dad said something near the end of this chapter of Robert's education that Robert never forgot. He said: "The main reason most people struggle financially is not that they do not earn enough. The main reason is that they were never taught the rules of the game. And if you do not know the rules, you cannot win. You will always be playing, but you will always be losing often without knowing why."

He continued: "The good news is that the rules are learnable. The language of money is learnable. The difference between an asset and a liability is learnable. The cash flow patterns are learnable. None of this requires exceptional intelligence. It requires attention, patience, and the willingness to think differently from the people around you."

Robert thought about his poor dad. He thought about how hard his father worked, how many books he had read, how much he knew about education and history and literature and science. His poor dad was genuinely one of the most intelligent people Robert knew. And yet, in this specific area in the language of money his poor dad was not literate. It was not his fault. Nobody had taught him. The school had not taught him. His parents had not taught him. The culture he grew up in had given him certain beliefs about money work hard, save carefully, buy a house, trust your pension and he had followed those beliefs faithfully, without ever realising that they were beliefs, not facts. Without ever realising that there was another language being spoken by a small group of people who saw the world of money very differently.

Robert made a decision. He would learn that language. He would study it the way a person learns a foreign language slowly at first, making mistakes, getting confused, sometimes feeling lost. But continuously. Without stopping. Until the language became natural, and the world of money opened up in a way that it had never opened up for his poor dad, despite all his father's brilliance.

Financial literacy, Robert understood, was not a gift. It was a skill. And skills could be learned by anyone willing to put in the time and the attention. That was the whole point of the lesson. That was why it had to be taught.

## Chapter Summary

**What you learned in Chapter 3:**

Chapter 3 introduces the second great lesson of Rich Dad Poor Dad: financial literacy the ability to read and understand the language of money. Rich dad argues that the most important education most people never receive is not school-based learning, but the ability to understand cash flow: the movement of money in and out of their lives.

The chapter builds on the assets and liabilities distinction introduced in Chapter 1, deepening it through three cash flow diagrams. The poor person's diagram shows income flowing directly to expenses, with nothing remaining. The middle-class diagram shows more income but also more liabilities a mortgage, car payments, consumer debt which create large monthly expenses that consume the salary just as completely. The rich person's diagram shows a different structure entirely: income flowing mainly from assets, and a portion of that income continuously reinvested to grow the assets column further.

The most controversial insight in the chapter is that a family home, for most people, is a liability and not an asset. Because it takes money out every month mortgage, taxes, insurance, maintenance it belongs in the liabilities column, regardless of whether its market value is rising. Rich dad distinguishes carefully between capital gains (the house becoming worth more over time) and cash flow (the monthly reality of money going out). An asset must produce regular income. A house you live in does not.

The chapter also introduces inflation as an invisible threat to financial wellbeing, explaining why saving money in a bank at low interest rates can actually leave a person poorer in real terms. The solution is to own assets whose income and value grow faster than inflation.

The chapter ends with a reflection: financial intelligence is not about exceptional talent. It is a learnable skill a language that anyone can study if they are willing to question what they have been taught and think differently from the people around them.

**Key ideas from Chapter 3:**
- Financial literacy = the ability to read the language of money (cash flow)
- Two words: assets (money in) vs. liabilities (money out)
- Three cash flow diagrams: poor, middle class, and rich each shows a completely different structure
- For most people, a home is a liability, not an asset it takes money out every month
- The middle-class trap: buying liabilities and calling them assets; lifestyle rising to consume every salary increase
- Pay yourself first: invest before paying obligations, to force the brain to find solutions
- Inflation silently reduces purchasing power assets whose value grows are the protection
- Financial intelligence is a skill, not a gift learnable by anyone willing to study it

## 📐 Grammar Notes Chapter 3

### 1. Present Simple Universal Truths and Definitions
Chapter 3, like the previous chapters, uses present simple to state facts about how money works. These are not descriptions of one moment they are general truths.

- *"An asset is anything that puts money into your pocket."*
- *"A liability is anything that takes money out of your pocket."*
- *"Inflation reduces the purchasing power of money over time."*
- *"The rich buy assets before they buy anything else."*

Present simple = this is how things work, always.

---

### 2. Relative Clauses Who, Which, That
Chapter 3 uses relative clauses to add information about nouns to define or describe them more precisely.

**Defining relative clauses (no commas essential information):**
- *"An asset is something **that** produces income."*
- *"People **who** buy liabilities and call them assets often feel financially stuck."*
- *"A salary **that** rises slower than inflation loses real value."*

**Non-defining relative clauses (with commas extra information):**
- *"His poor dad, **who** was extremely intelligent, had never learned the language of money."*
- *"Inflation, **which** is gradual and often invisible, can have large effects over time."*

---

### 3. Comparative Structures More Than, Less Than, As Much As
The chapter constantly compares two financial realities: working for money vs. making money work for you.

- *"The cost of living has risen **more than** salaries in many countries."*
- *"A rental property produces **more** income **than** a savings account."*
- *"Most people keep **far less** of what they earn **than** they realise."*
- *"The assets column must grow **faster than** the liabilities column."*

---

### 4. Passive Voice When the Actor Is Unknown or Unimportant
The passive voice appears in Chapter 3 when describing systems and rules that have no single author things that simply exist or are generally done.

- *"People **are told** that buying a home is the wisest financial decision."*
- *"Money **is taken** by the government before it arrives in your account."*
- *"Financial literacy **was never taught** in most schools."*
- *"Assets **are defined** by what they do, not by what they are called."*

Pattern: **Subject + is/are/was/were + past participle (+ by + agent, optional)**

---

### 5. Conditionals Type 1 and Type 2
Both first and second conditionals appear in this chapter.

**Type 1 (real possibility if + present simple, will):**
- *"If you pay yourself first, you will always find a way to pay your bills."*
- *"If the rent covers all costs, the property will put money in your pocket."*

**Type 2 (unreal or hypothetical if + past simple, would):**
- *"If people understood the difference between assets and liabilities, they would make very different decisions."*
- *"If you owned no liabilities, you would need much less income to live comfortably."*

The difference: Type 1 = a real plan. Type 2 = imagining a situation that does not currently exist.
