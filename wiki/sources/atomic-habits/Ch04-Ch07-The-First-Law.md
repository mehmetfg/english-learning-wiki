# Atomic Habits
## Chapters 4–7 The First Law: Make It Obvious
### Extended A2/B1 Reader

---

*The First Law of Behavior Change is: Make It Obvious. These four chapters explain how cues work the signals that start our habits. You will learn how to make good habits easier to start and how to stop bad habits before they begin. The key idea: you do not need more willpower. You need better design.*

---

## Part 1 The Invisible Habit

There is a training exercise used in Japan called "pointing and calling." Train operators the people who drive trains point their fingers at each important signal and say what it means out loud. When the driver comes to a red light, he points at it and says: "Signal red." When he checks the speed, he points at the dial and says: "Speed normal." He does this for every important action throughout the journey.

This might sound strange. Why would a professional need to say everything out loud? Because the human brain is very good at making things automatic. When we do something many times, it becomes a habit and habits happen below the level of conscious thought. After years of driving a train, a driver might stop a red light without even being aware of it. The action feels automatic. And automatic things feel safe but they are also easy to miss.

Pointing and calling works because it raises the level of consciousness. It forces the person to notice what they are doing. Studies in Japan showed that this method reduced errors in the train system by eighty-five percent. Eighty-five percent. Simply by pointing and saying what you see, the number of mistakes fell dramatically.

James Clear connects this idea to our habits. Most of us are not aware of our habits. We eat while we watch television, and we do not notice how much we have eaten. We pick up our phone, and we do not remember deciding to pick it up. We follow the same path to work every morning and barely notice the journey.

To change your habits, you must first see them. And the best way to see them is to write them down. James Clear suggests a tool called the Habit Scorecard. You write a list of everything you do in a typical day from the moment you wake up to the moment you fall asleep. Then, next to each item, you write: + for a good habit, – for a bad one, and = for a neutral one. You do not need to change anything at first. You only need to look. The act of seeing your habits clearly is the first step to changing them.

## Part 2 When and Where

Most people try to build a new habit by relying on motivation. They wait until they feel ready. They wait until they have energy. They wait for the right moment. But the right moment almost never comes. And so the habit never starts.

James Clear introduces a simple but very effective tool: the implementation intention. This is a specific plan for when and where you will do your new habit. It sounds simple. But the research is clear: people who make this kind of plan are much more likely to follow through.

The format is straightforward. You write a sentence like this: "I will [BEHAVIOUR] at [TIME] in [LOCATION]." For example: "I will read for twenty minutes at eight o'clock in my bedroom." Or: "I will do ten push-ups at seven in the morning in my living room." Or: "I will write in my journal at lunchtime at my desk."

Why does this work? Because vague intentions fail. "I will exercise more" is a wish, not a plan. It has no time. It has no place. When the day comes and you feel tired or busy, there is nothing to hold you to it. But "I will walk for thirty minutes at six-thirty in the morning from my front door" is a plan. Your brain knows exactly what to do and when. When six-thirty arrives, the decision has already been made.

The researchers who studied this found something interesting. People often think they fail because of lack of motivation or laziness. But in many cases, they fail simply because they never decided when and where. The habit had no home in their day. Once they gave it a specific time and place, their success rate went up significantly without any increase in motivation at all.

This is a powerful idea. You do not need to want the habit more. You do not need to feel more motivated. You only need to make a plan one sentence, with a time and a place. And suddenly, the habit has a home.

## Part 3 Stack Your Habits

Once you understand implementation intentions, you can use a more advanced version of the same idea: habit stacking.

Habit stacking is based on a simple observation. You already have many habits. You wake up, and you brush your teeth. You sit down at your desk, and you check your email. You pour a cup of coffee, and you look at your phone. These existing habits are already strongly connected in your brain. You do not need to decide to do them. They just happen, one after another.

Habit stacking uses these existing connections as a platform for new habits. The formula is: "After I [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]." For example: "After I pour my morning coffee, I will sit and write in my journal for five minutes." Or: "After I sit down at my desk, I will identify the three most important things I need to do today." Or: "After I get into bed at night, I will read for ten minutes before turning off the light."

The power of this approach is that you do not need to create a new time or a new place. You attach the new habit to something that already exists. The old habit becomes the cue for the new one. The chain of behaviours grows naturally.

You can build long chains using this method. For example: "After I wake up, I will make my bed. After I make my bed, I will do five minutes of stretching. After I stretch, I will drink one glass of water. After I drink the water, I will sit down and meditate for five minutes." Each habit triggers the next. The morning routine builds itself.

The key is to choose the right anchor habit the existing habit that comes just before your new one. The anchor should be a strong, reliable habit that you do almost every day. The stronger the anchor, the stronger the chain.

## Part 4 Design Your World

Chapter 6 introduces one of the most important ideas in the whole book: your environment shapes your behaviour more than your willpower does.

Think about the last time you ate something you did not plan to eat. Perhaps you walked into the kitchen and there was a bowl of chocolates on the table. You took one. Then another. You were not hungry. You did not decide to eat chocolates. But they were there, and you ate them. This is not weakness. This is how humans work.

We are all more sensitive to visual cues than we think. When you see food, you want food. When you see your running shoes by the door, you are more likely to go for a run. When your guitar is on a stand in your bedroom, you are more likely to pick it up and play. When your phone is on your desk, you are more likely to pick it up and look at it. The things you can see become the things you do.

This is why designing your environment is more powerful than relying on willpower. You do not need to have more self-control. You need to make the things you want easier to see and harder to ignore and make the things you do not want harder to see and easier to avoid.

Anne Thorndike, a doctor in Boston, used this idea to change what patients in a hospital cafeteria ate. She did not give them health advice. She did not teach them about nutrition. She just rearranged the cafeteria. She put water bottles next to the cash register, where they had always put fizzy drinks. She put fruit at the front of every display, instead of at the back. She made the salads easier to find and the chips harder. Within three months, sales of water had gone up by twenty-five percent. Sales of fizzy drinks had fallen by eleven percent. Nobody was told what to eat. The environment changed and behaviour changed with it.

The lesson is simple: you are not lazy. You are not weak. You are a person who responds to your environment like every other person on earth. So design your environment for the person you want to be.

## Part 5 The Easiest Way to Beat Bad Habits

Chapter 7 makes a surprising claim: people who have good self-control do not have more willpower than other people. They have simply arranged their lives so that they need willpower less often.

If you put someone with perfect self-control in an environment full of temptations, they will eventually give in because nobody can resist forever. The people we think of as "disciplined" are often just people who have designed their environment to remove temptations. They do not fight their bad habits every day. They simply avoid the situations where the habit would begin.

The key insight is this: you can never truly get rid of a bad habit. You can only change it or make it harder to do. A bad habit stays in your brain forever the neural pathway that created it does not disappear. What you can do is reduce your exposure to the cue. If you do not see the trigger, the craving never starts. And if the craving never starts, the response never happens.

This means that self-control is a short-term solution, not a long-term one. If you want to stop watching too much television, you could use willpower and simply decide to watch less. But this is exhausting. A better strategy is to unplug the television after each use, or to put the remote control in a difficult place to reach. Now you need to make an active effort to watch and the passive behaviour that used to happen automatically now requires a decision.

The most disciplined person is not the one who fights temptation the most. The most disciplined person is the one who has removed the most temptations from their life. They do not need to be strong, because they have removed the need to be strong. This is the real secret of self-control not resistance, but design.

## Chapter Summary

**What you learned in Chapters 4–7:**

Chapter 4 introduces the Habit Scorecard writing down all your habits so you can see them clearly. Pointing and calling shows that awareness alone reduces errors dramatically. Chapter 5 explains implementation intentions (I will [behaviour] at [time] in [location]) and habit stacking (After I [habit], I will [habit]). These tools give your habits a home in your day.

Chapter 6 shows that environment is more powerful than willpower. Visual cues drive behaviour what you see, you do. Dr. Thorndike's cafeteria experiment proves that changing the environment changes behaviour without any change in motivation. Chapter 7 reveals that disciplined people do not fight temptation they avoid it. Self-control is a short-term solution. Design is a long-term one.

**Key ideas:**
- Write your habits down awareness is the first step
- "I will [habit] at [time] in [location]" give your habit a home
- "After I [habit], I will [habit]" build chains
- Design your environment: put good things in sight, bad things out of sight
- Remove temptations don't fight them

## 📐 Grammar Notes Chapters 4–7

### 1. Future with "going to" Plans and Intentions
When describing implementation intentions, James Clear uses "going to" to express a concrete plan.

- *"I am going to read at eight o'clock in my bedroom."*
- *"She was going to rearrange the cafeteria not give lectures."*

Pattern: **Subject + am/is/are + going to + base verb**

---

### 2. "When" + Present Simple → Future result
This structure describes what happens when a habit cue appears a near-certain future event.

- *"When six-thirty arrives, the decision has already been made."*
- *"When you see food, you want food."*
- *"When your guitar is on a stand, you are more likely to pick it up."*

Pattern: **When + present simple, present simple** (for general/habitual truth)

---

### 3. Comparative Structures "more likely to"
This phrase appears constantly in the habit science sections.

- *"People who make a plan are much more likely to follow through."*
- *"When your shoes are by the door, you are more likely to go for a run."*
- *"A better strategy is to unplug the television."*

Pattern: **more likely to + base verb** / **more + adjective + than**

---

### 4. Passive Voice Describing Results
When describing research results or what happens in the environment, the passive is used.

- *"Sales of water had gone up by twenty-five percent."*
- *"Errors were reduced by eighty-five percent."*
- *"The environment was rearranged and behaviour changed."*

Pattern: **Subject + was/were + past participle**
