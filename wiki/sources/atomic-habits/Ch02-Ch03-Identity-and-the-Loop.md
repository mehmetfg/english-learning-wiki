# Atomic Habits
## Chapters 2–3 Identity and the Habit Loop
### Extended A2/B1 Reader

---

*These two chapters explain why most people fail to build lasting habits and what to do differently. Chapter 2 is about identity: who you believe you are. Chapter 3 is about the loop: the four steps that make a habit work. Together, they give you a new way to think about change.*

---

## Part 1 Three Layers of Behavior Change

When people want to change, they usually think about what they want to achieve. "I want to lose weight." "I want to read more books." "I want to run a marathon." These are all results things you can measure and see. This is the most common way to think about change. And it is also the shallowest.

James Clear says there are three different layers of behavior change. Think of them as three circles, one inside the other. The outermost circle is Outcomes what you achieve. The middle circle is Processes what you do, your daily habits and systems. The innermost circle the core is Identity. Identity is who you believe you are.

Most people start from the outside and try to work their way in. They begin with the result they want: "I want to be thin." Then they think about processes: "I should eat less and exercise more." But they never reach the centre. They never change how they see themselves. And this is exactly why their changes do not last.

The problem with starting from outcomes is that your motivation runs out. You work hard for a few weeks. You reach a small result. And then something difficult happens a bad day, a missed workout, a piece of cake and you go back to your old way. Because deep inside, you still believe the same things about yourself. You still think: "I am not an athletic person. I am not a reader. I am not a disciplined person." And your behaviour follows your beliefs.

James Clear's solution is to start from the inside. Start with identity. Ask yourself: "What kind of person do I want to be?" Not "What do I want to achieve?" but "Who do I want to become?" If you want to run every day, the question is not "How do I run more?" The question is: "What does a healthy, active person do?" And then you start doing those things not to reach a goal, but to become that person.

This seems like a small change in thinking, but it makes a very big difference. When your identity changes, your behaviour changes with it. Not as a struggle, but naturally the way water flows downward, without effort.

## Part 2 Becoming a New Person

So how do you change your identity? This is the most important question in the chapter. James Clear gives a simple but powerful answer: you change your identity by changing what you do. Not by deciding. Not by wishing. By acting.

He explains this with a beautiful idea. Every single action you take is a vote. When you act in a certain way, you are casting a vote for the kind of person you believe you are. If you go for a run today, you are casting one vote for "I am a person who exercises." If you sit down and read for twenty minutes, you are casting one vote for "I am a reader." If you choose the salad instead of the chips, you are casting one vote for "I am a person who eats well."

One vote does not win an election. One run does not make you a runner. One salad does not make you a healthy eater. But votes add up. Each small action adds another vote. And after many days, many weeks, many months the evidence begins to be impossible to ignore.

This is what James Clear calls "proving your identity to yourself through small wins." You do not need to be perfect. You do not need to change everything at once. You only need to begin casting votes in the direction of who you want to become. The identity comes from the evidence. And the evidence comes from the actions.

Here is an important example. Imagine two people who are trying to stop smoking. Someone offers both of them a cigarette. The first person says: "No, thank you. I am trying to quit." The second person says: "No, thank you. I am not a smoker." Do you hear the difference? The first person is fighting against their old identity. They still see themselves as a smoker who is trying to stop. The second person has already changed their identity. They are not a smoker anymore. The behaviour follows naturally.

This is the real goal of habit change: not to achieve something, but to become someone. The habits are not the destination. The habits are the way you prove to yourself and to the world who you truly are.

## Part 3 The Problem and the Solution

Chapter 3 introduces the habit loop the four steps that every habit follows. Once you understand these four steps, you can understand any habit. You can also use them to build new habits and to break old ones.

The four steps are: Cue, Craving, Response, and Reward. A cue is a signal something that triggers your brain to begin a habit. A craving is the desire or motivation that the cue creates. A response is the actual action you take the habit itself. And a reward is what you get from completing the habit, the good feeling that tells your brain: "that was worth doing."

James Clear divides the four steps into two phases. The first phase is the Problem Phase. This contains the cue and the craving. You notice something (the cue), and you want something because of it (the craving). For example: you are sitting at your desk working. You notice that you feel bored (cue). You begin to want some kind of stimulation or distraction (craving). Your brain recognises this as a problem a feeling of dissatisfaction that it wants to solve.

The second phase is the Solution Phase. This contains the response and the reward. You do something to solve the problem (response), and then you feel satisfied (reward). For example: you pick up your phone and scroll through social media (response). You feel a little better for a moment, less bored (reward). Your brain records this result: "this worked. Do it again next time you feel bored."

This is exactly how habits form. Your brain is always looking for patterns. When it finds an action that reliably solves a problem that turns a bad feeling into a good feeling it remembers. It stores this connection. And the next time the same cue appears, the brain starts running the same habit almost automatically, before you are even aware of it.

This is why habits can be so hard to change. By the time you notice you are doing something, your brain has already started. The habit began at the cue level in a signal so small and so quick that you did not notice it. Understanding this is the first step to taking control.

## Part 4 The Habit Loop in Practice

Now that you know the four steps, you can use them. James Clear introduces what he calls "Four Laws of Behavior Change" one law for each step of the habit loop. These four laws are the structure of the rest of the book.

To build a good habit, you need to:
make the cue **obvious**, make the craving **attractive**, make the response **easy**, and make the reward **satisfying**. These four words obvious, attractive, easy, satisfying will appear again and again throughout the book. They are the recipe for any new habit.

To break a bad habit, you do the opposite. You make the cue **invisible** so you never see the trigger. You make the craving **unattractive** so you do not desire it. You make the response **difficult** so it takes more effort. And you make the reward **unsatisfying** so your brain does not want to repeat it.

Think about a simple example. Suppose you want to eat less sugar. The cue might be: you see a packet of biscuits on your kitchen table. The craving: you want something sweet. The response: you eat a biscuit. The reward: a moment of pleasure. To break this habit, you first remove the cue you put the biscuits in a cupboard where you cannot see them. Without the visual cue, the craving is weaker. Without the craving, the response does not happen. And the reward never comes.

Now suppose you want to build a new habit eating a piece of fruit every morning. You make the cue obvious: you put a bowl of fruit on the kitchen table where you will see it first thing in the morning. You make it attractive: you choose fruit you actually enjoy, not fruit you think you should eat. You make it easy: you wash and cut the fruit the night before, so it is ready to eat with no effort. And you make it satisfying: you eat it with your morning coffee something you already enjoy so the experience becomes pleasant.

The four laws are not complicated. But they are powerful. And the rest of the book explains each one in detail, with stories, science, and specific strategies that you can use today.

## Chapter Summary

**What you learned in Chapters 2–3:**

Chapter 2 introduces the three layers of behavior change: outcomes, processes, and identity. Most people focus on outcomes what they want to achieve. James Clear argues that the most powerful change starts with identity who you want to become. Every action is a vote for your identity. Small wins build evidence. Evidence builds belief. Belief changes behavior permanently.

Chapter 3 explains the four-step habit loop: cue → craving → response → reward. The first two steps form the Problem Phase (you notice something and you want something). The last two form the Solution Phase (you do something and you feel satisfied). Habits form because the brain records what works. Understanding this loop allows you to design new habits intentionally and dismantle old ones.

**Key ideas:**
- Change your identity, not just your behaviour
- Every action is a vote for who you are
- Cue → Craving → Response → Reward
- 4 Laws: Make it obvious / attractive / easy / satisfying (good habits)
- 4 Inverse Laws: invisible / unattractive / difficult / unsatisfying (bad habits)

## 📐 Grammar Notes Chapters 2–3

### 1. Gerunds as Subjects
In this part of the book, abstract ideas are often expressed as actions. James Clear uses gerunds (verb + -ing) as the subject of sentences to describe processes.

- *"Changing your identity is the most powerful form of change."*
- *"Proving your identity to yourself through small wins is how habits form."*
- *"Starting from the outside and trying to work inward is the wrong approach."*

Pattern: **[Verb + -ing] + is/are + [description]**

---

### 2. Zero Conditional General Truths About Habits
The zero conditional (If + present simple → present simple) expresses facts that are always true. James Clear uses it to explain the mechanics of habits.

- *"If you act in a certain way, you cast a vote for that identity."*
- *"If the brain finds an action that reliably solves a problem, it remembers."*
- *"If the cue appears, the brain starts the habit."*

Pattern: **If + subject + present simple, subject + present simple**

---

### 3. Noun Clauses with "that"
James Clear often explains what someone believes or proves using "that" clauses.

- *"You prove to yourself that you are the kind of person who exercises."*
- *"Your brain records that this action worked."*
- *"The evidence shows that small actions add up over time."*

Pattern: **[verb of thinking/knowing/showing] + that + [clause]**

---

### 4. Imperatives for Instructions
When giving advice or explaining the four laws, the text uses imperative sentences the verb comes first, with no subject.

- *"Make the cue obvious."*
- *"Make the reward satisfying."*
- *"Put the fruit on the table. Wash it the night before. Choose something you enjoy."*

Pattern: **[Base verb] + [object/complement]**
