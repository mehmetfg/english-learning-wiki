# Don Quixote
## Chapter 2: The Windmills

---

### Before You Read

**The story so far:** Alonso Quixano, a thin gentleman from La Mancha, has read too many books about knights. He has gone mad. He believes he is a knight-errant named Don Quixote of La Mancha. He went on his first adventure, was beaten on the road, and came home hurt. While he slept, his friends burned his books. But Don Quixote did not stop. He found a neighbor, a simple farmer named Sancho Panza, and convinced him to come as his squire. He promised Sancho the governorship of an island one day.

**Key words for this chapter:**
- **squire** a knight's helper and companion who travels with him
- **windmill** a tall building with long turning arms, used to grind grain into flour
- **giant** a very large and powerful imaginary creature, much bigger than a person
- **enchanter** a person who uses magic to change things or trick people
- **lance** a long, sharp weapon that a knight carries on horseback
- **donkey** a small, sturdy animal that people ride or use to carry things
- **adventure** an exciting journey with unexpected events
- **monastery** a building where monks live and pray

---

### Part One: Two Men and Two Animals

It was a cool morning two weeks after Don Quixote came home from his first adventure. The village was still dark and quiet. The stars were still visible in the sky. The only sound was a dog barking somewhere in the distance.

Don Quixote was up before the sun. He dressed carefully. He put on his armor piece by piece. He fastened his sword. He picked up his lance. He went to the stable and saddled Rocinante. The old horse stood quietly and waited with the patience of old age.

Then Don Quixote went to the house next door. He knocked softly. After a moment, a sleepy and confused face appeared at the door.

It was Sancho Panza.

Sancho was a short, round man. He had a large stomach and small, quick eyes. His face was kind and a little simple. He was not a man who thought quickly or deeply. But he was honest, and he was good-natured, and he worked hard. He was a farmer, and the only exciting things that had ever happened to him were small things, the things of a quiet country life.

"It is time," said Don Quixote simply.

Sancho Panza blinked and rubbed his eyes. Then he remembered. Two weeks ago, this strange neighbor had come to his house in the night. He had talked for a long time about knights and adventures and islands. And Sancho had agreed to go. He had agreed because of the island. He, Sancho Panza, governor of an island. The idea still seemed impossible and also wonderful.

"I am ready," said Sancho.

He had packed some things into a bag. He had provisions, some bread and cheese and a skin of wine. He had a few other useful items. He had told his wife, Teresa, that he was going on a journey. Teresa had not been happy. She had asked many questions. Sancho had answered poorly and left before breakfast.

Sancho had a donkey. He loved his donkey very much. The donkey was gray and small and had a good character. Sancho put his bag on the donkey's back and climbed up himself.

Don Quixote looked at the donkey for a moment. In his books, squires always rode horses, not donkeys. But he decided not to say anything. The most important thing was to begin the adventure. Small details could be corrected later.

The two of them set out together into the dark morning.

---

### Part Two: Talking on the Road

They rode side by side. Don Quixote sat tall and straight on Rocinante. He held his lance upright beside him. He looked forward with great seriousness. Sancho sat comfortably on his donkey. He swayed a little from side to side as the donkey walked.

For the first part of the journey, they did not talk. Don Quixote was deep in thought, imagining the adventures ahead. Sancho was thinking about breakfast, which he had not had.

Then Sancho spoke.

"Sir," he said, "I have been thinking."

"What have you been thinking?" asked Don Quixote.

"I have been thinking about the island," said Sancho. "The island you will give me when we win it. I have been thinking about my wife Teresa. She is not a woman for fine things, to tell you the truth. She is very practical. But if I become a governor, she will have to become a fine lady, won't she? And my children, too. They will be noble."

Don Quixote smiled. He liked to hear Sancho talk about the island. It showed that Sancho was motivated. A squire needed to be motivated.

"All in good time, Sancho," said Don Quixote. "First we must have many adventures. We must prove ourselves brave and worthy. When we have done enough great deeds, kingdoms and islands will fall into our hands as easily as ripe fruit falls from a tree."

Sancho thought about this. "And while we are having these adventures," he said carefully, "do I get paid? I mean, apart from the island at the end. Is there a weekly wage? Or a monthly payment? Because I have a family to think about, you see. Teresa needs money to run the house. And the children eat quite a lot."

Don Quixote looked a little uncomfortable. In his books, squires did not talk about wages. They served their knights for honor and for the promise of future rewards. Nobody ever mentioned money in such a flat and practical way.

"In all the great books about knights and squires," said Don Quixote carefully, "I have never read of a squire who was paid a regular wage. Squires serve for glory and for the love of adventure. And, of course, for the great rewards that come at the end."

Sancho was quiet for a moment. He had not read any of the great books about knights and squires. He could not read at all, in fact. But he was a man of common sense, and something about this arrangement seemed unsteady to him.

"How long before the end?" he asked.

"Adventures take the time they take," said Don Quixote firmly, but kindly. "Have faith, Sancho. You will not regret this journey. I promise you that."

Sancho decided to accept this for now. He reached into his bag and took out some bread and cheese. He ate as his donkey walked. He offered some to Don Quixote, who accepted a little. They continued along the road, eating quietly.

The sun was fully up now. The plains of La Mancha spread out around them in every direction. The land was flat and dry. There were fields of grain and areas of brown grass. The sky was pale blue with a few thin clouds. The road stretched ahead, long and straight and dusty.

---

### Part Three: Sancho Asks Many Questions

After a while, Sancho had more questions. Sancho often had questions. His mind worked in simple, direct ways, and when something was unclear, he asked about it.

"Sir Don Quixote," he said, "when we fight enemies, is there a rule about who I fight? Do I fight the servants while you fight the lords? Or do we fight everyone together? I want to make sure I understand my duties."

"A squire normally fights the servants," said Don Quixote. "But in urgent situations, you may help me in any way that is needed. The main rule is this: you must not attack any person of noble birth. That is my responsibility as a knight."

Sancho nodded. "And what if I am frightened?" he asked honestly. "I am not a coward, you understand. But some situations might be frightening. Is there a rule about that?"

Don Quixote thought for a moment. "Fear is natural," he said. "Even great knights feel fear. The important thing is to act bravely even when you are afraid. That is the definition of courage. You do what must be done even though your legs are shaking."

Sancho considered this. His legs were not shaking at the moment. They were just sitting on either side of his donkey. But he appreciated the advice.

"And if I die?" said Sancho. He asked this very simply, as if it were a practical question about farming. "What happens to my wife and children if I die in one of these adventures?"

Don Quixote was a little surprised by the directness of the question. But he answered it.

"Your family will be well provided for," he said. "A knight never forgets the family of a faithful squire who died in his service. It is a matter of honor."

Sancho seemed satisfied with this. He put another piece of cheese in his mouth and swallowed it.

"One more question," said Sancho. "How do I know when something is a real adventure? I mean, how do I know the difference between an enemy giant and an ordinary big person? Or between an enchanted castle and an ordinary inn? You seem to know these things immediately. I am not sure I would."

Don Quixote smiled. This was actually a question he had thought about.

"Experience," he said. "After a few adventures, you will begin to see the world as a knight sees it. The important thing is to trust your knight's judgment. When I tell you something is a giant or an enchanted castle, you must believe me. Doubt is the enemy of adventure."

Sancho nodded slowly. He was not entirely sure about this. He had very good eyes, and he was fairly confident that a big person and a giant would look different. But he decided not to argue. He was new to this business. Perhaps things would become clearer with experience.

---

### Part Four: The Plains of Montiel

They had been riding for several hours. The road passed through the plains of Montiel, a wide and open region of La Mancha. There was nothing to see in any direction except flat land and sky.

Sancho was getting a little bored. Don Quixote was quiet. He was deep in his imagined world, thinking about the great deeds he would perform.

Then Sancho saw something.

In the distance, there were shapes on the plain. Tall shapes. He counted them. There were thirty or forty of them, standing in a group across the fields. They were tall and had long arms that moved slowly in the morning breeze.

"Sir," said Sancho, pointing. "What are those things over there?"

Don Quixote looked up. He stared into the distance. He saw the tall shapes. He saw the long turning arms. He saw the movement in the wind.

His eyes lit up.

"Fortune is guiding us even better than we could wish," said Don Quixote. His voice was full of excitement. "Look there, Sancho. Do you see them? Thirty or forty enormous giants? I am going to do battle with them. I will kill every one of them. The spoils from this victory will make us very rich. This is a righteous war, for it is a service to God to sweep such evil creatures from the face of the earth."

Sancho stared at the same distant shapes. He looked very carefully.

"What giants?" he said.

"Those," said Don Quixote, pointing clearly. "With the long arms. Some of them have arms nearly two leagues long."

Sancho kept staring. He was genuinely confused. He was a man of the land. He knew this region. He had seen these things before.

"Excuse me, sir," said Sancho carefully. "But those are not giants. Those are windmills. And the things that look like arms are the sails of the windmills. They turn in the wind to grind grain into flour. They are completely ordinary."

Don Quixote made a sound of impatience. He shook his head. He could not believe that Sancho was making this mistake.

"You do not know much about adventures," said Don Quixote. "Those are giants. I can clearly see them. Perhaps you need more experience to see these things correctly. In any case, if you are frightened, you may move away and say your prayers. I will fight them alone."

Sancho tried once more.

"Sir Don Quixote, please look again. Those are windmills. I am absolutely certain. I can see the wooden structures. I can see the "

"Enough," said Don Quixote. His voice was not unkind, but it was firm. He had already lowered his lance. He had already turned Rocinante toward the windmills. "Charge them I will, though you had ten times as many arms to fight with!"

---

### Part Five: The Charge

Don Quixote kicked Rocinante's sides. The old horse, surprised by the sudden urgency, broke into a slow run. It was not a fast run. Rocinante was not a fast horse. But it was a determined one.

Don Quixote rode toward the windmills at the best speed Rocinante could manage. He held his lance straight out in front of him. His shield was up. He was shouting.

"Do not try to run from me, cowards and vile creatures! It is only one knight who attacks you!"

A breeze came across the plain. The windmill arms began to turn a little faster, moving in slow, heavy circles.

"Now I see those arms moving," said Don Quixote to himself, with great satisfaction. "They are waving at me. They are threatening me. Good. Then I will deal with them all the more harshly."

He rode straight at the nearest windmill.

He hit it with his lance.

At that exact moment, the wind picked up. The long arm of the windmill caught the lance. The wood splintered. The arm continued its heavy turning. It caught Don Quixote himself. It lifted him and his horse completely off the ground and threw them across the field.

Don Quixote and Rocinante hit the ground hard. They rolled. The armor crashed and rattled. Rocinante made a shocked sound and struggled to his feet. Don Quixote lay still on the ground for a moment.

Sancho Panza had watched all of this from a safe distance. He kicked his donkey into a trot and came to his fallen master.

"Oh dear," said Sancho, climbing down from his donkey and kneeling beside Don Quixote. "I told you they were windmills. I said exactly that. They are windmills. I used the word windmills."

Don Quixote groaned. He was not seriously hurt, but he ached in many places. He had been thrown quite far. He lay on his back and looked up at the sky.

"Help me up, Sancho," he said.

Sancho helped him sit up, then helped him stand. Don Quixote's armor was dented. His lance was completely broken. The pieces lay scattered on the ground.

Don Quixote looked at the broken lance and was sad for a moment. Then his expression became firm and determined.

"The fortune of war," he said. "These things happen. A knight does not complain."

Sancho picked up the largest pieces of the broken lance. "What do we do now?" he asked. "Your lance is broken."

"A real knight does not need a lance," said Don Quixote. He thought for a moment. "Do you see that tree over there?" He pointed to a young oak tree near the road. "I will cut a branch from that tree. I will attach the spear tip to it. By tomorrow, I will have a new lance."

Sancho looked at the oak tree. It was not a very impressive tree. But he said nothing.

"And about the windmills," continued Don Quixote, "I understand now what happened. That wicked enchanter, Frestón, the one who stole my library and books, he was behind this."

Sancho looked confused. "Frestón?"

"He is my great enemy," said Don Quixote. "A powerful enchanter who has hated me for many years. He knew I was coming to fight those giants. So he changed the giants into windmills at the very last moment, just to take away the glory of my victory. That is exactly the kind of thing Frestón does."

Sancho thought about this explanation. It seemed very convenient. But he was Sancho Panza, a simple farmer, and this was only his second day as a squire. He did not feel qualified to argue about enchanters and their habits.

"I see," said Sancho. "And will this Frestón be a problem often?"

"Constantly," said Don Quixote cheerfully. "But do not worry. My strength and my courage will overcome him in the end. Good always defeats evil. That is the way of things."

Sancho nodded. He helped Don Quixote back onto Rocinante. He climbed back onto his donkey. They continued down the road.

---

### Part Six: That Evening

They rode until the sun was going down. Don Quixote rode in silence, thinking. His shoulder hurt from the fall, but he had said nothing about it. Knights did not complain about injuries. That was the rule.

Sancho rode behind him on the donkey, eating the last of his cheese. He was tired and his back was a little sore from the long day's riding. He was also thinking.

"Sir," said Sancho eventually. "Where are we going to sleep tonight? And is there going to be supper?"

Don Quixote looked around. They were in open country. There was no village visible in any direction. The sky was turning red and orange.

"Tonight, we will sleep under the stars," said Don Quixote. He said this with a romantic quality in his voice, as if sleeping outside was a wonderful and noble thing. "This is the life of the knight-errant. We sleep where fate carries us."

Sancho looked at the ground. It was hard and rocky. There were some dry bushes. Some insects made noises in the grass.

"In the books," he said carefully, "do the knights always sleep on the ground? Or do they sometimes sleep in a bed?"

"They sleep wherever they must," said Don Quixote. "Sometimes in a great castle. Sometimes under a tree. The knight accepts all conditions with equal grace."

Sancho accepted this. He took what remained of his food from his bag and shared it with Don Quixote. They ate sitting on the ground by the side of the road. The stars came out one by one. The night was cool and clear.

Don Quixote did not eat much. He was thinking about Dulcinea del Toboso. He was composing speeches to her in his mind. He said some of them quietly under his breath. He looked at the moon and thought of her face.

Sancho ate everything he had. He was very hungry. He drank from his wine skin. He lay down on the ground using his bag as a pillow. Within minutes, he was asleep.

Don Quixote sat awake for many hours. He thought about the giants, who had been windmills. He thought about Frestón. He thought about his broken lance. He thought about all the adventures that were still to come.

He was not discouraged. He was never discouraged for long. Each obstacle, each defeat, each fall, only made the story better. The greatest knights in his books had suffered many defeats before their final triumphs. He was patient. His time would come.

He fell asleep just before dawn, still sitting upright, leaning against a rock.

---

### Part Seven: The New Lance and a New Day

In the morning, Don Quixote cut a branch from a young oak tree. He worked carefully with his sword. He shaped the branch as best he could. He attached his spear tip to the end with strips of leather. The result was not impressive. The branch was slightly curved. The tip was not perfectly centered. But it was a lance of sorts.

Sancho watched this operation without commenting.

They set off again as the sun rose over the flat land. The morning was cool and quiet. Birds sang in the bushes. Rocinante walked steadily. The gray donkey walked behind him, carrying Sancho comfortably.

Sancho had a question again. He often had questions in the morning when his mind was fresh.

"Sir, about yesterday," he said. "The windmill situation. I want to make sure I understand. So the rule is: when you say something is a giant, I should believe it is a giant, even if it looks like a windmill to me?"

"Exactly," said Don Quixote.

"And if I say it looks like a windmill, you will say my eyes are deceived by an enchanter?"

"Likely," admitted Don Quixote.

"So whatever I see," said Sancho slowly, "my eyes might be wrong because of this Frestón person?"

"You are beginning to understand," said Don Quixote encouragingly.

Sancho was quiet for a moment, thinking about this. "This seems like a difficult system for a person like me," he said at last. "I am a man who trusts his eyes. My eyes have never been enchanted before. They have always been quite reliable."

"Before you became a squire," said Don Quixote gently, "you never moved in the world of knights and enchanters. Now you do. Different rules apply."

Sancho decided to stop thinking about this. It gave him a headache.

They continued in silence for a while. The road passed through some low hills. On the other side of the hills, the land opened up again into wide flat plains. They could see for many miles in every direction.

And in those miles, they could see many things. They could see fields. They could see some distant farms. They could see a flock of sheep moving slowly in the distance.

Don Quixote looked at all of this.

"A fine day for adventure," he said.

---

### Part Eight: The Friars on the Road

Around midday, they saw something coming toward them on the road. In the distance, two large dark shapes moved slowly. Behind the dark shapes was a large closed carriage. Behind the carriage, several men walked on foot.

Don Quixote stopped Rocinante. He stood very still and stared at the approaching group.

The two dark shapes were monks from a monastery. They were Benedictine friars, two of them, riding tall mules. They wore dark robes and wide hats to protect them from the sun. The large carriage behind them was carrying a wealthy Biscayan lady who was traveling to Seville to meet her husband. The men walking were servants.

This was an ordinary scene on a Spanish road. People traveled with monks for safety. Wealthy people traveled in closed carriages. Servants walked behind their employers.

But Don Quixote did not see an ordinary scene.

"Sancho," he said quietly, in a serious voice. "Either I am very wrong, or this is going to be the greatest adventure we have ever seen."

Sancho looked at the road ahead. He saw monks and a carriage. He thought they looked like monks and a carriage. But he waited.

"Do you see those two shapes in the dark robes?" said Don Quixote.

"The monks on the mules," said Sancho. "Yes."

"Those are not monks," said Don Quixote. His voice became very low and intense. "Those are enchanters. Sorcerers. They have kidnapped a princess, a great lady of noble birth, and they are carrying her against her will in that carriage. They are taking her to some terrible place. I must rescue her."

Sancho looked at the monks again. They were ordinary monks. He could see their robes. He could see their wide hats. They were moving very slowly and carefully on their tall mules because the road was uneven. They looked peaceful and not at all evil.

"Sir," said Sancho carefully. "Those really are monks. I can see their monk robes. I can see their "

"I will rescue the princess," said Don Quixote. He did not appear to hear Sancho. He was already positioning his lance. He was already straightening in his saddle.

Sancho closed his mouth. He had learned yesterday that arguing did not help. He held his donkey back and watched.

---

### Part Nine: The Challenge

Don Quixote rode to the center of the road. He positioned himself directly in the path of the approaching monks. He waited for them to come close enough to hear him.

The two monks rode forward. They were talking to each other about monastery business. They were not watching the road carefully. When they finally noticed the strange armored man in the middle of the road, they pulled their mules to a stop.

Don Quixote spoke in his loudest and most formal voice.

"Wicked and monstrous creatures!" he cried. "Stop immediately and release the princess you are carrying in that carriage! Release her at once, or prepare to face the consequences of your evil deed!"

The two monks stared at him. They looked at each other. Then they looked back at Don Quixote.

"Sir knight," said one of them, very carefully and politely. "We are not wicked or monstrous. We are monks of the Benedictine order. We are traveling on the road, as you can see. We do not know of any princess. We know nothing of what you are talking about."

"Enough of your lies!" shouted Don Quixote. He raised his lance. "Your enchantments and deceptions do not fool me! Release the princess immediately, or I will fight you both!"

The monks looked at each other again. They were peaceful men. They did not fight. They did not carry weapons. They were not prepared for this situation at all.

One of them pulled his mule sharply to one side and rode off the road into the field. He kept going, not stopping, until he was quite far from the road. The other monk did the same thing in the other direction.

Don Quixote watched them go with great satisfaction. He called after them.

"Run, cowards! You have only delayed the inevitable! I will find you again!"

Sancho Panza, watching from a safe distance, had missed nothing. He had seen the monks on their mules. He had seen them panic and ride away. He had seen them look at Don Quixote as if he were a madman. He felt a complicated mixture of feelings. Embarrassment, worry, and also a small amount of pity for the poor monks who had done nothing wrong.

But he did not have time to think about these feelings for long.

One of the servants who had been walking behind the carriage had decided to take action. He was a large man. He was not frightened of the strange armored knight. He walked forward and began to shout at Sancho Panza.

"What is the meaning of this?" the servant demanded. "Why is your master frightening peaceful travelers on the road? Those were holy men of God!"

Sancho tried to explain. He said something about enchanters and princesses. He did not explain it very well.

The servant did not like the explanation. He grabbed Sancho and threw him to the ground. He began to beat him.

Sancho shouted. He tried to defend himself. It did not go well. He was not a fighting man. He was a farmer. He was beaten quite thoroughly before the servant walked away.

---

### Part Ten: The Lady in the Carriage

While this was happening, something else was happening on the other side of the road. Don Quixote had ridden to the carriage. He positioned himself beside the carriage window and knocked on it with his lance.

A curtain moved. A woman's face appeared at the window. She was the Biscayan lady who was traveling to meet her husband. She looked at Don Quixote with complete amazement.

Don Quixote bowed as deeply as he could while sitting on a horse.

"Most noble and beautiful princess," he said, "you are free. Your captors have been defeated. They have fled from my presence like the cowards they are. You may continue your journey in safety."

The lady looked at him. She did not look like a person who had just been rescued. She looked like a person who had just woken up from a nap and discovered a madman at her window.

She said nothing.

Don Quixote waited. He expected her to thank him. He expected her to be very grateful.

Then someone else appeared. It was not the lady. It was a young man who had been riding with the lady's party. He was a Biscayan gentleman, a solid and practical man who worked as an assistant to the lady.

This man had a different reaction to the situation.

He was furious.

"Get away from that carriage," he said. His Spanish was not perfect. He was Biscayan, which meant he spoke Castilian Spanish with an accent and sometimes made small mistakes. But his anger was perfectly clear. "Get away from there this moment."

Don Quixote looked at the young man. He was a gentleman. He was well dressed. He was not a monk or a servant. He was a person of some standing.

"Are you perhaps the lady's protector?" asked Don Quixote.

"I am," said the Biscayan. "And I tell you to move away from this carriage now, before I do something about it."

Don Quixote straightened. His eyes narrowed. He recognized a challenge when he heard one.

"If you were a knight," said Don Quixote, "I would have already punished you for your insolence. But since you are clearly not a knight, I will only warn you once. Stand aside."

The Biscayan's face went red. He reached for his sword.

"I am as much a knight as you," he said. "And if you do not move away from this carriage, I will prove it."

Don Quixote raised his lance.

The Biscayan raised his sword. He grabbed a small cushion from the carriage with his other hand, to use as a shield.

The two men faced each other.

---

### Part Eleven: Sancho Gets Up

Sancho Panza was still on the ground. The servant had stopped beating him some minutes ago, but Sancho had stayed on the ground a little longer than necessary. His whole body hurt.

He slowly sat up. He looked around. He saw his donkey standing peacefully by the side of the road, eating some grass. He saw the carriage. He saw Don Quixote facing a young man with a sword.

Sancho got to his feet with difficulty. He brushed the dust off his clothes. He walked, somewhat painfully, toward his donkey.

"This is not going well," Sancho said to his donkey quietly. The donkey looked at him with large, calm eyes and continued eating grass.

Sancho looked at Don Quixote again. His master was about to fight a man with a sword. Don Quixote had a lance, which was an advantage. But the lance was a branch from an oak tree, which was less of an advantage.

"I wonder if we should have stayed home," Sancho said to the donkey.

The donkey did not reply.

Sancho decided the safest thing was to watch from where he was. He could not stop the fight. He was not a fighting man. He had already been beaten once today, and that was enough. He leaned against his donkey and watched.

---

### Part Twelve: The Fight with the Biscayan

Don Quixote and the Biscayan gentleman faced each other across the road. The carriage was between them. The servants stood back. The lady inside the carriage said some prayers.

The Biscayan said something that was intended to be very insulting. Because he did not speak Spanish perfectly, some of the insult was lost. But the general meaning was clear. He was saying that Don Quixote was not a proper knight and not a person of honor.

Don Quixote took this very seriously. He leveled his lance.

The Biscayan raised his sword and the cushion he was using as a shield.

They charged at each other.

They came together with a great crash. The Biscayan brought his sword down hard on Don Quixote's shoulder. It was a powerful blow. It went through the armor somewhat. It cut Don Quixote's ear. Blood ran down the side of his face.

Don Quixote's lance hit the Biscayan in the shoulder as well. The Biscayan swayed on his horse.

They pulled back. They faced each other again. Both were hurt. Both were determined.

Don Quixote raised his lance again. He was furious now. The blood from his ear ran down and dripped off his chin onto the armor.

"Be ready to die," he said.

The Biscayan raised his sword again. He said something in Biscayan that was probably also a threat.

The lady inside the carriage could not see what was happening. She could hear it. She could hear the clash of metal and the shouts of the men. She began to pray more urgently.

Her servants moved backward to an even safer distance.

Sancho Panza watched all of this with wide eyes. He had forgotten about his own hurts. He was genuinely worried now. He did not want his master to be killed. He liked Don Quixote, in his own way. The man was completely mad, but he was not cruel. He was not mean. He believed in what he was doing, even if what he was doing was strange.

"Please win," said Sancho quietly, to Don Quixote's general direction.

The two fighters were about to exchange another great blow. Don Quixote had raised his lance. The Biscayan had raised his sword. Both were ready to put everything into the next strike.

And then something stopped them.

---

### Part Thirteen: The Interruption

It was nothing dramatic. It was simply that the Biscayan's horse moved sideways at exactly the wrong moment. The horse was frightened by all the noise and the blood and the general excitement. It moved sideways and stumbled. The Biscayan was thrown slightly off balance.

This gave Don Quixote a fraction of a second more time.

He used it.

He brought his lance down with all his remaining strength onto the Biscayan's head, and the cushion that was serving as a shield.

The Biscayan fell from his horse. He hit the ground. He lay still.

Don Quixote rode to him immediately. He climbed down from Rocinante with some difficulty, because of his hurt shoulder and his ear. He stood over the fallen Biscayan. He drew his sword. He pointed it at the man's throat.

"Surrender," said Don Quixote. "Admit defeat."

The Biscayan was not unconscious. He was dazed, but he could hear. He lay on the ground and looked up at the sword pointed at him.

The lady inside the carriage now opened the door. She leaned out. She looked at the man on the ground, and then at the strange armored knight standing over him. She made a quick decision.

"Brave knight," she said, in her most respectful voice, "please spare this man. He is a good person who was protecting me. He meant no disrespect to a knight of your quality."

Don Quixote heard the lady's voice. He stood up straighter. He looked toward the carriage. He saw the lady there, leaning out, looking at him with what might have been respect.

He lowered his sword. He bowed.

"Lady," he said, "I spare him in your honor. Let him stand and promise that he will go to El Toboso, to present himself before the peerless Dulcinea del Toboso, and tell her what has happened here today."

The Biscayan sat up. He had no idea who Dulcinea del Toboso was. But he was willing to agree to almost anything at this point.

"I promise," he said.

Don Quixote sheathed his sword. He bowed to the lady again. He turned and walked back toward Rocinante.

Sancho came running up, limping a little from his own injuries.

"You won!" said Sancho. "Sir, you actually won!"

Don Quixote allowed himself a small smile. He was in a great deal of pain from his ear and his shoulder. But he had won. That was what mattered.

"Of course," he said simply. "Come, Sancho. Let us continue."

---

### Part Fourteen: After the Battle

They left the scene of the battle and continued down the road. The lady's carriage slowly started moving again in the opposite direction. The Biscayan gentleman, who was recovering from his fall, climbed back onto his horse with the help of servants.

Don Quixote rode with his back straight, despite the pain. His left ear was wrapped in a strip of cloth that Sancho had torn from his own shirt. The blood had stopped. But it still hurt very much.

Sancho rode behind him, aching from the beating he had received from the servant. They were both quiet for a while.

Then Sancho spoke.

"Sir," he said, "you are hurt."

"A knight does not notice small injuries," said Don Quixote.

"Your ear is bleeding through the cloth," said Sancho.

"A knight does not complain about blood," said Don Quixote.

Sancho decided to try a different approach. "Is there a rule," he said, "about a squire complaining about his own injuries? Because I was also beaten today. By a servant. And I was wondering if the rule about not complaining applies to squires as well."

Don Quixote considered this. "Squires may complain when they are genuinely hurt," he said. "Squires are not bound by the same rules of silence as knights."

Sancho immediately made a long sound of complaint. He described all of his injuries in detail. He described the places where he ached and the places where he hurt. He described the exact manner of the beating he had received. He did this for quite a long time.

Don Quixote listened patiently. He made appropriate sounds of sympathy.

After a while, Sancho ran out of complaints. He reached into his bag for his wine skin. He drank. He felt a little better.

"Tell me," said Sancho. "The lady in the carriage. Was she really a princess?"

"Without doubt," said Don Quixote.

"She seemed very normal," said Sancho. "She was dressed normally. She had a normal face."

"Enchanters disguise their prisoners carefully," said Don Quixote. "She may not have looked like a princess. But she was one. I could feel it."

Sancho accepted this. He had learned not to argue about such things. "And the monks," he said. "The ones who ran away across the fields. Were they really enchanters?"

"Clearly," said Don Quixote.

"And the Biscayan gentleman. Was he evil?"

"He was an obstacle," said Don Quixote, more carefully. "Not necessarily evil. Simply in the wrong place. He did not understand the situation."

Sancho nodded slowly. He was forming his own understanding of how adventures worked. Things that looked one way to him usually meant something completely different to Don Quixote. The same event was two different events depending on who was looking at it.

This was confusing. But he was getting used to it.

---

### Part Fifteen: Finding a Place to Rest

The sun was getting lower. They needed to stop for the night. Don Quixote's shoulder and ear needed proper attention. Sancho was very hungry. The bread and cheese were long gone. They needed food and rest.

"We must find a castle," said Don Quixote. "A proper castle, where a wounded knight may rest and recover in comfort."

Sancho looked around. He could see fields and hills and a distant village. "I see a village," he said.

"That may be an enchanted castle," said Don Quixote hopefully.

"It looks like a village," said Sancho. "I can see ordinary houses."

"We will investigate," said Don Quixote.

They rode toward the village. It was indeed a village, a small and ordinary one, with a few dozen houses and a church and a square. There were people going about their evening activities, drawing water, closing shutters, calling children in from the streets.

Nobody paid much attention to the two travelers at first. Then people began to notice the strange armored man with the bloody cloth around his ear, and the round little man on the donkey who looked like he had been through a difficult day.

Don Quixote looked for a castle. He found an inn.

In his imagination, the inn became a castle again. The innkeeper became a lord. The serving girl who came to the door became a lady of the castle.

He rode up to the door with great dignity and announced his arrival.

"Greetings," he called. "I am Don Quixote of La Mancha, knight-errant. I have been injured in the service of good. I request shelter and assistance for myself and my faithful squire, Sancho Panza."

The innkeeper, a woman this time, looked at the two of them. She had seen many strange people in her years of running an inn. She assessed the situation quickly. She saw a madman and a tired little man on a donkey. She also saw two potential customers who could pay for a room and supper.

"Come in," she said. "We have room."

Don Quixote thanked her with great ceremony. He called her a noble hostess. He praised the excellent quality of her castle. He said he would write of her hospitality in his memoirs.

She thanked him politely and showed them where to stable the horse and donkey.

---

### Part Sixteen: The Inn

The inn was simple but comfortable. There were a few other travelers staying there, ordinary people on ordinary journeys. They looked at Don Quixote with curiosity but said nothing.

The serving girl helped Don Quixote remove his armor. He had not taken off all his armor since he put it on two days ago. He was glad to be free of some of it. He kept his helmet, however, because the cloth around his ear was tied to it and removing the helmet would require removing the bandage.

The serving girl looked at his ear. She said it needed cleaning and proper bandaging. She brought water and cloth and did what she could.

Don Quixote sat very still while she worked. He was thinking about Dulcinea del Toboso. He was wondering if she would have heard yet about his great victory against the Biscayan gentleman.

She probably had not. She was a farm girl in a village. News traveled slowly.

But in his imagination, she knew. She was sitting in her tower, beautiful and gracious, and she had just received word of his victory. She was proud of him. She was thinking of him.

He felt better.

Sancho Panza had found the kitchen. He had talked to the cook with great charm and friendliness. He had explained that he and his master had had a very difficult day and were very hungry. The cook had given him some food, not the best food in the world, but warm and plentiful.

Sancho brought food to Don Quixote as well. They ate in Don Quixote's room, sitting across from each other at a small table.

"That was quite a day," said Sancho, eating with enthusiasm.

"It was an excellent day," said Don Quixote. "We defeated enchanters and rescued a princess and overcame a brave opponent in honest combat. What more could a knight-errant ask?"

Sancho thought about what he might ask for. A bed, for example. Which he was going to have tonight. Clean clothes, perhaps. Fewer beatings, ideally. More food. Maybe some advance warning before the next fight.

He did not say any of this. Instead, he said: "Yes, quite a good day."

After supper, Sancho helped Don Quixote onto a proper bed. Don Quixote was exhausted. His shoulder and ear hurt badly now that the excitement had faded. He lay back on the pillow with a long exhale.

"Sancho," he said, before he closed his eyes. "Tomorrow we continue."

"Yes, sir," said Sancho.

"The world is full of wrongs that need correcting," said Don Quixote. "There are giants yet to fight. There are prisoners yet to free. There are evil men who must be stopped."

"Yes, sir," said Sancho.

"And somewhere, an island is waiting for you."

Sancho's eyes, which had been going droopy, opened again. The island. He had almost forgotten about the island in the exhaustion of the day. But it was still there, in the future, waiting.

"Yes, sir," he said, more warmly this time.

Don Quixote closed his eyes. His face relaxed. In a few minutes, he was asleep.

Sancho found a pile of straw in the corner of the room and made himself a bed. He was asleep before his head had properly touched the straw.

They slept. Outside the inn, the village was quiet. The stars were out. Rocinante and the gray donkey stood in the stable side by side, eating hay. The night was peaceful.

Somewhere on the road, no doubt, another adventure was forming. Another wrong was waiting to be corrected. Another giant or enchanter or obstacle was preparing itself, unaware that a certain knight from La Mancha was coming.

Don Quixote and Sancho Panza slept, and gathered strength, and in the morning they would ride out again.

### Part Seventeen: A Note on Windmills

The windmills are still there.

They stand on the hill outside the village of Consuegra, in the region of La Mancha in Spain. They have stood there for hundreds of years. They are round white towers with long wooden arms. On a windy day, the arms turn slowly.

People come from all over the world to see these windmills now. They come because of Don Quixote. They stand at the bottom of the hill and look up at the tall white towers, and they think about the mad knight who charged at them on his thin horse.

When you see a windmill, you might think of Don Quixote. You might think of all the times in life when someone looks at an ordinary thing and sees something magnificent, something worth fighting for. Sometimes these people are wrong. Sometimes they are defeated and fall in the dust.

But sometimes, just sometimes, a person with an impossible idea changes the world.

Don Quixote did not defeat the windmills. He never did.

But we still remember him.

### After You Read

**What happened in this chapter?**

- Don Quixote and Sancho Panza set out together for the first time.
- Sancho asked about his wages and his island; Don Quixote explained the life of adventure.
- Don Quixote saw windmills and believed they were giants.
- He charged at the windmill with his lance, was thrown to the ground, and his lance was broken.
- He said the enchanter Frestón had changed the giants into windmills.
- Two monks on the road were "enchanters" carrying a princess in Don Quixote's imagination.
- Don Quixote challenged the monks, who fled.
- A Biscayan gentleman fought Don Quixote to protect the carriage.
- Don Quixote won the fight and spared the Biscayan's life.
- They found an inn for the night and rested.

**Think about it:**
- Why does Don Quixote believe the windmills are giants? What does this tell us about the power of imagination?
- Sancho knows the truth, but he follows Don Quixote anyway. Why do you think he does this?
- After the windmill disaster, Don Quixote said Frestón had changed the giants. Is this a sign of madness, or is it a kind of clever self-protection?

**A2-B1 Vocabulary to remember:**
- **provisions** food and supplies for a journey
- **friar** a religious man who belongs to a special order, like the Benedictines
- **dazed** confused and not thinking clearly, usually after a blow to the head
- **insolence** rude and disrespectful behavior
- **sheathed** put a sword back into its holder
- **lament** to express sadness or pain out loud
- **inevitably** certainly, unable to be avoided
