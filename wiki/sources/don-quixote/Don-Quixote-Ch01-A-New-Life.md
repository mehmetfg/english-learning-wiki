# Don Quixote
## Chapter 1: A New Life

---

### Before You Read

**The story:** Don Quixote is one of the most famous books in the world. Miguel de Cervantes wrote it in Spain more than four hundred years ago. The story is about a man who reads too many books about knights. He starts to believe that he is a real knight. He goes on adventures, but many things do not go as he plans.

**Key words for this chapter:**
- **knight** a man in the old days who wore metal armor and fought for good causes
- **armor** a metal suit that protects the body in battle
- **squire** a helper who serves a knight
- **lance** a long, sharp weapon that knights carry
- **chivalry** the code of honor and good behavior for knights
- **innkeeper** a person who owns an inn, a place where travelers sleep and eat
- **adventure** an exciting and sometimes dangerous journey or experience

---

### Part One: The Man in La Mancha

In a small village in a dry, flat region of Spain called La Mancha, there lived a man. His name was Alonso Quixano. He was about fifty years old. He was thin. His face was long and serious. He had a housekeeper who was nearly forty years old. She cleaned his house and cooked his meals. He also had a niece who was not yet twenty years old. She helped around the house too. There was also a boy who helped in the field and in the stable.

Alonso Quixano was not a rich man, but he was not poor either. He owned a little land. He had a horse, a hunting dog, and a few sheep. Each morning, he ate a simple breakfast. Most days, he had lentil soup, and on Saturdays, he ate scraps of meat. On Sundays, he ate a little pigeon. He spent most of his money on clothes and on books.

He loved books more than anything else in the world. But not just any books. He loved books about knights. These were stories about brave men in shining armor who rode across the land and fought monsters and evil men. These heroes saved princesses and helped the poor. They were loyal to their kings and to beautiful ladies. They were noble and strong. They never gave up.

Alonso Quixano read these books all day long. He read them in the morning before breakfast. He read them in the afternoon when the sun was hot. He read them at night by candlelight until his eyes hurt. He sold many pieces of land to buy more books. He forgot about his fields. He forgot about his sheep. He forgot about almost everything except his books.

His favorite author was a man named Feliciano de Silva. Alonso loved the complicated sentences in those books. He read sentences like: "The reason for your unreasonable cruelty to my reason weakens my reason so much that I have reason to complain about your beauty." He read these sentences many times. He tried to understand them. He could not understand them. But he kept reading. He thought they must be very important.

He also loved to debate great questions about knights. For example: Was Amadis of Gaul a better knight than Palmerín of England? This question was very serious to him. He thought about it for hours. He argued about it with his friends. He read books to find the answer.

Over many months, something began to happen to Alonso Quixano. He read so many books that his brain could not rest. He slept very little. He thought about knights all day and all night. Slowly, very slowly, he began to lose his understanding of what was real and what was a story. In his mind, the line between truth and fantasy became thin. Then it disappeared completely.

He now believed that everything in his books was true. He believed that the stories had really happened. He believed that there were still dragons in the mountains. He believed that sorcerers still cast evil spells on good people. He believed that there were enchanted castles hidden in the forests. He believed that damsels in distress waited in towers for brave knights to save them.

His brain, as people said, had dried out from so much reading. He had gone mad.

---

### Part Two: A Decision Changes Everything

One day, a great idea came to Alonso Quixano. It was the biggest idea of his life. It felt important and serious and absolutely right.

He would become a knight.

Not just any knight. He would become a knight-errant. A knight-errant was a knight who traveled the world looking for adventures. He would ride across Spain on his horse. He would fight evil men. He would protect the weak. He would correct the wrongs of the world. He would do all the things that the knights in his books did. And he would become famous, just like them.

This idea made him feel wonderful. It made him feel young and strong. It made him feel that his life had a great purpose.

But first, he needed to prepare. A proper knight needed several things.

He needed armor. He needed a good horse. He needed a name. He needed a lady to dedicate his deeds to. In all the books, a knight always did his brave deeds in the name of a beautiful and noble lady. Without a lady, a knight had no purpose.

Alonso Quixano went to work immediately.

---

### Part Three: The Armor

In the back of his house, there was old armor. It had belonged to his great-grandfather many years ago. It was dusty and rusty. Parts of it were broken. It had not been used for a very long time. But Alonso Quixano saw it with the eyes of a dreamer. To him, it was magnificent.

He carried the armor out of the storage room and cleaned each piece. He polished the metal. He rubbed away the rust as best he could. He put on the breastplate, the backplate, and the arm guards. He found the leg guards and the gauntlets for his hands.

But there was a problem. The helmet was incomplete. It only had the top part. It had no visor. The visor was the metal piece that protected the face. Without a visor, the helmet was not proper armor.

Alonso Quixano thought about this for a week. Then he had an idea. He would make a visor himself. He found some pieces of cardboard and attached them to the front of the helmet. It looked like a proper visor. He was very pleased with his work.

But then he wanted to test it. He wanted to make sure the visor was strong enough to protect him in battle. He picked up his sword and swung it at the cardboard visor. The cardboard fell apart immediately. In just one hit, his new visor was destroyed.

He was a little disappointed. But he was not discouraged for long. He made a new visor, this time more carefully. He attached it with thin strips of metal. He attached it very well. He looked at his new visor. It looked strong. It looked excellent.

He decided not to test it this time. If he tested it, it might break again. It was better, he thought, to believe it was strong. And so he believed it.

He put on the whole suit of armor and looked at himself in a mirror. He stood up straight. He lifted his chin. He looked very serious. He looked, he thought, exactly like a proper knight.

He was ready.

Almost.

---

### Part Four: The Horse

Now he needed a good horse. A knight-errant could not travel on just any animal. He needed a magnificent horse, a great and noble beast that would carry him across the world.

He had a horse. It was an old horse. It was thin and bony. Its legs were not strong. It was not fast. It had many problems that a good horse should not have. The horse's ribs were visible under its dull coat. Most people would say it was a poor and tired animal.

But Alonso Quixano did not see an old, tired horse. He saw a great and mighty steed. He decided that this horse needed a proper name. A knight's horse needed a name that sounded important and noble. He thought about names for four days.

He considered many names. He wanted a name that showed the horse's past and its future. He wanted a name that showed it was special. After much thought, he chose the name Rocinante.

In Spanish, rocín means an ordinary, low-quality horse. But by adding the word ante, which meant "before," the name became something more poetic. Rocinante meant something like "once an ordinary horse, but now something greater." He was very satisfied with this name. He thought it was clever and fitting.

---

### Part Five: His Own Name

Next, he needed a name for himself. He could not go on adventures as plain Alonso Quixano. That was the name of a thin, quiet man from a small village. That was not the name of a knight.

He thought carefully. A knight's name had to sound important and brave. It had to make people stop and listen. It had to make people feel that great things were possible.

He decided on Don Quixote.

Don was a title of respect in Spain. Quixote came from the word for the armor piece that protected the thigh. He thought this was a fine name for a knight. It sounded important. It sounded Spanish and noble.

But he also decided that he needed to add the name of his region. The great knights in the books always added their homeland to their name. Amadis was Amadis of Gaul. So he would be Don Quixote of La Mancha. He would represent his homeland. He would bring honor to the flat dry plains where he was born.

Don Quixote of La Mancha. He said it out loud. He liked the sound very much.

---

### Part Six: The Lady

There was one more thing he needed. The most important thing of all. He needed a lady.

In every book about knights, there was a beautiful and noble lady. The knight dedicated all of his adventures to her. He did everything in her name. When he won a battle, he thought of her. When he suffered hardship, he suffered for her love. The lady gave the knight a reason to be brave.

Don Quixote thought about this very seriously. He needed to find a lady to love. He needed someone whose beauty was extraordinary. He needed someone worthy of his devotion.

He thought of a girl from a nearby village called El Toboso. Her name was Aldonza Lorenzo. She was a farm girl, strong and practical. She worked hard and was healthy. She was not a noblewoman. She was not particularly refined. Don Quixote himself had barely spoken to her. He had seen her from a distance a few times in his life. But in his imagination, she was transformed.

In his mind, she became the most beautiful and perfect woman in the world. She was a princess. She was noble and gracious. She was everything a knight's lady should be.

He gave her a new name too. He could not dedicate his adventures to an Aldonza. That name was too ordinary. He chose the name Dulcinea del Toboso. Dulcinea sounded soft and sweet, like the word dulce, which means sweet. And del Toboso told people she was from El Toboso.

Dulcinea del Toboso. His lady. His inspiration. His reason to be brave.

He was now ready to begin.

---

### Part Seven: The First Departure

It was a hot summer morning in July. Before the sun was fully up, before his housekeeper was awake, before anyone in the village was moving, Don Quixote put on his armor. He put on Rocinante's saddle. He found his lance, which was old and long and had no shine. He took his shield. He took his sword.

He walked quietly out of the house. He did not tell his housekeeper where he was going. He did not tell his niece. He said nothing to anyone. He simply left.

He led Rocinante out of the stable and into the cool morning air. He looked at the flat fields of La Mancha around him. The sky was turning orange and pink at the edges. The land was quiet and still. The air smelled of dry grass and dust.

He climbed onto Rocinante's back. The horse stood patiently. It did not seem excited. But Don Quixote was very excited indeed. His heart was beating fast. This was the moment he had been waiting for.

He began to ride.

As he rode, he imagined the great story that someone would write about him one day. He imagined the famous historian who would record his adventures. He even imagined the opening words of the book. He said them out loud as Rocinante walked slowly across the plain.

"No sooner had the rising sun stretched his golden threads over the wide surface of the earth," he called out to no one, "than the famous knight Don Quixote of La Mancha left the plains of his home to journey into the wider world."

He felt very pleased with these words. He continued riding.

The morning passed. The sun rose higher. The land became hotter. There was no shade on the flat plains of La Mancha. The road was dusty. Rocinante walked slowly. Don Quixote did not mind. He was deep in his thoughts.

But then a small problem came to his mind.

He had not been properly made a knight.

In all the books, a knight had to go through a ceremony. He had to spend a night watching over his armor in a chapel or a church. Then, in the morning, a proper lord or nobleman had to tap him on the shoulder with a sword and speak the proper words. Only then was a man truly a knight.

Don Quixote had done none of this. He had simply put on his armor and walked out of his house. He was not a proper knight at all.

This was a serious problem. He rode and thought about it. He felt a little worried. But then he remembered some stories where this rule had been broken. He remembered some knights in books who had been made knights in unusual circumstances. He told himself that the first person he met who was important enough could make him a knight properly. He just needed to find the right person.

He felt better after thinking this. He continued riding.

---

### Part Eight: The Inn That Was a Castle

By the time the sun was setting, Don Quixote and Rocinante had traveled many miles. They were both tired. Don Quixote's legs ached from the unfamiliar weight of the armor. Rocinante plodded along with his head low.

Then, in the distance, Don Quixote saw something. It was a large building beside the road. In the fading light, he could see its walls and its doorway. On either side of the main entrance, he could see two shapes. Two women stood near the entrance of the building.

Don Quixote's heart leaped.

In his imagination, the building was transformed. The large, plain roadside inn became a magnificent castle. It had four towers with shining silver battlements. It had a drawbridge over a deep moat. It had a great gate with flags flying in the wind. Everything was wonderful and noble.

The two women at the entrance were not ordinary women at all. In Don Quixote's eyes, they were beautiful ladies of high birth. They were princesses enjoying the evening air at the gate of their royal castle.

Don Quixote straightened in his saddle. He pulled back his shoulders. He lifted his chin. He rode forward slowly and with great dignity, the way a knight should approach a castle.

He was almost at the gate when something unexpected happened. A man blowing a horn passed by on the road. The man was calling pigs in from the fields for the evening. It was just an ordinary farm activity, the sound heard all across Spain at sunset.

But to Don Quixote, the sound of the horn was something completely different. He heard it as the sound of a royal trumpeter announcing the arrival of a great knight at a noble castle. He felt honored. He smiled inside his helmet.

The two women at the entrance were, in reality, not princesses at all. They were young women from a nearby town. They had stopped at the inn for the night. They were traveling women, and they were quite ordinary. When they saw the strange man in old, dull armor riding toward them on a thin old horse, carrying a long lance, they were frightened. They turned to run inside.

Don Quixote saw their movement and spoke quickly. He used the most formal and old-fashioned language he knew, the language of the books he had read.

"Do not run, my ladies," he said. "Do not be afraid. The order of knighthood that I follow and practice does not permit me to do harm to anyone, and least of all to ladies of such high rank as your appearance shows you to be."

The women stopped running. They stared at him. They had no idea what he was saying. His words were so old and formal that they barely understood him. They looked at each other. Then one of them began to laugh. The other joined in.

Don Quixote was not insulted by the laughter. He thought they were laughing with pleasure because he had been so polite. He smiled warmly behind his visor.

---

### Part Nine: The Innkeeper

The owner of the inn came out to see what was happening. He was a fat, good-natured man. He had spent many years managing his inn on the busy road. He had seen many strange travelers in his time. But he had never seen anything quite like this.

He looked at Don Quixote carefully. He saw the old armor. He saw the cardboard visor. He saw the thin old horse. He saw the long lance and the old shield. He saw the serious expression on the man's face.

The innkeeper was a practical man. He could see that the man was not dangerous, just unusual. He also saw that the man might pay for a room and a meal. That was what mattered most.

He walked forward to greet the traveler. He bowed politely.

Don Quixote looked down at the innkeeper from on top of Rocinante. In his imagination, the innkeeper was transformed. He became the lord and governor of the great castle. He was a man of high importance and noble birth.

"Sir," said Don Quixote, speaking very formally, "I ask your permission to rest at your great castle this night. I am a knight-errant, traveling the world in search of adventure and in service of good."

The innkeeper blinked. Castle? He looked around at his inn. It was a simple building with a stable and a few rooms. There was nothing castle-like about it. But he was wise enough to understand that arguing with this man would be useless.

"Of course," said the innkeeper, bowing again. "You are most welcome here, sir knight. Please come inside. There is food and a stable for your horse."

Don Quixote was very pleased with this response. He climbed down from Rocinante, which was not easy in his heavy armor. He almost fell but caught himself. He then carried his lance toward the door of the inn. But the lance was so long that he could not carry it through the doorway. He stood for a moment, thinking about this problem.

The innkeeper offered to hold it for him. Don Quixote thanked him but refused. He leaned the lance against the wall outside. He walked inside.

The two women he had seen at the entrance were now inside. They helped Don Quixote take off his armor. This was necessary because he could not remove the armor himself. It required someone to help with the many straps and buckles.

Taking off the helmet was a problem. The cardboard visor that he had made was attached with a green ribbon that was tied in a tight knot behind his head. The women tried to untie it, but the knot was very tight. They could not get it loose. They did not want to cut the ribbon because Don Quixote told them it would damage the armor.

So in the end, Don Quixote kept the helmet on his head. He sat at the table in the inn still wearing his helmet. He could not eat normally because of the visor in front of his face. The women tried to feed him by lifting food under the visor. But it was not easy.

For wine, there was a hollow tube. They put one end under the visor and poured the wine in slowly. Don Quixote drank this way without complaint. He was satisfied.

He thought everything was going perfectly. He did not notice that the women were trying very hard not to laugh.

---

### Part Ten: The Great Request

During the meal, Don Quixote became increasingly troubled by one thought. He was not a proper knight. He needed to be officially made a knight by a man of rank and importance. He had been thinking about this problem since morning.

He looked at the innkeeper. In Don Quixote's eyes, the innkeeper was the governor of a castle and a man of great importance. Surely this man could make him a knight.

After the meal was finished, Don Quixote called the innkeeper to him. He spoke very seriously.

"Gracious governor of this castle," said Don Quixote, "there is a favor I must ask of you. It is of the greatest importance to me."

"Of course," said the innkeeper, wondering what this strange guest wanted.

"Tonight," said Don Quixote, "I wish to keep a vigil over my arms. I will watch over my armor in your courtyard or in your chapel. And in the morning, I ask you to perform the ceremony. I ask you to knight me properly, so that I may travel the world as a true knight-errant."

The innkeeper stared at him.

He now understood that his guest was not completely right in his mind. He was a madman. But he was a gentle madman, and he was not causing any trouble. And he seemed to believe everything he said with complete sincerity.

The innkeeper thought quickly. He had read a few books about knights himself, long ago. He knew about the ceremonies. And he was a clever man. He saw that if he played along, there would be no problem. If he argued, there might be trouble.

He nodded seriously. He put on his most formal expression.

"Sir knight," said the innkeeper, "I understand your request perfectly. I will be happy to help you. You must keep vigil over your armor tonight in the courtyard. And in the morning, I will perform the ceremony that will make you a true knight."

Don Quixote's face lit up with joy. He stood and bowed deeply to the innkeeper. He thanked him many times. He called him a noble and worthy governor.

"I must tell you," added the innkeeper, in a more careful tone, "that I was once a knight-errant myself. I traveled many lands and had many adventures. So I know the ceremony perfectly."

This was a complete lie. The innkeeper had never been a knight. He had never left his inn for more than a few days. But Don Quixote believed every word. He looked at the innkeeper with deep respect.

---

### Part Eleven: The Vigil

That night, when everyone had gone to sleep, Don Quixote went to the courtyard of the inn. He collected all of his armor, his lance, and his shield. He laid the armor pieces on the trough where horses drank water. Then he picked up his lance and began to walk slowly back and forth in front of the trough. He was keeping his vigil. He was watching over his armor, as a proper knight must do.

The night was quiet and still. The moon was bright overhead. The only sounds were the distant sound of horses and the crickets in the darkness. Don Quixote walked back and forth, back and forth. He thought about Dulcinea del Toboso, his beautiful imagined lady. He spoke quietly to himself, making speeches and prayers the way knights did in his books.

He was completely happy.

A few hours passed. Then, a muleteer who was staying at the inn came to the courtyard. He needed to water his mules. The animals were thirsty and needed to drink from the trough. But the trough was covered with Don Quixote's armor.

The muleteer did not understand why someone's armor was sitting on the water trough. He simply reached down and picked up the armor to move it out of the way.

This was a mistake.

Don Quixote saw the man touch his armor. He stopped walking. He stood very still. His face went rigid with anger. He raised his lance high above his head.

"Rash and insolent knight!" Don Quixote shouted. "Do not touch the arms of the most valiant knight-errant who ever wore a sword! Touch them at your peril!"

The muleteer did not listen to this speech. He probably did not understand all the old-fashioned words. He continued trying to move the armor.

Don Quixote struck him on the head with his lance. The muleteer fell to the ground. He did not get up for a while.

The noise woke some people in the inn. But Don Quixote returned to his walking, carrying his lance, watching over his armor. He was completely calm again. He had defended his arms. He had done his duty as a knight.

Later, a second muleteer came to water his mules. He had not seen what happened to the first man. He too tried to move the armor from the trough. Don Quixote struck him as well, though not quite as hard. This man fell and then crawled away, calling for help.

Now there was real noise and confusion. People came running. Several men who were staying at the inn came out into the courtyard. They could see that this strange armored man had hurt two of their companions.

They began to throw stones at Don Quixote from a safe distance.

Don Quixote was furious. He called out to them with great dignity. He told them that they were attacking a knight-errant, which was a terrible sin. He warned them of the consequences.

The innkeeper heard all the noise and came running out. He could see that the situation was becoming dangerous. He had to stop the fight before someone was seriously hurt.

He walked toward Don Quixote, speaking in a calm and respectful voice.

"Sir knight," said the innkeeper, "I understand that you have defended your armor with great courage. You have done everything correctly. But I must tell you something important. This is not an ordinary inn. This is an enchanted place. The people who threw stones at you were enchanted enemies. It was a test, and you have passed it."

Don Quixote stopped. He listened to the innkeeper.

"Because of your bravery tonight," the innkeeper continued, "it is no longer necessary to keep the vigil until morning. You have done enough. The ceremony can happen right now. I will knight you immediately."

Don Quixote considered this. It was unusual. In the books, the vigil always lasted the whole night. But the innkeeper's words sounded convincing. And the inn was enchanted, after all. Enchanted places had different rules.

He agreed.

---

### Part Twelve: The Ceremony

The innkeeper called for his account book. This was the book where he wrote down what his guests owed him money. But in the darkness of the courtyard, it looked something like a prayer book or a book of important records.

One of the women at the inn held a small candle. The other woman stood nearby. The muleteer who had been hit on the head was now standing up again. He was not badly hurt, but he was very angry. However, he stood and watched in confusion.

Don Quixote knelt on the ground in front of the innkeeper. He bowed his head. He was perfectly still and perfectly serious.

The innkeeper opened his account book. He held it in front of him as if he were reading from a sacred text. He spoke solemnly.

"By the authority vested in me as the lord of this castle," said the innkeeper, reading from a list of food prices without looking up, "I hereby dub thee a knight."

He closed the book. He raised his hand. He gave Don Quixote a strong blow on the back of the neck. Then he drew the sword at his side and tapped Don Quixote on each shoulder, first the right, then the left.

"I dub thee Don Quixote of La Mancha," said the innkeeper. "Rise, sir knight."

Don Quixote rose. He felt transformed. He felt that something important had happened, something real and true. He felt taller. He felt braver. He felt that his whole life had led to this moment.

One of the women came forward and fastened his sword around his waist. The other woman put on his spurs. They kept straight faces, though it was very difficult.

Don Quixote thanked them all with great seriousness. He called them noble ladies and thanked them for the honor they had shown him. He told the innkeeper that he was grateful beyond words. He said he would remember this castle and its noble lord forever.

The innkeeper told him, gently but firmly, that it would be a good idea to leave as soon as possible after sunrise. He said the enchanted nature of the inn might cause problems if Don Quixote stayed too long. He also suggested that a knight-errant needed money for his travels, and clean shirts, and some practical things.

Don Quixote had never thought about money. Knights in his books never worried about money. Things always worked out for them. But he politely said he would consider the innkeeper's advice.

Then he thanked everyone again. He went to the stable to find Rocinante.

---

### Part Thirteen: The Road Home

Before sunrise, Don Quixote saddled Rocinante and rode out of the inn yard. He was a knight now. A proper, official knight. He was deeply happy.

The road stretched out before him in the early morning light. The sky was pale blue with thin orange clouds near the horizon. The fields of La Mancha were flat and quiet. There were no people on the road. There were no sounds except the slow clop of Rocinante's hooves on the dirt.

Don Quixote rode and thought. He thought about all the adventures that were coming. He thought about Dulcinea del Toboso. He thought about the wrongs he would correct and the people he would help. He thought about the stories that would be told about him.

He felt very good. Everything was going exactly as it should.

But then, coming down the road toward him, he heard voices. He heard the sound of people walking and talking in a large group. He stopped Rocinante and waited.

Coming toward him was a group of merchants. They were traveling from Toledo to buy silk. There were six of them, and they had several servants and helpers. They were ordinary businessmen on an ordinary business trip.

But Don Quixote did not see ordinary merchants. He saw strangers on the road. In the world of his imagination, any stranger on the road was either an enemy to fight or a person to help. He did not know which yet.

He moved Rocinante to the center of the road. He picked up his lance and positioned his shield. He waited until the merchants were close enough to hear him.

Then he spoke in his loudest and most formal voice.

"All the world, halt!" he shouted. "And confess to all the world that there is no lady in the world more beautiful than the Empress of La Mancha, the peerless Dulcinea del Toboso!"

The merchants stopped. They stared at the man blocking the road. He was wearing old, dull armor and carrying a long lance. He was sitting on the thinnest horse they had ever seen. He looked completely serious.

The merchants were experienced businessmen. They traveled the roads constantly. They had met many strange people in their years of travel. They were not easily frightened, and they were not easily angered. They were practical men.

One of them spoke up. He spoke carefully and politely.

"Sir knight," he said, "we do not know this lady called Dulcinea del Toboso. We have never seen her. We cannot say that she is the most beautiful lady in the world because we have never met her. Perhaps if you show us her picture, or give us some sign or proof of her beauty, we will be happy to agree with you."

This was a completely reasonable thing to say.

But Don Quixote did not see it as reasonable. To him, it was an insult. The whole point was that you had to believe in Dulcinea's beauty without seeing proof. Faith did not require evidence. That was what faith was.

He shouted back at the merchant.

"The importance of the matter," he cried, "lies in the fact that you must believe it without seeing any proof! If I show you a picture, what virtue would there be in confessing what is so obvious? The important thing is that you believe it without seeing it!"

Another merchant, who had a slightly mischievous character, spoke up.

"But sir knight," said this man, still very politely, "perhaps you could at least tell us if this lady has any imperfections. All people have small imperfections. It would not dishonor her greatly to admit a small flaw."

This was too much for Don Quixote.

He raised his lance. He put his heels to Rocinante's sides. He charged at the merchant who had spoken.

Rocinante tried. The old horse was willing. He began to move forward. But Rocinante had a problem that his rider did not know about. The road at this point was uneven, and there was a hole. Rocinante stepped into the hole and stumbled. The horse fell forward.

Don Quixote, still in his heavy armor, was thrown from the saddle. He hit the ground very hard. The armor crashed and rattled. He lay in the road, on his back, unable to get up. The armor was too heavy. He struggled and struggled, but he could not rise.

---

### Part Fourteen: On the Ground

The merchants gathered around the fallen knight. They looked down at him. He was lying in the road, arms and legs moving helplessly, like a turtle on its back.

One of the servants with the merchants was not a kind man. He picked up Don Quixote's lance, which had fallen nearby. He broke it into pieces. Then he used the pieces to beat Don Quixote through the armor. He hit him many times.

Don Quixote cried out. He shouted insults. He threatened terrible revenge. He called on Dulcinea del Toboso to witness his suffering. He called on the gods and heroes of his books to help him.

No help came.

The servant grew tired of hitting him. The merchant group walked around the fallen man and continued on their road. They disappeared down the road, talking among themselves. After a few minutes, Don Quixote was alone in the road.

He lay there for a while. The sun was higher now and it was getting warm. He hurt in many places from the beating. But he was not broken. His spirit was absolutely unbroken.

He lay on his back and thought about the great knights in his books. He remembered a story about a knight called Valdovinos who was left wounded in a forest. He began to recite the words of that story. He recited them out loud, to the empty road and the open sky.

"Where are you, my lady?" he called. "Are you not aware of my suffering? Oh, that some other knight might come to my aid! Oh, that Dulcinea might see me now!"

He continued speaking and reciting for some time. Nobody heard him except some birds in a nearby field.

Then, fortunately, a farmer from his own village came down the road. This man recognized Don Quixote. He climbed down from his horse and came to help.

The farmer looked at the strange man in armor lying in the road. He removed the visor from Don Quixote's face. He recognized his neighbor.

"Señor Quixano," said the farmer. "What has happened? What is wrong?"

Don Quixote looked at the farmer. But he did not see his neighbor. In his imagination, the farmer was transformed. He became a character from one of his books. He spoke to the farmer using the name of this character.

The farmer shook his head. He helped Don Quixote stand. He took off the worst pieces of the armor so that Don Quixote could move more easily. He put Don Quixote on his own horse, since Rocinante would need to be led. He picked up the broken pieces of the lance and put them across Rocinante's back.

They began the slow journey home to their village.

Don Quixote talked the entire way. He spoke about knights and battles and the wrongs of the world. The farmer listened politely. He understood that his neighbor had gone quite mad. But there was nothing cruel in him. He listened and nodded and led the horse gently down the road.

---

### Part Fifteen: The Return

It was dark when they arrived at the village. The farmer wanted to avoid people seeing Don Quixote in his condition. He waited until later in the evening, when most people were inside their houses.

But the housekeeper and the niece had been waiting all day. They were very worried. They had discovered that morning that Don Quixote was gone. They had also discovered that the old armor was missing. They had a very bad feeling about what had happened.

When they saw the farmer leading Don Quixote home, with the broken armor and the old lance and the thin horse, they ran out into the street.

The housekeeper cried out.

"My lord!" she said. "What happened? What have you done to yourself? Why did you leave? We have been so worried!"

The niece said nothing. She just took his arm and helped him inside.

Don Quixote was exhausted and aching. He was helped to his bed. The women took off the remaining armor. They could see that he had been hurt. There were bruises on his body.

They brought him food and water. They put a cool cloth on his head.

Don Quixote ate a little and then grew quiet. His eyes were closing.

"Send for the priest and the barber," whispered the niece to the housekeeper. "We must talk about what to do. This cannot continue. He has gone completely mad."

"I know," said the housekeeper quietly. "I know."

They watched over him as he fell asleep. His face was peaceful. He was smiling slightly.

In his dreams, he was riding across great plains. He was defending the weak and fighting evil. He was winning. The world was full of adventures and wonders. Dulcinea del Toboso was watching from a high tower and smiling at his bravery.

Don Quixote slept.

He would not sleep for long.

---

### Part Sixteen: What Happened While He Slept

While Don Quixote slept, the housekeeper and the niece sent for two people. They sent for the village priest, whose name was Pero Perez. He was a learned man who had studied at a university. He was practical and sensible. He was also a close friend of Don Quixote's.

They also sent for the barber, whose name was Maese Nicolas. In those days, barbers did many things besides cut hair. They also pulled teeth and gave medical advice. The barber was another good friend of Don Quixote's. He often talked with him about books.

The priest and the barber arrived early the next morning. Don Quixote was still sleeping.

The housekeeper led them to the library. She told them everything that had happened. She showed them the bookshelves, full of books about knights and chivalry. She told them that these books were the cause of all the trouble.

"These books have stolen his mind," she said. "He reads them day and night. They are full of nonsense about knights and dragons and enchanted castles. And now he believes it all. He went out yesterday dressed in old armor and he has come back beaten and broken."

The priest nodded slowly. He looked at the shelves and shelves of books. There were hundreds of them. Some were very thick. Some were thin. Some were old and worn from being read many times.

"We must do something about these books," said the priest.

The barber agreed.

"We must burn them," said the housekeeper. She said this with great feeling. She was very angry at the books.

The priest was more careful. He was a man who loved books himself. He did not want to burn any book without looking at it first. He wanted to decide which books were truly harmful and which might be saved.

He began to go through the shelves, one book at a time, examining each one. Some he put aside to be burned. Some he said could be kept. He made judgments with great care and seriousness, as if he were judging real crimes.

Some books he saved because they were beautifully written or had good stories. Some he condemned because their stories were too wild and impossible. Some he could not decide about and put aside for more thought.

This took quite a long time.

While the priest and the barber worked through the library, Don Quixote slept.

When he finally woke, he was confused. For a moment, he did not know where he was. Then he remembered. He was home. He had been brought home by the farmer. He had been beaten on the road.

He lay still for a moment. His body hurt. But his mind was already moving forward. He was already thinking about his next adventure. He was already planning what he would do differently next time.

He knew now that he needed a squire. Every great knight had a squire, a helper who traveled with him. In the books, the squire helped with many practical things. He carried extra weapons and supplies. He helped the knight put on and take off his armor. He was a companion on the long, lonely road.

Don Quixote thought about who his squire could be. He thought about his neighbors and acquaintances. He thought about who might be willing to travel with him.

There was a man nearby, a farmer named Sancho Panza. He was a good man, simple and honest. He was not very bright, but he was kind. He had a wife and children. He was a poor man who always hoped for something better.

Don Quixote thought that Sancho Panza would make a very good squire. He would talk to him. He would convince him to come.

Don Quixote smiled to himself in the darkness of his room.

He was already planning.

---

### Part Seventeen: The Fire

The priest and the barber finished their review of the library. In the end, they had saved a small pile of books and condemned the rest. The housekeeper carried armload after armload of books out of the house. She threw them into a fire in the courtyard.

The books burned slowly at first, then faster. The pages curled and blackened. The leather covers cracked and split. Hours of Don Quixote's life, hundreds of stories, thousands of knights and dragons and enchanted princesses, all went up in smoke.

The housekeeper watched with satisfaction. She felt she was doing something good. She was protecting her master from these dangerous books.

But she made one mistake, in the opinion of the barber. She threw into the fire a book called Galatea, which the barber thought was quite a good book. And another book about the adventures of Tirante the White, which the priest actually admired very much.

Some good things went into the fire along with the harmful ones. This is often how it goes.

When the fire was done, the priest and the barber took further precautions. They sealed the door of the library room. They plastered over the doorway so that it could not be seen. They wanted Don Quixote to think the library had never existed.

They planned to tell him, when he asked, that an enchanter had come in the night and taken the library away. They thought this story would fit with his imagination.

They were right. This was exactly the kind of story that Don Quixote would believe completely.

---

### Part Eighteen: Three Weeks Later

For about three weeks after his return, Don Quixote stayed home. He was quiet. He was polite. He ate his meals. He talked with his friends the priest and the barber. He seemed almost normal.

His family hoped that the madness had passed. His housekeeper said prayers of thanks. His niece began to relax.

But Don Quixote was not cured. He was only waiting. He was planning. He was thinking about Sancho Panza.

One night, he went to Sancho Panza's house. He knocked on the door. Sancho Panza came out, rubbing his eyes.

Don Quixote spoke to him seriously and earnestly. He told him about being a knight-errant. He told him about the adventures that were waiting for them. He told him that he needed a squire, a loyal and brave companion to travel with him.

Sancho Panza listened with his mouth slightly open. He was not a quick thinker. But he listened to everything Don Quixote said.

Then Don Quixote told him the most important thing. He told him about the reward.

"In the books," said Don Quixote, "knights-errant often win kingdoms and great territories in their adventures. When they win such a territory, they give it to their squire to govern. If you come with me, Sancho, you could become the governor of an island."

Sancho Panza's eyes went wide.

An island. He, Sancho Panza, the poor farmer, governor of an island. With a palace and servants. With gold and fine clothes. His wife Teresa would be a great lady. His children would be nobles.

"I will come," said Sancho Panza.

He did not think very hard about whether this was likely. He did not question whether there were really islands to be won on the roads of Spain. He wanted to believe, and so he believed.

Don Quixote clasped his hand warmly.

"We leave soon," said Don Quixote. "Tell no one."

Sancho Panza nodded. He went back inside to bed. He dreamed of being a governor.

Don Quixote walked home in the dark, smiling. He was ready. This time he had a squire. This time he would be prepared. This time the adventures would go perfectly.

A new chapter of his life was beginning.

**What happened in this chapter?**

- Alonso Quixano read too many books about knights and lost his mind.
- He decided to become a knight-errant.
- He prepared old armor, named his horse Rocinante, and chose the name Don Quixote of La Mancha.
- He invented a perfect lady named Dulcinea del Toboso.
- He set out on his first adventure, thinking an inn was a castle.
- An innkeeper performed a fake knighting ceremony.
- He was thrown from his horse and beaten on the road.
- He came home hurt, but already planning his next journey.
- He recruited Sancho Panza as his squire with the promise of governing an island.
