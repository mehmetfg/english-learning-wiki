# Don Quixote
## Chapter 3: The Flock of Sheep

---

### Before You Read

**The story so far:** Don Quixote of La Mancha and his squire Sancho Panza are traveling the roads of Spain together. Don Quixote believes he is a great knight-errant. He has already attacked windmills, thinking they were giants. He has chased two monks off the road, thinking they were evil enchanters. He fought a Biscayan gentleman and won. He is now traveling again, looking for more adventures. Sancho Panza follows behind on his gray donkey, eating, complaining quietly, and hoping one day to govern an island.

**Key words for this chapter:**
- **flock** a group of sheep or birds moving together
- **sling** a simple weapon made of a strip of leather, used to throw stones at great speed
- **goatherd** a person who looks after goats
- **shepherd** a person who looks after sheep
- **funeral** the ceremony held when someone has died and is being buried
- **mourner** a person who is sad and weeping at a funeral
- **procession** a group of people moving together, usually in a line, for a ceremony
- **balm** a magical healing mixture that Don Quixote reads about in his books
- **lance** a long weapon carried by knights on horseback

---

### Part One: The Morning After

They left the inn early. The sun was just coming up over the hills. The road was quiet, and the air was cool. Birds were singing. Rocinante walked slowly but steadily. The gray donkey plodded along behind.

Don Quixote was in a reflective mood. His ear and shoulder still hurt from the previous day's battle. But he was not thinking about the pain. He was thinking about the nature of adventure and the life of the knight-errant.

Sancho was thinking about breakfast. There was none.

"Sir," said Sancho, after a while. "My stomach is empty."

"A true knight-errant does not always have food," said Don Quixote. "In the books, great knights sometimes go for days without eating. They do not notice because their minds are on higher things."

Sancho looked at his stomach. His mind was very definitely on food, which was a lower thing. "In the books," he said carefully, "did the squires also go without food? Or was it only the knights?"

Don Quixote thought about this. "The books do not mention squires eating very often," he admitted. "The stories focus on the knights."

"Perhaps the squires ate quietly in the background," said Sancho hopefully, "while the books were busy describing the knight's adventures."

"Perhaps," said Don Quixote.

"I think that is what happened," said Sancho with more confidence. "The squires were there in the background, eating properly and keeping up their strength, so they could be useful. It would make sense. A weak and hungry squire is no good to anyone."

Don Quixote smiled a little. He could not argue with Sancho's logic. "When we reach the next village, we will stop for food," he said.

Sancho cheered up considerably. The donkey seemed to walk a little faster as well, though that may have been the cooler morning air.

---

### Part Two: The Goatherds' Fire

Around midmorning, they left the main road and cut across a stretch of open land. The sun was getting warm. Dry grass covered the ground. In the distance, some low hills made lines against the blue sky.

They smelled the smoke before they saw the fire. A thin line of gray smoke rose up from behind a slight rise in the ground. Don Quixote rode toward it curiously. Sancho followed.

On the other side of the rise, there was a small camp. Four or five men sat around a fire. They were goatherds, rough and weather-beaten men who spent their lives in the open air, watching their herds. Their goats grazed nearby, black and brown shapes moving slowly through the dry grass.

The goatherds looked up when Don Quixote and Sancho appeared. They were surprised by the strange armored figure on the thin horse. But they were country people, and they were hospitable in the way that simple people often are. They invited the two travelers to sit and share their food.

There was roasted meat on the fire, and bread, and some rough wine in a leather flask.

Sancho sat down with great pleasure and immediately began to eat everything offered to him. He ate with the appetite of a man who had not had breakfast. The goatherds watched him with respect. They understood hunger.

Don Quixote sat down more carefully. He was a gentleman, and eating with common people was not entirely in keeping with the dignity of a knight. But he was hungry too, and the smell of the meat was very good.

The goatherds filled a bowl for him and set it on a flat rock in front of him. They did not have a proper chair or table, so Don Quixote sat on the ground, which was undignified for a knight. But he sat down with as much dignity as a man can manage while sitting on the ground in a dented suit of armor.

One of the goatherds handed him the leather flask of wine. Don Quixote took it. He looked at the men sitting around him. They were simple people. They had rough hands and sun-darkened faces. They did not know about chivalry or knights or the great stories of adventure. But they were real, and the fire was warm, and the meat was good.

Don Quixote felt something move in his heart.

He set down the wine flask and began to speak.

---

### Part Three: Don Quixote Speaks About the Golden Age

"Friends," said Don Quixote, in a warm and serious voice, "I want to tell you something."

The goatherds stopped eating and looked at him. They were not sure what was coming.

"You are fortunate," said Don Quixote. "More fortunate than you know. You live close to the earth. You live simply. You know what is real and what is important. In the great old days that men call the Golden Age, all people lived as you live now."

The goatherds looked at each other. Nobody spoke.

"In the Golden Age," continued Don Quixote, warming to his subject, "there was no greed. There was no injustice. The earth gave food freely to everyone. Nobody owned more than they needed. People were honest and kind. There was no need for weapons because there was no war. There was no need for locks on doors because there was no stealing. Justice was not a complicated system of courts and judges. It was simply what was right. Everyone knew what was right, and everyone did it."

He paused. The fire crackled. A goat bleated somewhere in the darkness beyond the firelight.

"That age is gone now," said Don Quixote sadly. "Now we live in an iron age, a hard age, a selfish age. That is why knights-errant were invented. When justice left the world, someone had to bring it back. The knight-errant is the person who rides through this hard world and tries, by strength and courage and goodness, to put some of the Golden Age back into it."

The goatherds listened politely. They did not entirely understand his speech. But they recognized the feeling behind it. It was the feeling of a man who wanted the world to be better than it was.

One old goatherd nodded slowly. "That is well said, stranger," he said. It was a simple thing to say, but he meant it.

Don Quixote accepted this with a slight bow.

Sancho had continued eating throughout the entire speech. He had heard Don Quixote speak this way before and had learned that the best thing to do was listen politely and eat quickly.

---

### Part Four: The Song in the Night

After the meal, the goatherds made room for the travelers to sleep by the fire. Don Quixote sat with his back against a tree and dozed. Sancho lay on the ground and was deeply asleep within minutes.

Later in the night, one of the younger goatherds took out a small instrument and began to play. It was a simple instrument, a kind of pipe, and he played it well. The music was slow and a little sad.

Then he began to sing. His voice was rough, but the song was beautiful. It was a song about a girl from the village. It was about her eyes and her hair and the way she walked. It was about how he loved her and how she did not notice him.

Don Quixote listened in the darkness. The fire had burned low. The stars above were very clear and very bright. The music and the night and the memory of the Golden Age all mixed together in his mind.

He thought of Dulcinea del Toboso. He thought of her as he always imagined her, beautiful and noble and gracious. He had never really spoken to her. He had seen her from a distance, years ago, a farm girl in a field. But in his heart, she was everything.

He sat very still and listened to the young man's song about his own lost love, and felt the strange and quiet sadness that falls on a person who loves someone they cannot reach.

Sancho slept on, undisturbed by music or philosophy.

---

### Part Five: Back on the Road

In the morning, they thanked the goatherds and continued on their way. Don Quixote was in a thoughtful and slightly sad mood. Sancho was well rested and had found some bread in his bag that he had forgotten about, so his mood was very good.

They traveled through rolling country. The road passed through a village, then through more open land, then through a small wood. They saw farmers in the fields. They passed a shepherd with his flock. They stopped at a stream to let Rocinante and the donkey drink.

It was during the afternoon, when the sun was at its highest and the world was very quiet and still, that Don Quixote saw something.

Far ahead, on the wide plain, there was a cloud of dust.

Not one cloud. Two clouds of dust. One on the left side of the plain, one on the right. Both were large. Both were moving.

Don Quixote stopped Rocinante. He sat very straight and stared at the two moving clouds of dust.

"Sancho," he said quietly. "Come here."

Sancho rode up beside him. He looked at the plain.

"What do you see?" said Don Quixote.

"Dust," said Sancho. "Two clouds of dust. Something is moving. Animals, maybe. Or a lot of people."

"Not just people," said Don Quixote. His voice was low and intense. "Not just animals. Look carefully. This is what fortune has arranged for us today. Two great armies are preparing for battle on that plain."

Sancho looked more carefully. He squinted. He could see the dust moving. He could see that something was creating it.

"Armies?" he said doubtfully.

"Two enormous armies," said Don Quixote. "They are approaching each other from opposite sides. Soon they will clash in the middle of that plain in a tremendous battle. We are here to witness it."

He began to ride forward, slowly at first, toward the plain.

---

### Part Six: The Two Armies

As they rode closer to the plain, the dust clouds grew larger. And then, through the dust, they began to hear sounds. A confused noise, a jumble of sound, like many things moving together.

Don Quixote's eyes were bright with excitement. He turned to Sancho.

"Do you hear them?" he said. "The armies! The sounds of soldiers and horses and banners! The clash of armor!"

Sancho heard the sounds. They were not the sounds of soldiers and horses. But Sancho had learned not to say what he actually heard. He just listened.

Don Quixote began to describe the scene that he saw. He described great armies with hundreds of thousands of soldiers. He named the kingdoms and the emperors who were leading each side. He invented countries and heroes. He described their armor and their flags and their battle cries. He spoke with the enthusiasm of a man reading the most exciting book in the world.

Sancho listened to all of this. He looked at the dust. He listened to the sound. He was fairly sure he knew what the dust was. He was fairly sure he knew what the sound was. But he waited.

They reached the edge of the plain.

The dust cleared enough to see.

Sheep.

There were two enormous flocks of sheep. Hundreds and hundreds of sheep, moving across the plain in two directions, raising clouds of dust with their hooves, making the loud, confused, bleating noise of many animals moving together.

The shepherds who were driving the flocks were hidden in the dust. The flocks were so large that they stretched far across the plain. From a distance, in the afternoon heat, with the dust rising and the sound carrying, it could almost look like something large and impressive.

Almost.

"Sir," said Sancho, very carefully. "Those are sheep."

"Hold your tongue, Sancho," said Don Quixote sharply. "Fear has confused your senses. What you see there is two great armies, just as I described. I will ride into the battle and support the weaker side, as a knight must do."

"Sir," said Sancho, a little desperately. "I can see them very clearly. They are sheep. I can count them, if you like. One sheep, two sheep, three "

But Don Quixote was already moving.

---

### Part Seven: The Charge

Don Quixote put his heels to Rocinante. He raised his lance. He rode down onto the plain toward the nearest flock of sheep.

He shouted. He called on the imaginary kings he had named to follow him. He declared his loyalty to one side of the imaginary battle. He insulted the imaginary kings on the other side. He rode hard into the middle of the flock.

The sheep scattered in all directions. They ran and bleated and crashed into each other. The shepherds shouted in alarm. The dust went everywhere.

Don Quixote rode through the flock, using his lance and his horse to knock sheep left and right. Several sheep were injured. Several more were knocked down. He was doing considerable damage to what was, in reality, someone's livelihood.

The shepherds were furious.

They picked up their slings. A shepherd's sling was a serious weapon. A man who spent his whole life throwing stones with a sling could throw with great speed and great accuracy. They were also not people who tolerated damage to their flocks patiently.

The stones began to fly.

The first stone hit Don Quixote on the side. He grunted. It hurt through the armor.

The second stone hit him on the hand. He dropped his lance.

The third stone hit him directly in the face. It knocked out two of his teeth on the left side of his mouth.

He gasped and almost fell from Rocinante. He reached for his horse's neck to steady himself.

A fourth stone hit him in the ribs. This one was harder than the others. He felt something crack, and a sharp pain went through his side. He was not sure if a rib was broken, but it hurt very badly.

The shepherds kept throwing. Don Quixote pulled Rocinante away from the flock and rode back across the plain. He was bleeding from his mouth. His ribs screamed with pain. His hand throbbed.

He rode back to where Sancho was waiting on the donkey. Sancho had watched the whole thing from the side of the road. His face showed a complicated mixture of concern and the kind of expression that says "I told you so" without saying any words.

---

### Part Eight: After the Sheep

Don Quixote stopped Rocinante beside Sancho. He was bleeding. He was in pain. His face was pale under the dried blood.

Sancho climbed down from his donkey. He helped Don Quixote down from Rocinante. Don Quixote sat on the ground heavily. He explored his mouth with his tongue and felt the empty spaces where his teeth had been.

"My teeth," he said. He sounded tired and stunned.

"I know," said Sancho. He sat beside him. "Sir, those were sheep."

"They were not sheep," said Don Quixote. His voice was quieter than usual. "They were soldiers. That enchanter Frestón changed them. He saw me coming to help the weaker side and changed the armies into sheep at the very last moment. To deny me the glory of the victory."

Sancho looked at the sheep, now regrouping across the plain. The shepherds were moving among them, checking for injured animals, muttering and shaking their heads. They occasionally looked in the direction of the strange armored man sitting in the road.

"Frestón does this a lot," said Sancho.

"He is persistent," admitted Don Quixote.

They sat in silence for a moment. Don Quixote touched his ribs carefully. They hurt very much. He winced.

"Sancho," he said, "I need you to do something for me."

"Yes, sir."

"I need you to look in my mouth. I believe I have lost some teeth. I want to know how many."

Sancho looked in Don Quixote's mouth. He counted. "Two," he said. "On the left side. Upper and lower."

Don Quixote nodded. "The books say that a knight should not complain about losing teeth in battle. It is one of the risks of the profession."

"The books are probably right," said Sancho. "Shall I try to clean the wound a little?"

"Yes," said Don Quixote.

Sancho cleaned the blood from Don Quixote's face with water from a small flask they carried. He did this carefully. He was not a trained healer. But he was a kind man, and he was gentle.

While he worked, Don Quixote spoke.

"Sancho," he said. "In my studies of the knights of old, I came across a recipe. A recipe for a miraculous balm."

"A balm?" said Sancho. He did not stop cleaning, but he listened.

"The Balm of Fierabras," said Don Quixote. "A magical healing potion mentioned in the old tales. A few drops of this balm can heal any wound. It can restore any injury. A knight who drinks it can recover from even the most terrible battle."

Sancho's eyes went wide. "That sounds very useful," he said.

"It is extremely useful," said Don Quixote. "I have memorized the recipe. I know exactly what goes into it. Once we reach a village with the proper ingredients, I will make it."

"And if you take it," said Sancho, "your ribs will stop hurting? And your teeth will come back?"

"The ribs will heal," said Don Quixote carefully. "The teeth are gone. The balm does not regrow teeth. Nothing can regrow teeth. But the pain in the ribs and the cut in the mouth will heal."

"What about squires?" said Sancho. "Can squires drink it too?"

Don Quixote considered this. "If a squire is injured while defending his knight, he may drink a little. But only a little. It is primarily for knights."

Sancho nodded. He decided to find out where to get the ingredients for this balm as soon as possible. If Don Quixote was going to keep charging into things, a healing potion seemed like a very practical thing to have.

---

### Part Nine: Evening on the Road

They rested for an hour to let Don Quixote recover slightly, then continued on the road. Don Quixote rode quietly, sitting carefully to avoid putting pressure on his ribs. Sancho rode behind him on the donkey.

The afternoon was long and hot. The road was dusty. Nothing notable happened for several hours, which was a relief to both of them.

Sancho thought about the sheep. He thought about the windmills. He thought about the monks. He was forming a clear picture of how adventures worked when Don Quixote was involved. There would be something ordinary on the road, a flock of sheep, or a mill, or a traveling group. Don Quixote would look at it and see something completely different. He would charge at it. Things would not go well. Don Quixote would come back hurt. He would say an enchanter had done it.

This was the pattern.

Sancho was not sure what to do about this pattern. He could not stop Don Quixote from charging. He had tried talking, and it had not worked. He could not physically stop a man on a horse. And he was, after all, a squire. His job was to follow and support, not to lead.

He thought about the island. Somewhere out there was an island, waiting for him to govern it. He had to hold onto that idea. Without the island, the whole arrangement made much less sense.

The island was still there. He just had to keep going.

They came to a small stream as the sun was getting low. They stopped to let the animals drink. Don Quixote got down from Rocinante very carefully, because of his ribs. He sat by the stream and put his feet in the water without taking off his boots, which did not really make sense, but he seemed to find it comforting.

Sancho found the last of the food in his bag and divided it between them. They ate. The stream made a quiet sound. The evening birds began to sing.

"Tomorrow," said Don Quixote, "we will find an inn. I will rest a day. My ribs need time."

Sancho was very glad to hear this. "Yes, sir," he said. "Rest is important."

"Knights in the books sometimes rest for many days after a great battle," said Don Quixote. "The battle with those enchanted armies was very hard. I am not ashamed to rest."

Sancho decided not to say anything about the armies being sheep. This was not the moment.

---

### Part Ten: The Night Road

They had not found an inn by the time it was dark. They kept following the road, hoping to reach a village, but no village came.

The night was cloudy. There was very little moonlight. The road was hard to see. Rocinante walked slowly and carefully. The donkey followed.

Don Quixote and Sancho were both tired. They were also a little nervous. The dark road in the night was not comfortable. Strange sounds came from the fields and woods on either side. The wind moved the trees.

Then Sancho saw something.

"Sir," he said, in a low voice. "Look."

Far ahead on the road, there were lights. Not the bright lights of a village. These were small, moving lights. Torches, perhaps. There were many of them. They were moving in a slow and orderly line along the road.

Don Quixote looked at the lights.

"What do you think it is?" said Sancho. He asked this carefully. He had learned to ask Don Quixote what he thought before saying anything himself. This saved time.

Don Quixote studied the lights. He could see the shape of the procession. He could see that the lights were being carried. He could see, as they got closer, that there was a large dark shape in the middle of the procession. Something being carried.

"A supernatural phenomenon," said Don Quixote. His voice was full of excitement again. "An adventure is presenting itself to us."

Sancho looked at the lights more carefully. He could now see, in the light of the torches, that the people carrying them were wearing white robes. He could hear, faintly, the sound of chanting. And he could see that the dark shape in the middle was a large covered box being carried on poles.

He knew what this was.

A funeral procession. Someone had died, and the body was being carried at night to the church in the next village, which was the custom in some parts of Spain. The people in white robes were mourners. The box was a coffin. The torches were to light the way in the dark.

"Sir," said Sancho. "I think that is a "

"I must investigate," said Don Quixote.

He rode forward to meet the procession.

---

### Part Eleven: The Procession in the Dark

The procession came closer. Now Don Quixote could hear the chanting clearly. It was a religious chant, low and rhythmic. The people carrying torches were dressed in white. Some of them also wore hoods.

Don Quixote rode to the center of the road and stopped. He held up his hand.

The procession slowed. The chanting stopped. The people in white hoods looked at the armored figure blocking the road.

Don Quixote spoke in his formal voice.

"Halt," he said. "Tell me who you are and what you carry. This is no ordinary thing, this procession in the dark. Something unusual is happening here. I demand to know what it is."

One of the mourners, who wore a more important-looking robe, stepped forward.

"Sir," said this man, politely, "we carry the body of a nobleman who died in the city. We are taking him to his village to be buried. Please let us pass."

Don Quixote heard the words "we carry" and "nobleman." In his imagination, the funeral procession was transformed. He did not hear "a dead nobleman being carried to his burial." He heard something else entirely. He heard a story about an injured knight being carried by mysterious figures in the night. He heard an enchantment.

"You have enchanted this knight," said Don Quixote, pointing at the coffin. "You have cast a spell on him. I demand that you release him."

The mourner blinked. He was a man who worked for the church. He was having a very strange night.

"Sir," he said again, more carefully, "this is a dead body. The gentleman died of a fever. We are "

"I will free him!" cried Don Quixote.

He charged.

---

### Part Twelve: The Confusion

Don Quixote rode into the procession. People scattered in all directions. The torches went every way. Some fell and went out. Others were dropped and rolled in the road. The people in white robes ran, stumbling over their long robes, shouting in alarm.

The coffin was dropped. It hit the road with a loud thud. The men who had been carrying it ran in different directions.

The mule that one of the mourners had been riding reared up in fright. The mourner, a scholar named Alonzo Lopez, was thrown to the ground. He tried to get up but could not. He had broken his leg in the fall.

Don Quixote rode through the confusion, calling out that he was freeing the enchanted knight, calling on Dulcinea del Toboso to witness his bravery.

After a few minutes, the procession was completely scattered. The road was covered in dropped torches and fallen items. The white-robed mourners were standing far back on either side of the road, at a safe distance. The coffin was lying in the road.

Sancho had watched all of this from a safe distance again. He had become very good at choosing exactly how far back to stand so that he could see without being involved.

Don Quixote rode back to the scattered scene. He seemed satisfied. He was breathing hard. His ribs hurt considerably from the effort. But he had done what a knight must do.

Then he heard a voice.

"You! Knight! You have broken my leg!"

Don Quixote looked around. On the ground, about twenty feet from the road, he saw a man sitting in the dirt, holding his leg. The man was wearing fine clothes under his robes. He was clearly a person of education and some importance.

Don Quixote rode over to him.

---

### Part Thirteen: The Scholar's Leg

"I am very sorry about your leg," said Don Quixote, looking down at the man on the ground. He sounded genuinely sorry. He was. He had not intended to hurt anyone. He had intended to free an enchanted knight.

"Sorry does not fix a broken leg!" said the scholar. He was in considerable pain. His name was Alonzo Lopez, and he was a man of learning. He could see, from his position on the ground, that the armored man above him was not entirely rational. He decided to speak carefully.

"Who are you?" he said, less angrily.

"I am Don Quixote of La Mancha," said the knight. "And who are you?"

"My name is Alonzo Lopez. I am a bachelor of arts. I was accompanying the body of my friend, a nobleman who died of a fever, back to his village. We were on a completely peaceful journey."

Don Quixote processed this information. A bachelor of arts was an educated man. The mourners were mourners. The coffin had contained a dead body. The procession had been exactly what it appeared to be.

A small cloud passed across Don Quixote's imagination.

But then it cleared, and the old certainty returned. Even if the procession had appeared normal, it was still possible that dark forces had been at work. Enchantments sometimes looked very ordinary. That was what made them so dangerous.

"I apologize for your leg," said Don Quixote again. "It was not my intention to hurt you. I was confused by the circumstances of the procession. In the dark, things are not always as they seem."

"In the light, they are usually clearer," said Alonzo Lopez dryly.

Sancho came forward. He looked at the scholar's leg. He was no doctor, but he could see it was broken. The bone had gone at the wrong angle.

"We should help him," Sancho said to Don Quixote. "He cannot stay on the ground."

Don Quixote dismounted. He helped Sancho gather some wood from the side of the road. They made a rough splint for the leg. This was not expert work. But it was done with genuine care. Don Quixote held the leg steady while Sancho bound the wood to it with strips of cloth.

Alonzo Lopez bit his lip and did not cry out, though it hurt very much.

"The others have all run away," said Sancho, looking around. The scattered mourners had not returned. The road was empty except for the dropped items and the coffin still lying in the middle.

"They will come back when they are sure the danger has passed," said Alonzo Lopez. He looked at Don Quixote. "Are you dangerous?"

"Not to innocent people," said Don Quixote seriously. "Only to evil ones."

"That is reassuring," said Alonzo Lopez, in a tone that suggested he was not entirely reassured.

---

### Part Fourteen: A New Name

While they waited for the mourners to return, Alonzo Lopez and Don Quixote talked. The scholar was in pain, but he was a man of words and ideas, and talking helped him forget the pain a little.

He asked Don Quixote about himself. Don Quixote told him. He described his life as a knight-errant. He described his horse Rocinante and his squire Sancho Panza. He described Dulcinea del Toboso and his devotion to her.

Alonzo Lopez listened carefully. He was an educated man and he could see that Don Quixote was not entirely sane. But there was something in the man that was difficult to dismiss. A sincerity. A genuine belief.

"You have frightened many innocent people tonight," said Alonzo Lopez.

"I regret that," said Don Quixote. He really did.

"The body you disturbed was a good man. He deserved a peaceful journey to his burial."

"I understand," said Don Quixote. His voice was quiet. "I made a mistake. It is one of the difficulties of this profession. In the dark, things that look like evil are sometimes just ordinary sadness."

There was a pause.

"What will you be called," said Alonzo Lopez, "from this night on? You have scattered a funeral procession and broken a scholar's leg. That deserves a name."

Don Quixote considered this. It was a reasonable observation. Great knights in the books often received new names or titles after memorable deeds, whether those deeds were triumphs or disasters.

"What would you call me?" he asked.

"The Knight of the Sorrowful Face," said Alonzo Lopez.

Sancho, who had been listening to all of this while eating a piece of bread he had found in his bag, looked up.

Don Quixote repeated the name quietly. "The Knight of the Sorrowful Face." He tried it again. "Don Quixote, the Knight of the Sorrowful Face."

He was quiet for a moment, thinking about whether he liked it.

"I accept this name," he said finally. "It is fitting. A knight who wanders the world correcting wrongs and fighting injustice is bound to wear a sorrowful face sometimes. The world is full of wrongs. That is a sorrowful thing."

Sancho looked at him. "You are also missing two teeth," he said. "That makes anyone's face sorrowful."

Don Quixote touched the gap in his teeth. "That too," he agreed.

---

### Part Fifteen: The Morning After the Procession

The mourners had eventually come back, cautiously, in small groups. They collected their dropped torches and their scattered belongings. They lifted the coffin carefully back onto their shoulders. They checked it had not been damaged. It had not. They gave Don Quixote and Sancho wide, wary looks. Then they continued down the road without speaking, moving quickly, and were soon gone.

Alonzo Lopez was helped by two of his companions, who had returned. They made a rough stretcher for him from tree branches and carried him. He was in pain and pale, but he would live. Before they left, he gave Don Quixote a long look.

"God protect you, sir knight," he said. There was a complicated mixture of feelings in his voice.

"And you," said Don Quixote. "I am truly sorry about your leg."

They parted ways. Don Quixote and Sancho continued along the road.

It was nearly dawn by now. The sky in the east was beginning to turn from black to deep blue. The stars were fading one by one.

"Sir," said Sancho, after a while. "That was a funeral."

"I know," said Don Quixote quietly.

"The coffin had a dead person in it."

"I know."

"Not an enchanted knight."

"I know that now."

There was a pause.

"Are there times," said Sancho carefully, "when things are just what they look like? When a funeral is a funeral and a flock of sheep is a flock of sheep?"

Don Quixote was quiet for a long time. He rode with his head slightly bowed. The night had been hard. His ribs still hurt. His teeth were still gone. He had hurt an innocent man and frightened a peaceful procession.

"Yes," he said finally. "There are such times."

Sancho was surprised to hear this. He waited.

"But I cannot always tell which times those are," continued Don Quixote. "That is the difficulty. Enchanters are very skilled. Their illusions are perfect. Sometimes the windmill is a giant. Sometimes it is a windmill. A knight-errant must be prepared to fight both. He cannot stop fighting just because he is sometimes wrong."

Sancho thought about this for a long time.

"That is actually quite a reasonable explanation," he said at last. "In a sort of difficult way."

"Everything about this profession is difficult," said Don Quixote. "I did not choose it because it was easy."

They rode on as the sun began to rise.

---

### Part Sixteen: Finding an Inn

They found an inn around midmorning. It was a real inn this time, not a castle of any kind, and Don Quixote was too tired and too sore to transform it into anything more grand.

The innkeeper was a woman of middle age with a practical face. She took one look at Don Quixote and immediately called for her daughter to bring clean water and cloth. She had seen injured travelers before. She knew what to do.

Don Quixote was put to bed in a room with a real bed, not straw on the floor. Sancho was given a cot in the same room. The innkeeper's daughter cleaned the wound on Don Quixote's face. She looked at his ribs and said they were probably bruised rather than broken, which was better news than Don Quixote had expected.

"Rest," said the innkeeper. "Two days at least."

"I cannot rest for two days," said Don Quixote. "There are adventures "

"Two days," said the innkeeper, in a tone that left no room for argument.

Don Quixote looked at her. She was a very practical woman. She reminded him, slightly, of his housekeeper back home. He found himself unable to argue.

"Two days," he agreed.

Sancho, from his cot, exhaled with great relief. He was asleep before Don Quixote had stopped talking.

Don Quixote lay back on his pillow. The ceiling of the room was ordinary wood and plaster. There were no tapestries or stone walls. It was an inn, not a castle. But the bed was soft and the room was quiet.

He thought about the Knight of the Sorrowful Face. His new name. He decided he liked it, despite how it had come about. A sorrowful face was an honest face. The world gave him reason for sorrow. The world was full of wrongs that he could not always correct, full of giants that turned into windmills, and armies that turned into sheep, and enchanted knights that turned out to be dead noblemen in coffins.

The world was harder than the books had promised.

But the books had also promised that the good knight persists. Through failure and defeat and pain and embarrassment, the true knight persists.

He would persist.

He closed his eyes.

Tomorrow, or the day after, he would get back on Rocinante. He would ride out onto the road again. He would face whatever came next.

And whatever came next would be an adventure. One way or another.

He slept.

### Part Seventeen: What Sancho Wrote Home

Sancho did not write home. He could not write. But on the second day at the inn, while Don Quixote was resting, Sancho talked to another traveler who was passing through. The traveler was going in the direction of Sancho's village. Sancho gave him a message to deliver to his wife Teresa.

The message was this: "Tell her I am well. Tell her I am on a great adventure. Tell her I hope to be the governor of an island before too long. Tell her the master is very determined and sometimes gets hurt, but he always gets up again. Tell her not to worry. Tell her I will come home when the adventures are done."

The traveler looked at him. "You are a squire?" he said.

"I am," said Sancho, with some dignity.

"To that fellow in the room upstairs? With the armor?"

"Yes."

The traveler thought about this. "Good luck to you," he said, and went on his way.

Sancho watched him go. Then he went back inside and ate some bread and cheese, because the inn provided lunch and he was not going to miss it.

Don Quixote slept for most of the two days. When he was awake, he was quiet and thoughtful. He did not talk very much about the funeral procession or the sheep. He spent time looking at the ceiling and thinking.

Once, he called Sancho to him and said: "Sancho. The name that scholar gave me. The Knight of the Sorrowful Face. I want you to use it from now on. When people ask who I am."

"Yes, sir," said Sancho.

"Don Quixote of La Mancha, Knight of the Sorrowful Face."

"I will remember it."

Don Quixote was quiet again. Then: "Do I really have a sorrowful face?"

Sancho considered this honestly. "A little," he said. "Especially since the teeth."

Don Quixote smiled, a slightly lopsided smile because of the missing teeth. "Ah well," he said.

He closed his eyes again.

**What happened in this chapter?**

- Don Quixote and Sancho camped with goatherds and Don Quixote gave a speech about the Golden Age.
- Don Quixote saw two flocks of sheep and believed they were two great armies preparing for battle.
- He charged into the flock, injured several sheep, and was hit by sling stones from the angry shepherds.

- Don Quixote thought it was an enchanted knight and attacked the procession.

- Alonzo Lopez gave Don Quixote the name "The Knight of the Sorrowful Face."
- They found an inn and rested for two days.

**Think about it:**
- Don Quixote's speech about the Golden Age shows that he believes in a better world. Is this a kind of madness, or a kind of wisdom?

- Why do you think Alonzo Lopez gave Don Quixote the name "Knight of the Sorrowful Face"? Was he being cruel, or was he being honest?
