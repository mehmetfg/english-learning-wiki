## Scene 1 The Preparation

He did not sleep that night, or rather he slept in the way that is not really sleeping lying flat on his back, eyes closed, mind moving continuously through the same dark passages. He was aware of every sound in the building. The creak of the staircase when the wind moved through the hallway. A door somewhere below him, opening and closing. A cat on the roof. The slow, regular drip of something water, somewhere that he had never noticed before.

When the grey light of early morning came through his window, he was already sitting up.

He had been thinking about the preparation. Every detail of it. In the night, while pretending to sleep, he had worked through everything in his mind the way a carpenter measures wood before cutting measuring twice, measuring three times, wanting no mistakes.

There was one problem he had not solved. The most important practical problem.

The axe.

He needed an axe. He had thought about a knife it was simpler, easier to carry, easier to hide. But he had decided against a knife. A knife required closeness and steadiness and a kind of controlled force that he was not certain he could maintain in the moment when the moment came. An axe was definitive. An axe ended things quickly, completely. He needed something that would not require him to think once he had begun.

He could not buy one he had almost no money and a student purchasing an axe would be remembered. He could not carry one up the stairs from his own room too visible, too obvious, someone might see him on the staircase.

But there was a porter in his building. The porter was a man named Nastasya's uncle no, that was wrong, he confused the details the porter was a man who kept various tools and household objects in a small cupboard near the entrance. Raskolnikov had seen the cupboard open once. He had seen the handle of an axe inside it.

The porter left very early in the morning to help at the market. This was known. He left before six and did not return until noon. The cupboard was not always locked sometimes it was open, sometimes not.

This was the chance.

He got up. He put on his coat. He had made a modification to the coat two days earlier a small loop of thread sewn inside the lining, on the left side, just below the breast pocket. The loop was exactly the right size to hold the handle of an axe securely and invisibly. He had thought of this himself. He was pleased with this detail, in the way a person can be pleased by a practical solution even in the middle of something dark.

He went quietly down the stairs. He reached the bottom. He looked toward the porter's door. It was closed. He listened. No sound from inside.

He tried the cupboard. It opened.

The axe was there.

He took it. He placed the handle in the loop inside his coat. He felt the weight of it settle against his side. He closed the cupboard carefully. He went back upstairs to his room without making a sound.

He sat on his bed and held very still for a moment. His heart was beating fast. He waited until it slowed.

It was just after six o'clock in the morning. He had until evening.

He needed to wait. Waiting was the hardest part. He had always known, in some part of his mind, that the waiting would be the hardest part. The action itself would last only minutes. But the waiting the hours between the decision and the moment those would be the longest hours of his life.

He lay down on the bed. He stared at the ceiling. He made himself breathe slowly and regularly.

He thought about nothing. He tried to think about nothing. He was not entirely successful.

## Scene 2 The Crossing

The hours passed with terrible slowness. He lay on his bed and the morning moved around him like something solid like water that he was submerged in, thick and difficult to move through. He heard his building wake up around him: Nastasya in the kitchen below, the sound of a door, footsteps on the stairs, someone's voice briefly loud and then quiet again.

He did not eat. There was nothing to eat, and he did not want anything. His stomach felt closed, as if his body had decided to concentrate all its energy elsewhere and had sent hunger away on an errand.

At some point in the late morning he slept for perhaps an hour a genuine sleep, surprisingly and woke feeling no better and no worse. He lay and waited. He was not afraid, exactly. He was something different from afraid. He was in a state of extreme suspension, like a stone at the top of an arc, the moment before gravity takes it downward.

At seven o'clock in the evening he stood up.

He checked the axe. He put on his hat. He looked around the room once, at the bed and the table and the window and the torn wallpaper, as if making a record of it. Then he went out.

The evening was warm. The city at seven o'clock in July had a particular quality the worst of the day's heat had passed but the air still held it, like a room that has been hot all day and takes a long time to cool. The streets were busy. This was good. He needed people around him. A man walking alone in an empty street was noticeable. A man walking in a crowd was invisible.

He walked steadily, neither too fast nor too slow. He kept his eyes forward and slightly down, in the manner of a person going somewhere specific, not lingering, not looking at shop windows, not available for conversation. He passed through two streets, then a third. He crossed the canal bridge. He turned into the side street.

The building was ahead of him.

He slowed slightly without meaning to. His body was doing things without asking his mind. He noticed this with a part of his mind that remained cool and observing even now the part of him that had been watching himself for months, examining himself, asking: *are you capable of this?* That part was still watching. Still making notes.

He went into the courtyard. He did not look up at the windows. He had learned from his previous visit that looking up at windows was something guilty people did people who were checking whether anyone could see them. He looked at the ground. He walked to the door. He began to climb.

The staircase was the same as before. He had climbed it only once, on his preparation visit, but he had studied it carefully then and he remembered it completely. The third step creaked on the left side. The landing between the second and third floors had a broken window that let in light. The fourth floor was at the top twelve steps more from the third-floor landing.

He counted as he climbed. Not because counting was necessary but because counting gave his mind something to hold onto while the rest of him kept moving upward.

He reached the fourth floor. He stopped in the hallway outside her door.

It was quiet. From somewhere in the building, very faint, there was the sound of a radio no, there were no radios in 1865, he reminded himself automatically, as if the thought came from someone else there was the sound of music, a stringed instrument, someone practising something. Very faint. It stopped. Then silence.

He waited thirty seconds. He heard nothing from behind her door.

He raised his hand and knocked.

## Scene 3 The Door

There was a long pause after his knock. Long enough that he thought: she is not home. She has gone out. It is over before it began. He felt something that was neither relief nor disappointment something more neutral than either, the feeling of a machine that has been set running and now has nothing to run against.

Then he heard the shuffling sound. The careful footsteps. The silence of someone standing on the other side of the door.

He kept his face composed. He thought about his face about what it looked like from the outside, what expression it was making. He had practiced this. He wanted to look like a young man with something to pawn. Ordinary. Expected.

The chain was unfastened. The bolts were drawn. The door opened.

She looked at him. Those bird's eyes, sharp and watchful. She recognised him he saw this he had been here before and she had a good memory for faces.

"Good evening," he said. His voice was steady. He was surprised by how steady it was. "I have brought you something else. A silver watch. Quite good quality."

She looked at him for another moment. Then she stepped back to let him in.

The room was exactly as he remembered it. The furniture in the same places. The chest near the wall. The shelf with the small objects. On the table there was a book open she had been reading, or perhaps she had fallen asleep over it and a lamp that gave out a yellow, slightly unsteady light.

She was alone. He registered this. The sister Lizaveta was not here.

"Let me see it," she said. Her voice was the same thin, high voice. She held out one dry hand.

He reached inside his coat. His right hand found the object he had prepared not a watch, there was no watch, he had prepared a piece of wood wrapped in paper to look like something packaged. He held it out. She reached for it.

And then everything happened very fast.

He had thought, during all the months of planning, that the moment itself would feel slow. He had read accounts of such things in books, in newspapers and people always described the moment of action as slow, as expanded, as larger than ordinary time. It was not. It was fast. It was faster than anything he had experienced.

She took the package and turned toward the lamp to see it better. Her back was half toward him. The lamp was on the table and she was moving toward it and her back was turned and he was already reaching inside his coat the other side, the left side and his hand found the handle.

He was behind her. He did not think. He had decided a very long time ago that when the moment came he would not think, because thinking was exactly what would stop him, and he had been right about this, because the part of him that thought was not present in this moment only the part of him that moved.

He raised his arm.

She turned her head. Just slightly. As if she had heard something some change in the air behind her. Her sharp bird's eye, on the edge of her vision, caught a movement.

It was too late.

The room went very quiet.

## Scene 4 The Search

He did not look at what was behind him. He turned away immediately and went to the chest near the wall.

His hands were working before his mind had given any instruction. He had thought about this part too during the months of planning, he had spent as much time on what came after as on the act itself, because he knew that the act would be fast and that he would need to move quickly and clearly in the time that followed. The mind might stop. The hands must not.

The chest had a lock. He knew this. He had studied the lock on his preparation visit a simple padlock, old, the kind that sometimes opened without the key if you lifted the lid at a particular angle while pressing the front of the lock. He had thought about bringing tools. He had decided against it too much to carry, too many things to go wrong.

He tried the lid. It was locked firmly. He straightened up and looked around the room. The keys. Where were the keys?

He went to the body. He did not look at the face. He looked only at the waist, where a long chain hung with several keys attached. He unfastened the chain. His hands were shaking now he noticed this as a fact, without alarm, the way you notice that it has started to rain. The shaking did not stop him.

He found the right key on the third attempt. The chest opened.

Inside, there was more than he had expected. Much more. The chest was deep and it was full small boxes, wrapped packages, velvet pouches that felt heavy in his hand, paper envelopes held closed with string. He had brought a bag small, cloth, sewn inside his coat in the same way as the loop and he began to put things into it. He did not stop to look at what he was taking. He took everything small that felt heavy. Rings. Watches. Small boxes. He worked as quickly as he could.

He was aware of time. Every second was a second in which someone could knock at the door, or a sound from the stairs could announce a visitor, or something could go wrong in one of the hundred ways that he had listed and memorised during his months of planning. He worked fast. He did not think about anything except the next object and the next object and the next object.

He found the money in a paper envelope at the bottom of the chest. He did not count it. He put it in the bag.

He was almost finished. He straightened up and looked around the room to see whether he had missed anything visible. He had not.

And then he heard the sound on the stairs.

He froze.

It was the sound of footsteps slow, ordinary footsteps, someone climbing without any urgency. He stood completely still in the middle of the room and listened. The footsteps continued up the staircase and stopped at the landing just outside the door.

Then a knock.

He did not move. He hardly breathed.

The knock came again louder this time. Then a voice. A woman's voice, simple and direct.

"Alyona Ivanovna? It's Lizaveta. Are you there? I've brought the washing."

He stood in the room with the bag in his hand and thought nothing at all. His mind was entirely empty for a long moment as empty as a room with all the furniture removed.

Lizaveta. The sister. She had come back.

He heard her at the door. Then the door which had not been properly latched when he had come in swung open.

She was a large woman, larger than her sister, with a broad, plain face and wide eyes. She was carrying a bundle of folded linen under one arm. She saw him immediately. Her eyes went to him and then past him, to the floor near the table. Her face changed. Her mouth opened.

She had no time to make a sound.

He did not want to think about what happened next. Later much later he would think about it, and it would be the part of the night that he could never put away, the part that would not stay at a distance, the part that returned to him most clearly and most often. Not the other part. This part. Lizaveta's wide eyes. Lizaveta's open mouth. Lizaveta, who had never hurt anyone, who was simple and good Marmeladov had said so, someone had said so who had simply come at the wrong time, carrying the washing, carrying the folded linen under her arm.

He took the bag. He checked the room once more. He went to the door.

## Scene 5 The Escape

He stood inside the door and listened.

The staircase was silent. But it was the silence of a place that might stop being silent at any moment the silence of a city building in the evening, where people went up and down constantly, where the next set of footsteps could come at any second.

He had to go down. But he could not go down while the staircase was occupied. He had to wait for a clear moment.

He stood just inside the door and listened. He held the bag with both hands. His coat was buttoned. He looked ordinary he had to believe this, he had to hold onto the idea that he looked ordinary, that his face was composed, that there was nothing about the way he stood that could tell a story.

From below, he heard sounds. Two male voices workmen, from the sound of them, talking easily about something, climbing together. He heard them pass the third floor, then the fourth floor landing his floor and continue up to the fifth. A door opened and closed. Silence again.

He waited another full minute. He counted the seconds.

Then he opened the door and went out onto the landing.

He went down quickly but without running. Running on the stairs was the thing a frightened person did. He was not frightened. He was functioning. There was a difference, and the difference was important.

He reached the third floor. Then the second. On the first floor landing, he nearly stopped. Nearly. There was a door open on the first floor wide open and inside the room a man was standing with his back to the hallway, talking to someone that Raskolnikov could not see. He kept walking. He walked past the open door in three steps and reached the ground floor and went through the entrance and out into the courtyard.

The evening air hit him.

He crossed the courtyard. He went out through the main entrance into the street. He turned left and began to walk. Not running. Walking. He was a person going somewhere. He had always been a person going somewhere.

He turned the first corner. He turned the second corner. He went down a street that ran parallel to the canal. He was putting distance between himself and the building, street by street, and the streets were busy with evening people families returning from somewhere, workers, students and he was among them and indistinguishable from them and this was the plan, this was exactly the plan, this had always been the plan.

But something was wrong.

He knew something was wrong before he understood what it was. The knowledge arrived first as a physical sensation a heaviness in his legs, a buzzing in his ears, a strange difficulty in understanding what his eyes were telling him. The sounds of the street became very loud and then very distant and then very loud again.

He was going to faint. He understood this with the cool, detached part of his mind. He was going to faint in the street.

He did not faint. He kept walking. He breathed carefully. He focused on the stones of the road in front of him, on putting one foot after the other, on the simple and achievable task of the next step and the next step and the next step. Slowly, slowly, the heaviness receded. The buzzing faded. The street sounds normalised.

He kept walking.

He did not take the direct route back to his building. He had planned this too he went in a longer arc, through streets that avoided the main routes, that went around the market and along the back of a row of warehouses and through a small square where a fountain stood dry and inactive in the summer heat. He added twenty minutes to his journey. These were the most important twenty minutes.

He reached his street. He reached his building. He went in through the main door and up the stairs the familiar dark staircase, the familiar smell of dust and wood and he went into his room and closed the door behind him.

He stood in the middle of the room.

He was home.

He had done it. He had come back. He was standing in his room and the walls were around him and the ceiling was low above him and the bed was in the corner and everything was where it had always been. He was here.

He looked at his hands. They were shaking badly now. He had not allowed them to shake during the walk but now they shook completely, as if they had been waiting for permission.

He went to the table. He put the bag down on it. He needed to hide the things. He needed to do this now, before anything else. He could not leave them visible.

But first he looked at his coat. There was blood on it. A small amount, but visible if you were looking for it. He took the coat off. He needed to deal with the coat. He would wash it no, washing might not be enough he would hide it. He would deal with it in the morning.

He was thinking in small pieces. This was deliberate. He could not allow himself to think in large pieces right now. Large pieces would be too large. Small pieces were manageable. The coat. The bag. The axe he needed to put the axe back. He had forgotten the axe was still in his coat. He took it out and looked at it.

He went to the window. He looked out at the street below. Ordinary people, ordinary evening, ordinary city.

He went to the bed and sat down. He held the axe across his knees and stared at the wall. He remained like this for a very long time not thinking, not sleeping, not moving. Simply existing in the room, in the evening, in the aftermath of the largest thing he had ever done.

At some point, he lay down. He did not take off his boots. He did not cover himself. He lay on his back with his eyes open and stared at the ceiling.

The ceiling was grey and low and familiar. He had stared at it hundreds of times before.

It looked exactly the same as it always had.

This was the strangest thing of all.

## Chapter Summary

**What happened in Chapter 3:**

Raskolnikov spent the night preparing. In the early morning he took an axe from the porter's cupboard and hid it in a loop inside his coat. He waited all day the longest hours of his life. In the evening he crossed the city to Alyona Ivanovna's building, climbed the stairs, and knocked at her door. She let him in. He acted quickly. He searched the chest and took the money and the valuables. Then Lizaveta the pawnbroker's gentle sister arrived unexpectedly. He could not leave a witness. He hid on the staircase while two workmen passed, then walked out of the building and through the city. He nearly fainted in the street but kept going. He reached his room, closed the door, and lay on his bed staring at the ceiling. The ceiling looked exactly the same as always.

**Key events:**
- The axe taken from the porter's cupboard using a prepared loop inside the coat
- The preparation visit is confirmed he knew the apartment, the lock, the layout
- Alyona Ivanovna killed with the axe
- Lizaveta the unexpected witness who arrived at the wrong moment
- The escape he walked calmly through the evening city, adding twenty minutes to avoid main routes
- The aftermath he arrives home, sits on his bed, and stares at the unchanged ceiling

**Key theme:**
The chapter is built on a terrible irony: Raskolnikov planned everything perfectly the axe, the loop, the route, the timing but the one thing he did not plan for was Lizaveta. She was the part of the plan that did not exist in his theory. His theory spoke of a useless life against useful ones. Lizaveta was not useless. She was, by all accounts, gentle and good. And she died because she arrived at the wrong time carrying clean linen.
