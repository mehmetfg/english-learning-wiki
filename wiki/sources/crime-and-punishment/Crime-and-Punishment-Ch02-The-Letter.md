# Crime and Punishment
## Chapter 2 The Letter
### Extended A2/B1 Reader | ~10,000 words

---

*Previously in Chapter 1: Raskolnikov is a young ex-student living in extreme poverty in Saint Petersburg. He has been thinking for months about a terrible plan something he believes only certain people are capable of doing. He visited a pawnbroker named Alyona Ivanovna to observe her apartment. He met a ruined man called Marmeladov, whose daughter Sonya sacrifices herself so her family can eat. That night, lying in his small dark room, Raskolnikov made a decision. He is going to carry out the plan.*

## Scene 1 Morning in the Garret

He did not sleep well. He never slept well. But this night had been worse than usual he moved in and out of sleep like a person crossing between two rooms, never fully entering either one. He would close his eyes and the darkness behind them would fill with shapes: the pawnbroker's sharp bird's eyes, Marmeladov's large shaking hands, the face of the girl on the bench by the embankment.

When he finally woke properly, the room was full of grey morning light. It was early the sounds from the street below were still the quiet sounds of the first hour, the slow sounds of a city that had not yet fully begun. A cart on the stones. A dog barking somewhere far away. The distant bell of a church.

He lay still for a moment, looking at the ceiling. Then he remembered.

The thought came back immediately, the way a tooth that has been hurting reminds you of itself the moment you wake up. He remembered his decision of the previous night. He turned it over in his mind in the cold morning light. It did not look different. It did not look smaller or less serious than it had at midnight. If anything, in the clear morning air, it looked more definite. More real.

He sat up.

On the table near the door, there was something he had not noticed the night before. A letter. It was propped against the candle holder. It was addressed to him in a handwriting he knew immediately the careful, slightly uneven handwriting of his mother. The letters were formed with great effort, as if each one cost something.

He stared at it for a moment without moving. Letters from home always cost him something too.

He got up and took the letter. He sat down on the edge of the bed and held it in both hands. The envelope was worn at the corners it had travelled a long way to reach him. He could see where it had been folded and refolded. He turned it over. His mother had sealed it carefully.

He opened it.

The letter was long. It was written on both sides of several sheets of paper. The handwriting was small and dense his mother had used every centimetre of the paper, because paper cost money and she had very little of it. In places, where she had pressed the pen too hard, the ink had bled through to the other side.

He began to read.

### 📖 Vocabulary Scene 1

**propped** *(verb, past prop)*
Placed against something to keep it upright and prevent it from falling. If you prop something against a wall, you lean it there.
*"The letter was propped against the candle holder, waiting for him to find it."*

**definite** *(adjective)*
Clear, certain, and without any doubt. When something feels definite, it feels real and fixed it is not going to change.
*"In the morning light, the decision looked more definite not a late-night thought, but a real plan."*

**dense** *(adjective)*
Packed closely together, leaving very little empty space. Dense handwriting is very small and close together, with very little space between lines.
*"The handwriting was small and dense she had used every centimetre of the paper."*

**bled through** *(verb phrase bleed through)*
When ink is too heavy, it passes through the paper to the other side and makes marks there. "Bleed" in this sense has nothing to do with blood it just means a liquid spreading where it should not.
*"In places where she had pressed the pen too hard, the ink had bled through to the back of the page."*

**worn** *(adjective)*
Damaged from a lot of use. When something is worn, you can see that it has been handled many times it looks old and tired.
*"The corners of the envelope were worn the letter had been on a long journey."*

**dense** *(adjective)*
→ *See above*

**uneven** *(adjective)*
→ *See Chapter 1, Scene 1*

**envelope** *(noun)*
A flat paper container that you put a letter inside and seal before sending it.
*"He turned the envelope over and saw that his mother had sealed it very carefully."*

**centimetre** *(noun)*
A unit of measurement one hundredth of a metre. Used here to show that the mother left no empty space at all on the paper.
*"She had used every centimetre of the paper, writing in tiny letters to fit as much as possible."*

Moving repeatedly between sleeping and being awake, without properly doing either. Used to describe a night of poor, broken sleep.
*"He moved in and out of sleep all night, never resting properly."*

## Scene 2 What the Letter Said

*My dearest Rodya,*

*It has been three weeks since your last letter, and I confess that your silence has caused me much worry. Your sister Dunya and I have been thinking of you constantly. I hope this letter finds you in good health, though I know from your previous letters that the situation in the city has been difficult. I send you what I can it is not much, but it is given with all my love.*

*I must tell you about something that has happened here. I want to tell you honestly, because I know you prefer honesty to comfortable lies. We have had a very hard few months, and I think you deserve to know the truth.*

*You know that Dunya was working as a governess for a family here the Svidrigailov family. Arkady Ivanovich Svidrigailov is a man of some importance in this district. His wife, Marfa Petrovna, hired your sister to look after their children and to teach them French and music. The pay was not generous, but it was steady, and Dunya was grateful for the work. She sent us a little money each month, which helped us considerably.*

*What I have to tell you now is difficult.*

*During her time in that household, Svidrigailov began to pay your sister a kind of attention that was not appropriate. He spoke to her privately on several occasions. He said things to her that a respectable man should not say to a young woman in his employment. Dunya behaved correctly throughout she kept her dignity, she did not encourage him, and she told no one at first because she did not want to cause trouble for the family. She thought she could manage the situation herself.*

*But then Marfa Petrovna found out. And instead of believing Dunya, she chose to believe her husband. She dismissed your sister immediately and told everyone in the district what she claimed had happened not the truth, mind you, but her own version of events, which painted Dunya as the guilty party.*

*You can imagine what this did to Dunya's reputation. In a small town, reputation is everything. She could not find another position. People looked at her differently. Some of her former acquaintances stopped greeting her in the street.*

*However, Rodya, I have something good to tell you as well. Something has happened that I believe will solve all of our difficulties.*

*A man named Pyotr Petrovich Luzhin has asked for Dunya's hand in marriage.*

Raskolnikov stopped reading.

He read that sentence again.

He held the letter tightly and read further.

*Mr. Luzhin is a respectable man of about forty-five. He is a lawyer with a good position and a comfortable income. He is going to Saint Petersburg soon on business he plans to establish himself there permanently and he wishes to take Dunya with him as his wife. He has been very correct in his approach. He has spoken to me personally. He is not a romantic man, I will not pretend otherwise, but he is decent and he is serious about Dunya's welfare.*

*Rodya, I know what you are thinking. You are thinking that she is marrying him for the money. You are thinking that this is a sacrifice. I want to tell you that it is not as simple as that. Dunya has thought carefully. She has made her own choice. She is not a child. She understands what kind of man Luzhin is and she has decided that a steady, respectable life is better than what she has now.*

*Also and I tell you this only because I know you Mr. Luzhin has mentioned that he may be able to help you. To find you a position. To assist you in your career. He knows people in the city. He is a man of influence.*

*We are coming to Saint Petersburg soon, Rodya. Dunya and I are coming. We will see you. Everything will be different.*

*Your loving mother,*
*Pulcheria Alexandrovna Raskolnikova*

He put the letter down on the bed beside him. He sat very still for a long time.

Then he stood up and went to the window. He looked down at the street below. A woman was carrying a large basket. Two men were talking near a doorpost. A child ran across the road.

His hands, he noticed, were shaking slightly. He pressed them flat against the window frame.

*A steady, respectable life.*

He repeated the phrase in his mind. He turned it over. He understood exactly what it meant. He had read the letter carefully and he had understood every word his mother had carefully chosen, and every word she had carefully avoided.

His sister was going to marry a man she did not love. She was going to marry a man forty-five years old, a lawyer, careful and correct and without warmth, because he had money and a position and connections in the city. She was doing this because of him because Raskolnikov had failed his studies, had stopped paying his fees, had allowed himself to sink into this grey poverty at the top of a dark staircase. She was selling herself to fix the problem that he had become.

*She is marrying him for the money.* His mother had written: *I know what you are thinking.* Yes. He was thinking exactly that. And his mother knew it, and she had written the letter anyway, because what was the alternative? To refuse Luzhin and keep suffering? To watch Dunya work as a governess until some other respectable family accused her of something and destroyed what little reputation she had left?

He thought of Sonya. He thought of Sonya in her doorway, handing in money and then going away. He thought of Marmeladov saying: *she does it for us. She never complains.*

He thought: there is always someone who pays. There is always a sister or a daughter who pays the price that the men in the family cannot or will not pay. And it is never called a sacrifice when men do it. It is only a sacrifice when women do it.

He was so angry that he had to sit down.

### 📖 Vocabulary Scene 2

**governess** *(noun)*
A woman who is employed to live in a family's house and teach the children. In the 19th century, this was one of the few respectable jobs available to educated women from poor families.
*"Dunya worked as a governess, teaching the children French and music."*

**district** *(noun)*
A region or area, usually an administrative division of a town or country. People who live in the same district know each other's business.
*"Svidrigailov was a man of some importance in their district."*

**appropriate** *(adjective)*
Suitable and correct for a particular situation. Behaviour that is not appropriate is wrong or unacceptable in that context.
*"He spoke to her in ways that were not appropriate for a man in his position."*

**employment** *(noun)*
The state of having a job and being paid for work. Your employment is your job and your relationship with your employer.
*"A respectable man should not say such things to a young woman in his employment."*

**reputation** *(noun)*
The opinion that people in general have about someone what people say and think about you. A good reputation is hard to build and easy to destroy.
*"Marfa Petrovna told everyone in the district, and Dunya's reputation was ruined."*

A person you know but who is not a close friend. An acquaintance is someone you might greet in the street and speak to briefly.
*"Some of her former acquaintances stopped greeting her in the street."*

**income** *(noun)*
The money that a person earns or receives regularly, for example from a job or business.
*"Luzhin was a lawyer with a good position and a comfortable income."*

**influence** *(noun)*
The power to affect how people think or what they decide. A person of influence knows important people and can use those connections to help or hurt others.
*"Luzhin was a man of influence he knew people in the city."*

**sacrifice** *(noun)*
When you give up something important or valuable so that someone else can benefit, or so that something larger can be achieved.
*"Raskolnikov understood that his sister's marriage was a sacrifice made for him."*

## Scene 3 The Walk

He needed to go out. He could not stay in the room with that letter. The room was too small and the thoughts inside him were too large and together they would crush him.

He put on his coat and his terrible hat and went down the stairs without caring about whether his landlady would see him. He went out into the street and began to walk.

The morning had turned into a hot, flat, white day. The kind of day in Saint Petersburg in summer that gives you no shadow anywhere. The sun sat directly above and pressed everything down. The smell of the city was worse in this kind of heat every smell concentrated, every bad thing doubled.

He walked fast. He did not choose a direction. His legs moved and he followed them. He crossed the canal bridge and went along the embankment and then turned into the wider streets near the market. He passed stalls selling bread and fish and vegetables. He passed a church. He passed a group of children who were chasing something in the dust. He saw none of them properly.

He was thinking about Luzhin.

He had formed a picture of the man from his mother's letter. A lawyer of forty-five. Correct and decent. A man of influence. He could see him perfectly without having met him. He had seen men like Luzhin his whole life men with careful coats and careful manners and careful words, men whose everything was calculated, who measured out their kindness the way a merchant measures out expensive goods. Men who did not love but who acquired. Who did not give but who invested. Who did not marry but who arranged.

Luzhin would come to Saint Petersburg with Dunya on his arm. He would establish himself in his profession. He would go to dinner parties and speak in careful sentences. And Dunya would sit beside him and pour tea for his guests and smile at the right moments and thank him for the life he had given her. And at night she would be his wife, this careful, humourless man, and she would have agreed to this freely, and his mother had written the word *freely*, and he thought: what does it mean to choose freely when all your other choices have been taken away?

He sat down on a low wall near the market and pressed his hands together between his knees.

He thought about the theory. His theory. The one he had been developing for months, the one that had appeared first as a thought in the night and had grown until it filled every part of his mind.

The theory said: there are two kinds of people in the world. The ordinary kind, and the extraordinary kind. The ordinary kind live by the rules. They follow the law. They accept what they are given. They suffer and they do not question the reason for their suffering. They are the majority. They are necessary without them, nothing would be stable. They are, in a sense, the material from which history is made.

But then there is the other kind. The extraordinary kind. These are the people who see beyond the ordinary rules. These are the Napoleons, the great commanders, the discoverers, the builders of new worlds. These people do not ask whether an action is permitted. They ask whether it serves a great purpose. And if it does, they act. And history judges them not by what they did, but by what they achieved.

Napoleon crossed a border. Napoleon killed people. Napoleon broke laws. But history calls him great because what he built was large enough to justify what he broke.

Raskolnikov had written this idea down once, in an article. He had sent it to a small journal. He thought they had probably thrown it away without reading it. But the idea was real. The idea was true. He was certain of it.

The question was only: which kind was he?

He sat on the low wall in the hot white light and asked himself this question honestly, the way he always asked himself questions without sympathy, without mercy, with the cold precision of a man examining a wound. Was he extraordinary? Did he have the capacity that his theory required? Or was he, despite all his intelligence, despite all his reading and his thinking simply ordinary?

He did not know. And not knowing was the worst thing of all.

He got up and began to walk again.

## Scene 4 The Dream

He walked until he was tired and then he went back to his room. He did not eat anything. He lay down on the bed without taking off his coat and almost immediately in the way that happens when the body is exhausted but the mind has gone too far he fell into a deep and troubled sleep.

And he dreamed.

In the dream, he was a child. He knew this immediately, the way you know things in dreams without being told he felt small, his legs were short, his hand was inside a larger hand, and the hand belonged to his father. His father was walking with him on a road outside a town, a road he remembered from childhood, a road that led past a tavern and a church and then out into open countryside.

It was evening in the dream. The light was low and golden and long. The road was dusty. And on the road ahead of them, there was a crowd.

He and his father moved toward the crowd because that is what you do in dreams you move toward what frightens you, not away from it.

The crowd was gathered around a cart. The cart was old and heavy and it was being pulled by a very old horse a small, thin horse with ribs that showed through its dull coat, a horse that had clearly been working too hard for too many years. Several men were already sitting in the cart too many men for a horse so small and so tired. One man was standing up in the cart, holding the reins and shouting at the horse.

"Go!" the man was shouting. He was a large man, red-faced, clearly drunk. "Go! Pull! What is wrong with you?"

The horse could not move. The horse stood with its legs slightly apart and its head low and its eyes half closed. It was not being stubborn. It was simply at the end of what it could do.

The crowd laughed. Some of the people in the crowd shouted things. The drunk man in the cart shouted more. And then he took the whip.

The child Raskolnikov the dream-child watched. He wanted to look away. He could not look away. He stood next to his father on the edge of the crowd and he watched the man in the cart bring the whip down on the horse's back. Once. Twice. The horse's body moved under the blows, absorbing them. It made a sound, a low, helpless sound. Its legs trembled.

"More men!" the drunk man shouted. More men climbed into the cart, laughing. The horse's legs were shaking now, shaking badly, the small body pressed impossibly low under the weight.

The whip came down again. And again. And again.

The child Raskolnikov broke away from his father's hand. He ran forward through the crowd, pushing between people's legs and bodies. He reached the horse. He put his small arms around the horse's neck. He pressed his face against the horse's face. The horse's eye was large and dark and wet. It looked at him. It saw him. It was not a stupid eye there was something in it that was fully alive and fully aware of what was happening to it.

He felt the blows coming down through the horse's body. He pressed himself against it. He could not stop the blows. He could not do anything. He was a child and he was too small and there were too many men and the man with the whip was too large and too drunk and too certain of his right to do what he was doing.

The horse went down.

It went down slowly, the way large things fall not all at once, but in stages, each stage more final than the last. First the front legs folded. Then the whole body tilted. Then the horse lay in the dust of the road and did not get up.

The crowd made a sound.

The child stood next to the horse in the dust. He was crying. He did not know when he had started crying. He looked at the horse's large dark eye, still open, and he understood with the absolute certainty of a dream that the horse was dead.

He woke up.

He sat up on his bed, gasping. The room was full of dim afternoon light. His coat was damp with sweat. His cheeks were wet. He had been crying in his sleep.

He sat on the edge of the bed and pressed his face into his hands. He breathed slowly. His heart was beating too fast.

He sat like that for a long time. The sounds of the street below came up through the window ordinary sounds, the sounds of a city that did not know about his dream.

When he lifted his face from his hands, his first thought was clear and immediate: *I cannot do it.*

He said it aloud, quietly, to the empty room: "I cannot do it."

He felt something release inside him something that had been wound very tight for a very long time. A loosening. A relief so strong it felt almost physical. *I cannot do it. It was a terrible idea. I am not capable of it. I am not Napoleon. I am not extraordinary. I am an ordinary man with terrible ideas and I cannot do it and I will not.*

He stood up. He went to the window. The afternoon was still hot and white. Below him, life continued without him.

He felt, for the first time in months, something like peace.

Taking in or receiving something without letting it pass further. When a body absorbs a blow, it receives the impact into itself.

Moved so that one side was higher than the other; leaned sideways. When something tilts, it loses its upright position.

The feeling of something being set free or loosened after being held tight for a long time. Relief can feel like a physical release.

## Scene 5 The Overheard Conversation

He put on his hat and went out. He did not have a clear destination. He wanted to walk, to breathe, to feel the small peace that had come to him in the room after the dream and to see whether it would survive contact with the city.

It did not entirely survive. But it did not entirely leave either.

He walked for a while along streets he knew, past buildings he had passed a hundred times. He thought about his sister's letter it was still in his coat pocket but he thought about it more calmly now, as if the dream had drained something out of him and left a quieter space behind.

He would write to his mother. He would tell her what he thought about Luzhin calmly, without anger, with reasons. He would tell her that Dunya should not marry a man she did not respect simply because he had money. He would find some other way. He would go back to his studies. He would find work. There were things he could do. He had not tried all of them yet.

He found himself near a market square that had a tavern on its far side. It was a low building with several tables outside on the pavement. He was thirsty. He had some small coins in his pocket. He sat down at one of the outside tables and ordered a drink and sat watching the square.

Two men sat at the table next to his. They had been there before him and they had drinks in front of them that were nearly finished. They were talking. They were not talking quietly.

He did not listen at first. He was looking at the square. He was thinking.

Then one word caught his attention. A name.

The name was Alyona Ivanovna.

He became very still. He did not turn his head. He stared at the square in front of him and let his hearing focus on the table beside him.

"The old woman," one of the men was saying. He was young a student, from the sound of him. "I went there last week. She gave me almost nothing for the ring. A quality silver ring. She gave me practically nothing."

"That's how she works," said the other man. He was older. "She's been doing it for twenty years. Takes everything, gives nothing. Half the students in this part of the city have left something with her."

"It's a crime," said the student. "Honestly. What she does is basically robbery in reverse. You bring her something worth ten roubles and she gives you one and a half. What can you do about it? You need the money. She knows you need the money."

"She's worth a great deal," said the older man. He said it simply, as a fact. "She keeps everything in her apartment. Cash, valuables, documents. Everything. She doesn't trust anyone."

"She lives alone?"

"More or less. Her sister comes sometimes. The sister is a simple woman Lizaveta. She does the shopping, the cleaning. Comes and goes. But the old woman is alone most evenings."

There was the sound of a glass being set down.

"You know," said the student, "I have thought about this. I have genuinely thought about it. That old woman what is she? She is a louse. She is a louse who lives by sucking blood from people who are already barely surviving. She has no family, no one who loves her, no one who would genuinely miss her if she were gone. She is cruel to her own sister. She uses her own sister as a servant and pays her nothing. And meanwhile she sits on a pile of money and she lends it out at fifty percent interest and she gets richer and richer while the students around her get poorer and poorer."

"What are you saying?" said the older man. His voice was cautious.

"I'm saying hypothetically that if someone were to remove that old woman from the world, the world would not be worse for it. It might be better. Take her money and give it to the poor students who need it. Give it to good causes. One life that is useless and harmful, against a hundred lives that could be improved. The mathematics seems obvious."

There was a pause.

"You would never actually do it," said the older man.

"No," said the student. He laughed. "Of course not. I'm not built for it. But you see what I mean. Hypothetically."

"Hypothetically," said the older man. "Yes. Hypothetically I see what you mean."

They went on talking about other things. After a minute, Raskolnikov stopped listening.

He sat very still at his table. The glass in front of him was untouched. The sounds of the square came to him from far away a cart, children, a woman calling someone's name.

A strange feeling had come over him. Not peace. Not the peace he had felt after the dream. Something quite different. Something colder and more precise.

He had just heard, from the mouth of a stranger, his own thoughts. His own theory. His own mathematics. Not in his own words the student had said it more crudely, more carelessly but the structure was the same. The idea was the same. One useless life against many useful ones. The calculation was the same.

And then this was the detail that cut through everything else like a blade he had just heard that Alyona Ivanovna would be alone that evening.

He paid for his drink. He stood up. He walked away from the tavern without hurrying.

He walked for a long time.

When he reached his building, he stood outside it for a moment in the evening light. The city was settling into its long summer dusk. Above the rooftops, the sky was pale gold.

He went inside. He climbed the stairs. He went into his room and sat down on the bed.

The peace from the dream was gone. In its place was something else something very clear and very cold and very still. A decision that had made itself without asking him, a decision that had been there all along, waiting for the right moment to become undeniable.

He looked at the ceiling.

He had one day.

**louse** *(noun)*
A small insect that lives on the body of an animal or person and feeds on their blood. Used here as an insult calling someone a louse means they are a parasite, someone who takes from others.

**interest** *(noun)*
In the context of money, the extra money you pay back when you borrow. If you borrow ten roubles at fifty percent interest, you must pay back fifteen.

In a way that is imagined and not real used to discuss a possibility without saying it will actually happen. "Hypothetically speaking" means "just as a thought experiment."

**harmful** *(adjective)*

## Chapter Summary

**What happened in Chapter 2:**

Raskolnikov wakes to find a letter from his mother. The letter tells him two things: first, that his sister Dunya was badly treated by her employer Svidrigailov and had her reputation destroyed; second, that Dunya is going to marry a lawyer named Luzhin a respectable but cold man partly to help Raskolnikov. He goes out, furious, comparing Dunya's situation to Sonya's. He sits with his theory about extraordinary people who can act beyond ordinary moral rules. That afternoon he sleeps and has a terrible dream about a horse being beaten to death and wakes saying: *I cannot do it.* For a brief moment he feels peace and decides to give up the plan. But at a tavern, he overhears two strangers discussing Alyona Ivanovna and one of them says exactly what Raskolnikov has been thinking for months. He also learns that the pawnbroker will be alone the next evening. He walks home, and the decision returns stronger and more final than before.

**Key characters introduced:**
- **Dunya (Avdotya Romanovna)** Raskolnikov's sister; intelligent, proud, sacrificing herself for the family
- **Pulcheria Alexandrovna** Raskolnikov's mother; loving, worried, trying to see the best in everything
- **Luzhin** mentioned in the letter; cold, calculated, respectable lawyer
- **Svidrigailov** mentioned; the employer who harassed Dunya
- **Lizaveta** mentioned; Alyona's simple, gentle sister who comes and goes from the apartment

**Key themes developed:**
- **The parallel sacrifice** Dunya marrying Luzhin echoes Sonya's situation exactly
- **The theory tested** Raskolnikov explicitly thinks through his "extraordinary person" idea
- **The dream as warning** the horse dream shows his human capacity for compassion
- **The coincidence that decides everything** the overheard conversation removes the last obstacle
