# Crime and Punishment
## Chapter 1 The Plan
### Extended A2/B1 Reader | ~10,000 words

---

*About this story: Crime and Punishment was written by Fyodor Dostoevsky, a Russian writer, in 1866. The story takes place in Saint Petersburg, Russia. It follows a young man named Rodion Raskolnikov. He is poor, intelligent, and deeply unhappy. He has an idea a terrible idea and he cannot stop thinking about it. This is the story of what happens when a person believes that he is special enough to break the rules that everyone else must follow.*

## Scene 1 The Garret

The room was very small. It was not really a room. It was more like a large box, or perhaps a very small cave. The ceiling was low. If a tall man stood up straight and raised his arm, his fingers would touch it. The walls were covered in old yellow wallpaper. In many places, the wallpaper was torn and hung down in long, dry strips. Under the wallpaper, there was grey plaster. In some places, even the plaster had fallen away, leaving the dirty wall bare beneath.

There was one window. It was small and looked out onto the street below. The glass was thick and not very clean. Through it, the street appeared blurry, like a scene in a dream. You could see shapes people walking, a cart moving slowly, the grey stones of the road but everything was soft and unclear.

In the corner of the room, there was a bed. It was narrow, just wide enough for one thin person. The blanket on the bed was old and full of holes. There was a table near the window with a candle on it. The candle was nearly finished. There was a chair. There were a few books on the floor. There was almost nothing else.

This was the room of Rodion Raskolnikov.

He was lying on the bed when the story begins. He was not sleeping. He had been lying there for several hours, staring at the ceiling. His eyes were open but he was not really looking at anything. He was thinking. He was always thinking. His mind was like a machine that could not stop it turned and turned, going over the same ideas again and again.

He was young, perhaps twenty-three years old. He was tall and thin. He had dark hair and dark eyes. His face was fine and sharp it was the face of a person who was used to thinking, not used to resting. Even when he lay still, there was something tense about him, something pulled tight, like a string on an instrument that has been wound too far.

His clothes were terrible. His coat was old and grey. It had once been a dark colour black, perhaps, or deep brown but time and dirt and rain had washed all the colour out of it. There were holes at the elbows. The collar was frayed. His trousers were not much better. His boots had been repaired so many times that the original boot was hard to find under all the layers of new leather and thread.

But Raskolnikov did not notice his clothes. He had stopped noticing them a long time ago. He had stopped noticing many things.

He was a student. Or rather, he had been a student. He was supposed to be studying law at the university in Saint Petersburg. But he had not paid his fees for many months. He had stopped attending his lectures. He had, in a quiet way, stopped being a student though he had not officially left. He still lived in the student part of the city. He still thought like a student always reading, always questioning, always taking ideas and turning them over in his mind to see what was underneath.

He had come to Saint Petersburg from a small town far away. His mother and his sister still lived there. His mother sent him what money she could, but she had very little. His sister worked as a teacher in a wealthy family's house. She too sent him money sometimes. But it was never enough. The city was expensive. The room cost money. Food cost money. Even a candle cost money.

He had stopped eating properly. Some days he ate nothing at all. Some days he ate a small piece of bread or a bowl of thin soup from a cheap cook shop on the corner of his street. He was often hungry, but he found that when he was deep inside his thoughts, he did not feel the hunger so much. His thoughts were big enough to fill the empty space.

He lay on the bed and thought about the plan.

He had been thinking about the plan for a very long time. Weeks. Perhaps months. It had started as a small idea, an abstract thought, the kind of thought a person has in the middle of the night and then forgets in the morning. But this thought had not gone away. It had grown. It had developed roots, like a plant that finds water in dry ground. Now it filled his mind almost completely.

The plan was simple. The plan was also monstrous.

He pushed this thought away. He was not ready to think about it directly. Not yet. First, he needed to take a step. Just one small step. Not the step. Just a step toward it.

He sat up. The bed frame made a sound. He put his feet on the floor and sat there for a moment with his hands on his knees. His head felt heavy. He had not slept well. He had not slept well for many nights.

He needed to go out.

There was a woman. A pawnbroker. Her name was Alyona Ivanovna. She lived in a building not far from his own. He had been to her once before, many weeks ago, when he needed a small amount of money. He had given her his father's old watch. She had given him very little money for it much less than it was worth. She was a shrewd woman, not a generous one. She knew that people who came to her were desperate, and she used their desperation to her advantage.

He needed to go to her again. He needed to see her, to observe her, to study how she lived and how her building was arranged. He told himself this was necessary. He told himself he was only going to look.

He stood up. He put on his hat. His hat was almost as ruined as his coat. It was a tall summer hat, now crushed and stained. He was ashamed of it. But shame, like hunger, was something he had learned to put aside.

He went to the door of his room. He opened it carefully, quietly. He did not want to see his landlady. He owed her money. He owed her a great deal of money. When he saw her on the stairs, she sometimes looked at him with a particular expression not angry, exactly, but cold and distant, the expression of a person who has been patient for too long and whose patience is finally running out.

He went down the stairs. The staircase was dark and smelled of dust and old wood and something cooked a long time ago. He went through the door at the bottom and stepped out into the street.

The air hit him at once. It was summer July and Saint Petersburg in July was hot and dirty. The city did not become beautiful in summer the way some cities do. It became heavier. The heat pressed down on the streets. The smell of the river mixed with the smell of the markets and the horses and the rubbish and the unwashed bodies of too many people living too close together. The light was bright and flat. There were no shadows to rest in.

Raskolnikov walked. He kept his head slightly down. He did not want to look at people. He did not want them to look at him.

He crossed two streets, then turned into a side street, then into another. He knew this part of the city well. He had walked these streets hundreds of times. He knew which corners were noisy and which were quiet. He knew where to go to avoid the crowds and where the streets were so narrow that two carts could not pass each other.

He arrived at the building. He stopped and looked at it. It was an ordinary building, five storeys high, grey stone, with a wide entrance that opened into a courtyard. He went inside.

### 📖 Vocabulary Scene 1

**garret** *(noun)*
A small, uncomfortable room at the very top of a building, just under the roof. Poor people often lived in garrets because they were the cheapest rooms.
*"His garret was so small that he could touch the ceiling with his hand."*

**plaster** *(noun)*
A soft material used to cover walls and ceilings. When it is wet, you can shape it. When it dries, it becomes hard and smooth. Old plaster often cracks and falls off the wall.
*"The plaster on the walls had turned grey and was falling away in pieces."*

**frayed** *(adjective)*
When cloth is old and the edges have come apart into loose threads. A frayed collar means the edge of the collar is torn and messy.
*"His shirt was so old that the cuffs were completely frayed."*

**abstract** *(adjective)*
Existing only as an idea, not as something real or physical. An abstract thought is a thought about a concept, not about a specific real thing.
*"At first, the plan was just an abstract idea he did not believe he would really do it."*

**monstrous** *(adjective)*
Extremely shocking, wrong, or terrible like something a monster would do.
*"The thought was monstrous, and yet he could not push it away."*

**pawnbroker** *(noun)*
A person whose job is to lend money to people. You bring something valuable to them a watch, a ring, a coat and they give you money. If you pay them back, you get your thing back. If you cannot pay, they keep it and sell it.
*"He had taken his father's watch to the pawnbroker and received very little money for it."*

**shrewd** *(adjective)*
Very good at understanding situations and using them to your advantage, especially in business. A shrewd person is clever and practical, but not always kind.
*"She was a shrewd woman who always paid less than things were worth."*

**desperation** *(noun)*
The feeling of having no good choices and no hope. When you are desperate, you will do things you would not normally do.
*"She knew that desperation brought people to her door, and she used this knowledge well."*

**observe** *(verb)*
To watch something carefully and quietly, in order to learn or understand something about it.
*"He told himself he was only going to observe to look at the building and learn its layout."*

**patience** *(noun)*
The ability to wait calmly for something without becoming angry or upset. When someone's patience "runs out," they can no longer wait calmly.
*"His landlady had been patient for many months, but her patience was finally running out."*

## Scene 2 The Pawnbroker

The staircase inside the building was dark and narrow. The steps were made of old wood and some of them moved under his feet. He climbed slowly, listening. He did not want to pass anyone on the stairs. He did not want anyone to see him and remember his face.

The pawnbroker lived on the fourth floor. By the time Raskolnikov reached her door, he was slightly out of breath not from the climbing, but from the tension. His heart was beating faster than it should have been for a simple visit.

He stood at the door and waited for a moment. He made himself breathe slowly and steadily. He straightened his coat with one hand, a habit he had even though straightening it made very little difference. Then he knocked.

For a moment, nothing happened. Then he heard sounds from inside a shuffling, slow sound, like someone walking very carefully across a floor, not wanting to make noise. Then silence. Then the silence of someone standing on the other side of the door and listening.

He knew she was looking at him through the small peephole in the door. He stood still and tried to look as ordinary as possible. This was not easy for him. He was not an ordinary-looking person.

After another moment, he heard the sound of a chain being unfastened, then a bolt being drawn back, then another bolt. The door opened.

She was small and old. She was not simply old she was very, very old, the kind of old that makes a person seem like they belong to a different world than the young. Her hair was thin and white. Her eyes were small and sharp, like a bird's eyes bright and watchful and quick. She wore a fur jacket over her shoulders, even in the summer heat. Her hands were thin and the skin on them was spotted with age.

She looked at him without speaking. She simply looked, waiting for him to explain himself.

"Good day," said Raskolnikov. His voice was steady. He was pleased by this. "My name is Raskolnikov. I came to see you some weeks ago. You may remember me."

She continued to look at him without expression.

"I brought you a watch then. I would like to bring you something else today. I have a silver cigarette case." He reached into his coat pocket and took out the case. It was old but good quality something that had belonged to his father. He held it out toward her.

She took it in her dry fingers. She turned it over. She examined it carefully. She held it up toward the window where the light was better. She pressed her thumbnail against it to test the silver.

"Very little," she said. Her voice was thin and high, like the voice of someone much older than even she appeared. "Very little. You expect more, but I cannot give more."

"How much?" he asked.

She named a sum. It was, as he had expected, much less than the case was worth.

He kept his expression calm. He nodded, as if thinking. But he was not thinking about the money. He was looking at the room behind her. He was studying it.

The apartment was not large, but it was much better than his own room. There were two windows. There was good furniture old but solid. There was a chest with a lock on it near the wall. On a shelf, he could see several small objects watches, rings, a silver brooch, small items that other people had brought to her in desperation. She did not trust banks. He had heard this. She kept her money and her valuables in the apartment itself.

He looked at the door. He looked at the lock. He noted how the furniture was arranged.

She was watching him. Her sharp bird's eyes moved from his face to his hands to his face again.

"You are looking at my things," she said. It was not an accusation, exactly. It was simply an observation, stated plainly.

"I was thinking," he said quickly. "I was thinking whether I might bring you something else in addition. I have a few books. University books. They are in good condition."

"I do not take books," she said. "Books are worthless to me. Nobody wants to buy old textbooks."

"I see," he said. "Then just the case, for now."

She went to the chest and opened it with a key that hung at her waist on a long chain. She took out the money and counted it into his palm. Her fingers touched his hand briefly. They were cold and very dry.

He put the money in his pocket.

"My sister is sometimes here," she said, as he was turning to leave. She said it in a flat voice, as if she was mentioning something without importance. But he heard it as information. "She helps me in the evenings. She is a simple woman. Simple but good."

"I see," he said again.

"Come again if you have something worth bringing," she said. And she closed the door.

He stood in the hallway for a moment. He could hear the bolts being drawn back, the chain being fastened. Then silence.

He went down the stairs. He went through the courtyard and back out into the street. He stood in the hot afternoon light and breathed.

His hands were not shaking. He noticed this with some surprise. He had expected them to shake. He felt nothing, or almost nothing only a strange, flat feeling, like the surface of a lake after a stone has sunk to the bottom.

He began to walk. He did not think about where he was going. His feet chose the direction. He walked along the canal. The water was slow and the colour of dirty green glass. On the other side of the canal, there were grey buildings with rows of identical windows.

He thought: *It would take seven minutes. Perhaps eight. Not more than ten.*

He pushed the thought away. Not yet. Not now.

He walked until his legs were tired. He walked until the sun moved lower in the sky and the heat became slightly less crushing. Then he found himself outside a tavern a low, dirty tavern with a wooden sign above the door and the smell of beer and cheap food coming through the open window. He had not planned to stop here. But he was tired and he did not want to go back to his room.

He went inside.

## Scene 3 The Tavern

The inside of the tavern was dark and cool compared to the street. There were wooden tables and wooden benches. The floor was old and uneven. Most of the tables were empty at this hour it was too early for the evening crowd. A few men sat alone with glasses in front of them. A boy behind the counter looked up when Raskolnikov came in, then looked away again.

Raskolnikov sat down at a table near the wall. He ordered tea and a small piece of bread. He did not have much money but he had a little some from the case, some that he had been saving from before. The tea arrived quickly. It was strong and dark and very hot. He held the glass with both hands and felt the warmth go into his fingers.

He looked around the room. There was a man at the table across from him. He had not noticed the man at first because the man was sitting very still, the way people sit when they are deep inside their own sadness. He was perhaps fifty years old, or perhaps less it was hard to say, because his face had the worn quality of a face that has lived through a great deal of difficulty. He was a tall man, once perhaps impressive, but now soft and collapsed, like a building whose walls have slowly given way. He was dressed in a coat that had once been respectable official-looking, with buttons but was now stained and old.

The man had a glass in front of him. He had been drinking. His eyes, when they moved, were slightly unfocused. But when he looked at Raskolnikov directly, they sharpened suddenly, as if a lamp had been turned up.

"You are a student," the man said. He said it not as a question but as a simple statement of fact.

Raskolnikov was surprised. He looked at the man more carefully.

"Or you were a student," the man added. "I can see it. I know these things." He paused. "I, too, was once a man of rank. I was a titular councillor. Ninth rank. In another life."

He said this last phrase "in another life" with a particular kind of bitterness. Not the fresh bitterness of a recent wound, but the old, dried bitterness of something that happened long ago and has been thought about too many times.

"My name," said the man, "is Marmeladov. Semyon Zakharovich Marmeladov. I am aware that I am not a person who deserves attention. I am aware of how I appear." He gestured vaguely at himself at his coat, at his glass. "But there is something I wish to say, and I find that I need a listener. You appear to be a listener. Young men who think are always better listeners than men who simply act."

Raskolnikov said nothing. But he did not look away. There was something about this man. Something that held his attention despite himself.

Marmeladov leaned forward slightly. "May I ask do you know what it means to have nowhere to go?"

Raskolnikov looked at him. He thought for a moment. Then, very quietly, he said: "Yes. I think I know."

Marmeladov regarded him with surprise and something like recognition the recognition of one drowning man seeing another.

"Then you understand," Marmeladov said. He sat back. He picked up his glass, looked at it, put it down again without drinking. "I have a family," he said. "A wife. Children not mine by blood, but mine in all the ways that matter, because they have no one else and neither do I. And there is my daughter. My Sonya. She is my own from my first wife, who died. Sonya is good. She is the best person I know. She is better than me. She is better than almost anyone."

He paused. He stared at the table. When he looked up again, his eyes were bright with something that was not quite tears but was close to them.

"I had a position," he said. "I had a government post. It was not much, but it was something. It was sufficient. And then I lost it. Because of this." He touched his glass. "I lost it because of this. And my wife Katerina Ivanovna she is a good woman. She is an educated woman. She is from a better family than mine. She is proud. She suffered greatly when I lost my position. She suffers still. She is ill. Her lungs. She coughs. At night, you can hear her cough through the walls."

He stopped speaking. He looked out toward the door of the tavern. Outside, a cart passed with a heavy, rolling sound.

"But I found a new position," he continued. "Recently. Just a few weeks ago, a man I knew from before he was kind enough, or forgetful enough, to give me a chance. I had a position again. I had a salary again. I told myself this time. This time I would be different."

He smiled. It was a terrible smile the smile of someone who knows how the story ends before he has finished telling it.

"And then," said Raskolnikov, speaking for the first time in several minutes. His voice was quiet and careful. "And then?"

"And then," said Marmeladov, "I took all the money. All of it. Five days ago. I took it and I came here. And I have been here since, more or less. Or somewhere like here." He spread his hands on the table. They were large hands, the hands of a man who was once physically strong. Now they were shaking slightly, the small constant tremor of someone who has been drinking for many days.

"My wife does not know where I am," he said. "My children are hungry. My Sonya " He stopped.

"What about your daughter?" Raskolnikov asked. Something was pulling him forward. He wanted to hear the rest.

Marmeladov looked at him. His eyes were very steady now, very serious. "My daughter supports us," he said. "Do you understand what I mean when I say that?"

A moment passed. Raskolnikov understood.

"She chose it herself," Marmeladov said quickly. "I did not ask her. God is my witness I never asked her. She simply went out one morning. She came back with money. And she said nothing and I said nothing and from that day forward " He pressed his lips together. "She lives separately now. She has a yellow ticket. She is registered. She does her what she does. And she brings us money. She brings her stepmother money for the children's food. She brings it quietly, without asking for thanks. And she never complains. Never. In my whole life I have never seen anyone who suffers without complaining the way my Sonya suffers."

He reached for his glass. This time he drank. He set the glass down empty.

"And you," said Raskolnikov. "You took the salary money."

"I did," said Marmeladov. He said it plainly, without defence. "I took it and I came here and I have been drinking since Thursday. And today is I no longer know exactly what day it is. But I know that I must go home. I know this. I simply cannot make myself do it yet. Because when I walk through that door, she will look at me. Katerina Ivanovna will look at me. And she will not shout. She has gone past shouting. She will simply look at me with those eyes she has those terrible, patient, exhausted eyes and I will understand exactly what I am. And I am afraid."

He said the last two words very softly.

A long silence followed. Somewhere at the other end of the tavern, a man laughed loudly at something. The sound fell into the silence and disappeared.

"Why do you tell me this?" Raskolnikov asked.

Marmeladov looked at him. "Because," he said, "you did not look away. Everyone looks away. You are the first person in several days who looked at me and kept looking. It is a small thing. But it is enough."

### 📖 Vocabulary Scene 3

**uneven** *(adjective)*
Not flat or smooth. An uneven floor has parts that are higher and lower, making it difficult to walk across without stumbling.
*"The floor of the tavern was old and uneven one leg of every table wobbled."*

**rank** *(noun)*
A person's level or position in a system such as the government, the army, or a company. Higher rank means more power or importance.
*"He had once held a position of rank in the government, but those days were far behind him."*

**titular councillor** *(noun)*
A specific rank in the Russian government in the 19th century the ninth rank in a system of fourteen. It was a low position with very little power or pay.
*"Marmeladov had once been a titular councillor a small official with a small salary."*

**vaguely** *(adverb)*
In a way that is not clear or specific. If you gesture vaguely, you make an unclear movement that points in a general direction.
*"He gestured vaguely at his own coat, as if to say: look at the state of me."*

**recognition** *(noun)*
The feeling of seeing or understanding something you have experienced before. When two people share the same difficult experience, they may feel recognition when they meet.
*"He looked at Raskolnikov with something like recognition the look of one drowning man seeing another."*

**sufficient** *(adjective)*
Enough. If something is sufficient, there is as much as you need not more, not less.
*"The salary was not large, but it was sufficient to keep the family fed and warm."*

**post** *(noun)*
A job, especially an official or government job.
*"He had lost his government post because of his drinking."*

**tremor** *(noun)*
A small, uncontrolled shaking movement of the hands or body. Often caused by illness, cold, fear, or drinking too much alcohol.
*"His hands rested on the table with a constant small tremor."*

**witness** *(noun)*
A person who sees something happen. "God is my witness" is a phrase used to say something very strongly it means: I am telling the complete truth.
*"God is my witness I never asked her to do it," he said."*

**registered** *(adjective)*
Officially recorded in a government document or list. In Dostoevsky's time, women who worked as prostitutes had to be officially registered and carry a yellow identity document.
*"She lives separately now. She has a yellow ticket. She is registered."*

## Scene 4 Marmeladov's Story

They sat in the tavern for another hour. Marmeladov ordered more to drink. Raskolnikov ordered more tea. Outside, the light changed slowly. Evening was beginning somewhere above the rooftops, out of sight.

Marmeladov kept talking. Once he had begun, the words came without stopping, as if a pressure inside him had been released. He was not a stupid man Raskolnikov could see this clearly. He was educated. His speech was careful and formed. He used long words and constructed his sentences with a kind of painful precision. But there was something broken at the centre of it all. Something that had cracked long ago and had never been repaired.

He told Raskolnikov more about his wife. Katerina Ivanovna had been the daughter of an army officer an important man, a man of some status in their provincial town. When her first husband died, she had been left with three small children and very little money. She had married Marmeladov because there was no better option. Not because she loved him. Not because she believed in him. Because he had a position and a salary and a room, and she had three hungry children and no other door to knock on.

"She is educated, I tell you," Marmeladov said, with a kind of fierce, wounded pride. "She can read French. She received a certificate a gold medal from her school. She was the best student in her class. She had a life ahead of her a life of refinement, of culture, of the things a mind like hers needed. And then one thing after another a bad marriage, her husband's death, poverty and then me." He made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "And then me."

Sonya, he explained, was from his first marriage. His first wife had also been gentle and also good, and she had also died. He said this simply, without much expression, as if the death of his first wife was something so far in the past that he had used up all the feeling he had for it.

Sonya had grown up gentle and quiet and deeply, inconveniently kind. She was not clever in the way her father meant clever she was not analytical or sharp. But she felt things very deeply and she cared for people without condition, the way a child does before the world teaches them to be careful with their feelings.

When the family had nothing no food, no money, when Katerina Ivanovna was coughing blood and the small children were crying with hunger it was Sonya who went out. She was eighteen. She put on her best dress and she went out.

"She came home," Marmeladov said slowly. "She came home and she put thirty roubles on the table. Thirty roubles. She did not speak. She did not look at me. She looked at her stepmother and put the money on the table and then went and lay on her bed and pulled the sheet over her head. And then she was still."

He was quiet for a moment.

"My wife," he said, "my wife looked at the money. And then she got up and she went to Sonya's bed and she knelt down next to it. And she stayed there, kneeling at the bed, for a long time. I don't know what she said. She spoke quietly. I could not hear the words. But I knew what she was doing. She was asking forgiveness. She was asking it from an eighteen-year-old girl who had gone out in her best dress so that we could eat."

Raskolnikov looked down at the table. He was holding his empty glass. He turned it slowly in his fingers.

"Sonya comes to us still," Marmeladov continued. "Every week. She brings what she can. She does not stay long. She cannot stay long she has her yellow ticket, and registered women are not supposed to mix freely with respectable families, and so she stays in the doorway and passes the money in and then goes away again. My wife accepts it. What can she do? The children need to eat."

He paused again. Then he said something that Raskolnikov did not expect.

"I believe in God," Marmeladov said. "I want you to understand that. I believe in God and I believe in his mercy and I believe that even a person like me even a person who has done everything wrong, who has failed everyone who needed him, who has taken money from a daughter who earned it through suffering even such a person may be forgiven. This is what I believe."

"By what right?" Raskolnikov said. He said it without emotion. It was a genuine question. "By what right can you expect forgiveness?"

Marmeladov looked at him. The sharp light came back into his eyes. "By no right," he said immediately. "That is precisely the point. Not by right. By mercy alone. Mercy is different from right. Right says: you earned this, so you receive it. Mercy says: you deserve nothing, and here it is anyway. If mercy were only given to those who deserved it, it would not be mercy. It would simply be payment."

Raskolnikov thought about this. He turned it over in his mind the way he turned all ideas, looking at it from different angles. He did not entirely disagree. But he also did not entirely agree. He was not a person who believed easily in mercy neither divine mercy nor human mercy. He believed in logic and in the weight of facts.

"There is another kind of thinking," Raskolnikov said quietly. He was not sure why he was speaking. Something in the conversation was pulling words out of him. "There is a kind of thinking that says certain people are above mercy, above forgiveness, above the ordinary rules. Not because they are better in a moral sense. But because they are different. Because they are capable of something that ordinary people cannot do. Because they see further."

Marmeladov looked at him with genuine curiosity. "What kind of thing can they do?"

"Anything," Raskolnikov said. And then, as if catching himself: "I mean, anything that serves a greater purpose. History is full of such people. They broke laws. They crossed lines. And the world called them criminals at first. But later, the world called them great. Because what they did served something larger than themselves."

A silence settled between them. Marmeladov looked at the younger man for a long time. His expression was unreadable.

"You are very young," Marmeladov said at last. "And you are very intelligent. These two things together can be dangerous."

Raskolnikov said nothing.

"I should go home," Marmeladov said. He stood up slowly. The movement clearly cost him effort. He steadied himself against the table. "I should go home. My wife will be waiting. Her eyes will be terrible. The children will be hungry. And I will walk through the door anyway, because what else can I do? There is always tomorrow. And even I, who have no right to hope, continue somehow to hope."

He looked at Raskolnikov with a complicated expression sadness and warmth and a kind of dark humour all together. "Would you be kind enough to see me home? It is not far. I do not entirely trust my own legs at this moment."

Raskolnikov looked at him. He thought of several reasons to say no. Then he stood up and left money on the table for the tea and the bread. And he followed Marmeladov toward the door.

## Scene 5 The Night Walk

They walked out of the tavern together into the evening street. The heat of the day had finally begun to soften. There was a slight movement of air from the direction of the river. It was not a pleasant breeze it smelled of water and mud and the accumulated breath of the city but after the closed air of the tavern, it felt like relief.

Marmeladov walked slowly. Raskolnikov walked beside him, ready to put out a hand if the older man stumbled. But Marmeladov moved with a kind of careful dignity, placing each foot with deliberate attention, like a man crossing a stream on wet stones. He did not fall.

They crossed one street and then another. The streets were quieter now. The market stalls had been packed away. A woman was washing her doorstep with a large brush. Two boys were chasing each other around a lamp post, laughing at something Raskolnikov could not see.

"She will be at the table when I come in," Marmeladov said, more to himself than to Raskolnikov. "She will be sitting straight she always sits straight, even when she is tired, even when she is coughing. It is her pride. Her pride is the last thing she has and she holds onto it with both hands."

Raskolnikov listened but said nothing.

"The children will be asleep, or pretending to sleep," Marmeladov continued. "The little ones Polenka, she is the eldest, she is ten. She understands more than a ten-year-old should understand. She looks at me sometimes with eyes that are too old for her face. It is difficult to bear."

They turned into a narrow street and then into a courtyard. The building here was older than Marmeladov's description had suggested. It was large and dark, with peeling paint and a staircase that was poorly lit. There was a smell of boiled cabbage and damp wood.

From somewhere above them, before they even reached the stairs, they heard voices. One voice was clear and sharp and high a woman's voice, speaking very fast, in clipped, controlled sentences that had the quality of someone who was furious but had decided to be furious in an orderly way. Between the sentences, there was coughing.

Marmeladov stopped at the bottom of the stairs. He looked up. He took a long breath.

"That is her," he said quietly. "She is talking to the neighbour, most likely. When she is very upset, she speaks to the neighbour through the door. The neighbour is a widow. She has her own problems. But she listens." A pause. "She will stop when she sees me."

He began to climb. Raskolnikov followed.

When they reached the landing on the third floor, a door was open. Inside, Raskolnikov could see a narrow room with a table and several chairs. A woman stood near the table. She was not tall, but she held herself very straight, as Marmeladov had described. She was younger than Raskolnikov had imagined perhaps thirty-five, or perhaps illness made her seem younger. Her face was thin and very pale. Her cheekbones were prominent. Her eyes were bright not with happiness, but with the brightness of fever.

She looked at Marmeladov.

And then she was silent. Exactly as he had described. She stopped speaking and she looked at him and the expression on her face was terrible. Not anger. Not even disappointment. It was the expression of someone who has been hoping for something small and has had that small hope taken from them once again. An expression of a person who knows what she knows, who has always known it, and who is simply very, very tired of knowing it.

Marmeladov went toward her. Raskolnikov stayed in the hallway.

"Katerina," said Marmeladov. He said her name quietly and plainly, without excuse. "I am home."

She did not respond. She looked at him for another moment. Then she turned away and sat down at the table and put her hands flat on the surface and looked at nothing.

Raskolnikov watched from the hallway for a moment longer. Then he quietly turned and went back down the stairs.

He came out into the cool night air. Above him, the sky was dark and the stars were beginning to appear, though the lights of the city made them faint and pale. He stood in the courtyard and looked up at them.

He thought about Marmeladov. He thought about Katerina Ivanovna's eyes. He thought about Sonya, who he had never seen the girl in the doorway, passing money in and then going away again. He thought about mercy and about logic and about whether these two things could ever be reconciled.

He began to walk. He walked without a destination. He crossed the canal bridge and walked along the embankment. The water below was black and slow. The lamp posts made orange circles of light on the stones. He passed other people men walking home from work, a couple talking quietly, a soldier standing in a doorway.

Then he saw the girl.

She was sitting on a bench near the edge of the embankment. She was young perhaps fifteen or sixteen. Her dress was dishevelled and her hat had slipped sideways. She was not asleep her eyes were open but she had the loose, unfocused quality of someone who was not entirely conscious. She was swaying slightly.

Behind her, about ten steps back, a stout man in a light coat was watching her. He was well-dressed. He had a watch chain. He was pretending to look at the water, but every few seconds his eyes moved back to the girl.

Raskolnikov felt something sharp and cold go through him. He understood immediately what was happening.

He walked over to the girl and sat down on the bench near her. "Are you all right?" he asked quietly.

She looked at him. Her eyes were unfocused. She was probably drunk, or had been given something. She was alone and she was in danger.

He looked up at the stout man, who had moved a few steps closer. Their eyes met. The man looked away at once, with the particular speed of someone caught doing something he knows is wrong.

Raskolnikov sat next to the girl and waited. After a few minutes, the stout man turned and walked away slowly, in the direction of the next bridge.

He remained on the bench until he was sure the man was gone. The girl's head drooped forward. She was falling asleep on the bench.

He found a police officer a hundred metres down the embankment and explained the situation as briefly as he could. The officer looked at the girl, looked at Raskolnikov, and went to deal with the matter.

Raskolnikov walked away. He felt nothing particular about what he had just done. He had done it automatically, the way you pull your hand back from a flame without deciding to.

He walked on. The streets were emptying. He walked toward his own neighbourhood without hurrying. His thoughts were settling, the way sediment settles in water after something has disturbed it.

He thought: there are people who can act and people who cannot. There are people who see a situation and move through it directly, without hesitation and people who stand at the edge forever, thinking.

He thought: for weeks and months he had been standing at the edge. He had been thinking. He had been turning the idea over and over, examining it from every side, looking for the flaw in his own reasoning.

He had not found one.

He reached his building. He went up the stairs in the dark, moving quietly, missing the third step from the top it creaked, and he had learned long ago to avoid it. He reached his door and opened it.

He went inside. He lay down on his bed without taking off his coat. He stared at the ceiling.

The ceiling was grey and low and familiar. He had stared at it hundreds of times. He knew every crack in it, every place where the plaster had shifted.

He thought about Alyona Ivanovna. He thought about her sharp bird's eyes and her dry fingers and the chest with the lock on it.

He thought: *Seven minutes. Perhaps eight. Not more than ten.*

He closed his eyes.

Outside, the city was quiet. The summer night was warm and the stars were faint above the rooftops. In the rooms below him, his landlady slept. In the room next to his, someone coughed once and was silent. On the embankment near the canal, the bench where the girl had sat was now empty.

Everything was still.

In the silence, Raskolnikov lay and thought. And the thought that had been growing in him for months settled into him now like something heavy and final, like a stone that has at last found the bottom of the river.

He was going to do it.

## Chapter Summary

**What happened in Chapter 1:**

Raskolnikov is a young ex-student living in extreme poverty in Saint Petersburg. He owes money to his landlady and has almost no food. He has been thinking for months about a terrible plan. In this chapter, he goes to see a pawnbroker named Alyona Ivanovna not to complete the plan, but to observe her apartment and prepare himself mentally. Afterward, he sits in a tavern where he meets Marmeladov, a ruined government official. Marmeladov tells him about his family his proud, ill wife Katerina Ivanovna, and his daughter Sonya, who sells herself so her family can eat. Raskolnikov helps Marmeladov walk home and sees Katerina Ivanovna's terrible, exhausted eyes. On the way back, he protects a young girl from a dangerous man on the embankment. That night, lying on his bed, he arrives at a decision. He is going to carry out the plan.

**Key characters introduced:**
- **Rodion Raskolnikov** the protagonist; young, intelligent, deeply poor, and carrying a dangerous idea
- **Alyona Ivanovna** a pawnbroker; old, shrewd, and cold
- **Marmeladov** a ruined official; educated, broken, ashamed, but capable of deep feeling
- **Katerina Ivanovna** Marmeladov's wife; proud, ill, exhausted
- **Sonya** Marmeladov's daughter; kind and sacrificing (mentioned but not yet seen)

**Key themes introduced:**
- **Poverty and its effects** on thinking, dignity, and choice
- **The "extraordinary person" theory** Raskolnikov's idea that certain people are above ordinary moral rules
- **Mercy vs. logic** the debate between Raskolnikov and Marmeladov
- **Guilt and shame** Marmeladov as a mirror of what guilt does to a person over time
