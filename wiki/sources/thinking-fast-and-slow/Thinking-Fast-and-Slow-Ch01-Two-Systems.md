# Thinking, Fast and Slow
## Chapter 1 Two Systems
### Extended A2/B1 Reader

---

*About this book: Thinking, Fast and Slow was written by Daniel Kahneman and published in 2011. Kahneman is a psychologist who spent his life studying how people think and make decisions. He worked together with another scientist named Amos Tversky for many years. Together, they discovered that people make many predictable mistakes when they think. These mistakes are not random they follow patterns. In 2002, Kahneman received the Nobel Prize in Economics for this research. This book is the story of everything he learned. It is a book about your mind how it works, why it sometimes fails you, and how you can think a little more clearly.*

## Part 1 A Man Who Studies the Mind

Daniel Kahneman grew up in France and Israel. He was born in Tel Aviv in 1934, but his family moved to France when he was very young. During World War Two, life was very dangerous for Jewish families in France. The German army occupied the country, and Jewish people were persecuted. Young Daniel and his family had to hide and move from place to place to stay safe. These were difficult, frightening years.

But even in those hard times, Daniel was curious. He was a boy who asked many questions. He wanted to understand why people behaved the way they did. Why did some people become cruel? Why did others become kind? Why did human beings make such strange and often terrible choices?

After the war, Kahneman's family moved to what would become the new state of Israel in 1948. Kahneman studied psychology at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, and he fell in love with the subject immediately. Psychology is the scientific study of the mind how people think, feel, learn, remember, and behave. For Kahneman, it felt like the most important subject in the world.

After completing his degree, he went to the United States to study more. He earned his doctorate at the University of California, Berkeley. Then he returned to Israel and began to teach. He became a professor at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem.

At the university, he worked with a brilliant colleague named Amos Tversky. Tversky was also a psychologist, and he had a sharp, fast mind he was known for being able to think quickly and argue brilliantly about almost any topic. Kahneman was different more careful, more self-questioning, slower to reach conclusions. Together, they were a remarkable team. Their personalities balanced each other perfectly.

For many years, Kahneman and Tversky studied one central question: how do people make decisions? When we face a choice, what happens inside our minds? Are we rational meaning, do we think carefully and logically? Or do we use shortcuts, emotions, and instincts?

The answer they discovered surprised many people and eventually changed the world of economics, psychology, and policy.

## Part 2 The Two Systems

The most important idea in Kahneman's book is this: your mind has two different ways of thinking. He calls them **System 1** and **System 2**.

Understanding these two systems is the key to understanding everything else in the book. So let us look at them carefully.

---

**System 1** is fast.

System 1 works automatically. It operates without effort, and you cannot easily control it or turn it off. It is always running even when you are not paying attention. System 1 is the part of your mind that recognises faces, understands language, reads emotions, avoids sudden dangers, and makes quick judgements.

Think about what happens when you see a person's face and immediately know whether they look angry or happy. You did not have to think about it. It happened instantly. That is System 1.

Think about what happens when you are driving a car on a road you know very well. Your hands turn the wheel, your foot presses the brake and the accelerator, your eyes scan the road all without conscious effort. You can even have a conversation at the same time. That is System 1 doing the driving for you.

Think about what happens when someone asks you, "What is two plus two?" You do not stop and think carefully. The answer four comes to you immediately, automatically. That is System 1.

System 1 is incredibly useful. Because of System 1, you do not have to think carefully about every single moment of every single day. You can walk, talk, breathe, recognise danger, understand speech, and navigate familiar places all at the same time, without effort. System 1 makes life manageable.

But System 1 has a serious problem. It is very fast but it is not always right. It makes quick judgements based on patterns, experience, and emotion. And sometimes, those judgements are wrong. Sometimes what feels right is actually a mistake. System 1 is confident, but it can be fooled.

---

**System 2** is slow.

System 2 is the careful, logical part of your mind. When you need to think seriously about something when you need to concentrate, calculate, compare, or reason System 2 becomes active.

Think about what happens when someone asks you, "What is seventeen multiplied by twenty-four?" This time, the answer does not come automatically. You have to stop. You have to think. You have to work through the calculation step by step. Your attention becomes focused. You probably stop doing anything else while you think about this. That is System 2 at work.

Think about what happens when you are trying to park your car in a very small space on a busy street. You slow down. You turn carefully. You look in your mirrors. You concentrate completely. You might even stop talking if someone is in the car with you, because you need all your attention for the parking. That is System 2.

Think about what happens when you fill in a tax form, or try to understand the instructions for a new piece of technology, or study a difficult subject for an exam. That effort, that concentration, that feeling of your brain working hard that is System 2.

System 2 is more accurate than System 1. When System 2 works properly, it can reason logically, check for mistakes, and arrive at correct conclusions. But System 2 has a very big problem: it is lazy.

System 2 requires effort. It requires energy. It is slow. And because it is slow, expensive, and tiring, your mind tries to use it as little as possible. Your mind prefers the fast, automatic, effortless answers of System 1. System 2 only steps in when System 1 cannot handle something on its own or when the stakes are high enough to justify the effort.

## Part 3 A Simple Test

Kahneman loves to use a famous little test to show how the two systems work. Here it is. Read it carefully:

*A bat and a ball together cost £1.10. The bat costs £1 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?*

When most people read this problem, an answer comes to them very quickly automatically. The quick answer that jumps into most people's minds is: ten pence.

But that answer is wrong.

Think about it more carefully. If the ball costs ten pence, and the bat costs £1 more than the ball, then the bat costs £1.10. But the bat plus the ball would then cost £1.20 not £1.10. So the quick answer is wrong.

The correct answer is five pence. If the ball costs five pence, then the bat costs £1.05. And five pence plus £1.05 equals £1.10. That is correct.

This problem was given to students at several famous universities including Harvard, MIT, and Princeton, some of the most prestigious universities in the world. More than fifty percent of the students gave the wrong answer. They said "ten pence." Even very intelligent, well-educated people fell into the trap.

Why? Because System 1 gave a quick, confident, and wrong answer. And System 2 did not bother to check it. System 2 was lazy. It accepted the answer from System 1 without doing the hard work of checking whether it was correct.

This is one of the central lessons of the book: System 1 is often wrong, and System 2 often does not check its work. This is why we make so many mistakes not because we are stupid, but because our fast, automatic mind gives us answers that feel right but are actually wrong.

## Part 4 How System 1 Makes Mistakes

System 1 is not bad. It is not the enemy of good thinking. Most of the time, System 1 works very well. It keeps you safe, helps you understand the world quickly, and allows you to function in daily life without being completely exhausted. The problem is that System 1 has specific weaknesses specific types of situations where it almost always gets things wrong. These weaknesses are called **cognitive biases**.

A cognitive bias is a systematic mistake in thinking. "Systematic" means it follows a pattern it is not random. The same bias causes the same type of mistake in the same type of situation, again and again, in person after person. You are not alone in your mistakes. Billions of people share the same cognitive biases, because all human minds are built in the same basic way.

Kahneman spent his career studying these biases. He found hundreds of them. In this chapter, we will look at the most fundamental ones the ones that come directly from the way System 1 operates.

---

**The First Problem: System 1 Jumps to Conclusions**

System 1 does not wait for all the information before making a judgement. It makes quick judgements based on whatever information is available even when that information is incomplete.

Imagine you meet a person for the first time. Within seconds before you have heard them speak a single word you have already formed an opinion about them. Are they trustworthy? Are they friendly? Are they intelligent? System 1 has already answered these questions, based on things like their face, their clothing, the way they stand, and the expression on their face. System 1 made a judgement in the blink of an eye.

Sometimes this quick judgement is correct. If someone is smiling warmly and offering their hand, System 1 correctly recognises them as friendly. But sometimes this quick judgement is very wrong. A person might look cold and unfriendly, but actually be kind and thoughtful. A person might look confident and impressive, but actually be incompetent. System 1 judges by appearances and appearances can be deceiving.

This tendency to jump to conclusions based on first impressions is very powerful. It affects us in many important situations. Research shows that people decide whether they like a politician within seconds of seeing them on screen before hearing a single policy or argument. Research shows that people judge how competent a face looks, and that this judgement predicts election results with surprising accuracy. Research shows that in job interviews, the first few seconds often determine whether someone gets the job the rest of the interview is often just confirmation.

System 1 jumps to conclusions. And very often, we never question those conclusions, because they feel right.

---

**The Second Problem: System 1 Creates Stories**

System 1 cannot tolerate chaos or confusion. It needs things to make sense. So when information is incomplete or contradictory, System 1 does something remarkable: it invents a story.

Your mind takes the information it has, fills in the missing pieces with assumptions and guesses, and creates a smooth, coherent narrative a story that makes sense. This happens automatically, without effort, and without your awareness. You do not know that your mind is filling in gaps. It all feels like reality.

Kahneman calls this principle **WYSIATI**. This stands for: "What You See Is All There Is." System 1 acts as if the information it currently has is all the information that exists. It does not worry about what it does not know. It builds the best possible story from whatever is available and it believes that story completely.

This is why witnesses in court cases are so often wrong. When a person witnesses a crime or an accident, their System 1 immediately creates a story. It fills in details that the person did not actually see. It creates a coherent narrative. But later, when they are asked to testify in court, they do not say, "I saw this, but I'm not sure about that." They say with complete confidence, "I saw this, and this, and this" even when some of those details came from their imagination, not from their eyes.

This is also why conspiracy theories are so powerful. A conspiracy theory is a story that explains everything. It connects the available evidence into a neat, satisfying narrative. System 1 loves stories that make sense. A good conspiracy theory gives System 1 exactly what it wants: a complete, coherent explanation for events that might otherwise seem confusing and random. The fact that the story is wrong does not matter to System 1. All that matters is that the story is satisfying.

---

**The Third Problem: System 1 Relies on Feelings**

System 1 does not think in facts and logic. It thinks in feelings. When System 1 evaluates a situation is this dangerous or safe? is this person good or bad? is this idea right or wrong? it consults feelings, not arguments.

This is called the **affect heuristic**. The word "affect" here means feeling or emotion. The word "heuristic" means a mental shortcut a quick way of reaching an answer without doing a full analysis.

Here is how the affect heuristic works. When you encounter an idea, a person, or a situation, System 1 immediately checks: does this feel good or bad? pleasant or unpleasant? safe or threatening? The feeling comes first, almost instantly. Then System 1 constructs reasons to support that feeling. It does not start with the reasons and then arrive at the feeling it starts with the feeling and then finds the reasons.

This is the opposite of how we like to think we think. We like to believe that we gather information, evaluate it carefully, weigh the evidence, and then reach a conclusion. But very often, it works the other way around. We have the feeling first. Then we invent the reasons.

Kahneman describes this with a simple example. Ask people whether they think a technology is risky, and whether they think it is beneficial. For most technologies, there is no logical connection between risk and benefit something can be risky and very beneficial at the same time, like surgery. But research shows that if people have a good feeling about a technology if they like the idea of it they rate it as having high benefits and low risks. If they have a bad feeling about a technology if something about it makes them uncomfortable they rate it as having low benefits and high risks. The feeling drives both judgements simultaneously.

In other words: people do not ask "is this risky?" and "is this beneficial?" as separate questions. They ask "do I like this?" and then give high ratings to the dimension they care about, and low ratings to the other.

## Part 5 System 2 Is Not as Strong as You Think

People often believe that System 2 is in charge. We like to think that we are rational, logical thinkers. We believe that we gather evidence, weigh the facts, and make careful, considered decisions. We believe that we are, in general, sensible.

Kahneman's research suggests this is largely an illusion. System 2 is in charge of the story we tell about ourselves. But System 1 is in charge of most of what we actually do.

Here is a striking demonstration. In one famous experiment, researchers asked people to watch a short video. In the video, two groups of people one wearing white shirts, one wearing black shirts were passing basketballs to each other. The task was simple: count how many times the players in white shirts pass the ball.

People watched carefully and counted the passes. Most people got the number right, or close to it. Then the researchers asked: "Did you notice anything unusual in the video?"

Many people said no, nothing unusual.

But something very unusual had happened in the video. A person in a gorilla costume had walked into the middle of the screen, stopped, beat their chest, and walked out. The gorilla was on screen for a full nine seconds.

Approximately half of the people who watched the video did not see the gorilla. They were concentrating on counting the passes, and the gorilla which was completely visible, impossible to miss went entirely unnoticed.

This experiment is called the **invisible gorilla** experiment. It demonstrates a phenomenon called **inattentional blindness** the failure to notice something that is right in front of you when your attention is focused elsewhere.

What does this tell us about System 2? It tells us that System 2 has very limited resources. When System 2 is focused on one task counting passes it cannot also process other information. The gorilla did not register. System 2 was fully occupied.

And here is something even more unsettling: most of the people who missed the gorilla were completely confident that they had seen everything. They did not think they had missed anything. System 2's blindness to the gorilla did not make people uncertain. They still felt sure of their perception.

This is one of the most important lessons Kahneman offers: we do not notice the limits of our own thinking. When System 2 fails, we do not feel it failing. We continue to feel confident, informed, and in control even when we are not.

## Part 6 Two Characters in Your Head

Kahneman writes that it is helpful to think of System 1 and System 2 as two different characters almost like two people living inside your mind. They have different personalities. They want different things. They behave in different ways.

System 1 is like an impulsive, confident, fast-talking person. It speaks first. It never hesitates. It is always sure of itself. It does not question its own conclusions. If you ask System 1 a question, it will answer immediately even if it does not really know the answer. System 1 hates uncertainty and ambiguity. It will create a story, a pattern, an explanation even when none really exists just to avoid the discomfort of not knowing.

System 2 is like a careful, methodical, somewhat tired person. It is more accurate than System 1, but it is slow and it requires effort. System 2 would rather not work if it does not have to. It often endorses whatever System 1 suggests, without checking carefully. System 2 is the boss in theory but System 1 is often the boss in practice, because System 2 is too tired or too busy to oversee everything.

The problem is that the two characters do not always work together well. When the right answer requires careful reasoning, System 2 needs to override System 1. But System 2 is lazy. And System 1 is confident. So very often, System 1 gives an answer, System 2 does a very quick check (or no check at all), and the wrong answer gets accepted as correct.

This is not a design flaw that can be fixed. This is simply how human minds are built. Evolution built System 1 to be fast and efficient and speed was more important than accuracy for most of human history. When a predator was approaching, the right answer was: run. There was no time to think carefully. The fast, automatic response was the response that kept people alive.

System 2, with its capacity for careful reasoning, is much more recent in evolutionary terms. It is powerful, but it is also fragile easily tired, easily distracted, easily overwhelmed. And it requires education and practice to develop.

Kahneman does not tell you that you can switch off System 1 or make System 2 do all your thinking. That is not possible. But he does suggest that knowing about these two systems understanding how they work and where they go wrong can help you make better decisions. You can learn to slow down in situations where System 1 is likely to mislead you. You can learn to ask, "Is System 2 awake here? Have I actually checked this?" You can learn to recognise the situations where your automatic thinking is likely to be wrong.

## Part 7 Seeing With Fresh Eyes

One of the most beautiful things about Kahneman's book is this: once you understand System 1 and System 2, you start to see the world differently. You begin to notice the fast, automatic thinking everywhere. You begin to see it in advertising, in politics, in conversations, in your own daily decisions.

Walk into a supermarket, and you will see that products are placed at eye level because they are easier to reach and easier to reach means more likely to be chosen by System 1, which always prefers the path of least effort. The store is designed to work with your System 1.

Watch a political advertisement, and you will notice that it does not try to give you facts and arguments. Instead, it gives you images a smiling leader with children, a beautiful flag in the wind, a dark and threatening image of the opponent. It is speaking directly to System 1, using emotion and association rather than reason and evidence.

Listen to a car salesperson, and notice how they start with a very high price an anchor before offering a lower one. The first number your System 1 hears becomes a reference point that shapes how you feel about every number that comes after. This is called the **anchoring effect**, and it is one of the most powerful and consistent findings in psychology.

Notice how, when you feel good when the sun is shining, when you have just eaten, when you are with friends you are more likely to trust your first impressions, more likely to believe that things will work out, more likely to take risks. And notice how, when you feel tired or stressed, you are more likely to rely on System 1, more likely to make impulsive decisions, more likely to make mistakes.

All of this is System 1. It is always there, always running, always influencing your thoughts and choices in ways you usually do not notice.

## Part 8 The Question Kahneman Always Asks

When Kahneman makes a judgement or decision, he has trained himself to ask a specific question: **"Is this System 1 talking, or System 2?"**

This is a very simple question, but it is very powerful. When you ask it, you slow down. You create a small moment of reflection between the automatic feeling and the action. And in that small moment, System 2 has a chance to step in.

For example, imagine you are at a meeting and someone presents an idea. Immediately, you feel certain that the idea will not work. You feel it strongly. You are ready to say so. But before you speak, you ask: "Is this System 1 talking? Why do I feel so sure? Do I have actual evidence, or just a feeling?"

If you stop and ask this question, you might realise that your certainty came from something irrelevant perhaps because you dislike the person who presented the idea, or because the idea feels unfamiliar, or because it contradicts something you have always believed. None of these are good reasons for being certain the idea will fail. System 1 gave you certainty without evidence.

Kahneman does not promise that asking this question will always give you the right answer. System 2 makes mistakes too. And sometimes, the right answer really is the immediate, instinctive one sometimes System 1 is correct. The point is not to distrust all fast thinking. The point is to know when to distrust it.

The situations where System 1 is most likely to be wrong include:
- When the decision is complex, with many variables
- When you are tired, stressed, hungry, or distracted
- When the situation is unfamiliar
- When there are strong emotions involved
- When you have a lot to gain or lose
- When you are being influenced by things that should not matter like the way a question is phrased, or the first number you heard

In these situations, System 2 needs to be switched on deliberately. The effort is worth it.

## Part 9 Two Systems in Real Life

To close this chapter, let us look at how the two systems affect some important areas of daily life.

---

**Eating**

Every time you eat, both systems are involved. System 1 makes you reach for the biscuits on the counter, simply because they are there and visible. It makes you eat more when food is served on a large plate. It makes you choose the item at the top of the menu because reading through all the options requires System 2 effort. It makes you feel full later when you are distracted, because System 1 does not track your food intake carefully when your attention is elsewhere.

System 2 is the part of you that says, "I should eat something healthy." But as the day goes on and System 2 becomes tired from work and decisions, it becomes harder and harder to override System 1's preferences for easy, pleasurable, familiar food.

This is why many diets fail. They require System 2 effort constantly and System 2 runs out of energy. The solution which Kahneman does not directly state but which comes from his research is to design your environment so that System 1 naturally leads you to the right choices. Put healthy food where it is easy to see and reach. Put unhealthy food out of sight. Let System 1 work for you, not against you.

---

**Relationships**

In relationships with friends, family members, partners, or colleagues System 1 is extremely active. It makes quick judgements about people's moods, intentions, and character. It reads facial expressions and body language automatically. It creates stories about why people behaved the way they did.

The problem is that System 1 often creates the wrong story. If a friend does not reply to your message quickly, System 1 might immediately create a story: "They are angry with me." Or "They don't care about me." These stories feel very real and very certain. But they might be completely wrong your friend might simply be busy, or have forgotten, or not have seen the message.

The stories System 1 creates about other people can cause enormous damage in relationships, because we react to the story rather than to reality. Learning to pause and ask, "Is this story accurate? Do I actually have evidence for this?" can make a significant difference.

---

**Money**

When it comes to money, System 1 creates some of its most serious and expensive mistakes.

One of the most important of these is related to **loss aversion** an idea we will explore in much more detail in a later chapter. The basic finding is this: losing money feels much worse than gaining the same amount of money feels good. Research suggests that a loss feels approximately twice as painful as an equivalent gain feels pleasurable. So losing fifty pounds hurts about as much as gaining a hundred pounds feels good.

This means that System 1 treats gains and losses very asymmetrically very unequally. And this causes many poor financial decisions. People hold on to losing investments too long, hoping they will recover, because the pain of selling at a loss is too great. People refuse to sell a house at a small loss, even when selling would be the financially sensible decision. People take irrational risks to avoid accepting a loss.

All of this comes from System 1. Loss aversion is not a logical, calculated response. It is an emotional, automatic one.

---

**Health**

When people receive medical information, System 1 plays a powerful role in how they interpret it. Research shows that doctors who describe the same treatment in different ways get very different responses from patients.

If a doctor says, "This operation has a ninety percent survival rate," patients are much more likely to agree to the operation than if the same doctor says, "This operation has a ten percent mortality rate." Ninety percent survival and ten percent mortality mean exactly the same thing. But the first sentence sounds reassuring (System 1 likes it), and the second sentence sounds frightening (System 1 does not like it).

This is called the **framing effect** the way information is framed, or presented, changes how we feel about it and therefore what we decide. And the framing effect works entirely through System 1. System 2, if it were doing its job, would recognise that the two sentences mean the same thing. But very often, System 2 does not check.

## Part 10 What This Chapter Has Taught Us

We have covered a great deal in this first chapter. Let us take a moment to bring it all together.

The human mind has two systems for thinking. System 1 is fast, automatic, effortless, and emotional. It runs all the time, without your awareness. System 2 is slow, deliberate, effortful, and logical. It is activated when you need to concentrate on something difficult.

System 1 is essential without it, daily life would be impossible. But System 1 also makes predictable mistakes. It jumps to conclusions based on first impressions. It creates stories to fill in missing information. It relies on feelings rather than logic. It makes you more likely to believe things that are familiar or easy to understand. It makes you very sensitive to losses and relatively insensitive to equivalent gains.

System 2 is meant to check System 1's work but it often does not bother. It is lazy. It is easily tired. And it does not notice its own failures. When System 2 misses something, it does not feel uncertain. It continues to feel confident and correct.

Understanding all of this gives you a small but real advantage. You cannot eliminate System 1 or force System 2 to work harder all the time. But you can learn to recognise the situations where your fast thinking is most likely to lead you astray. You can learn to pause, to ask questions, to slow down. And in those moments of slowness, you give yourself the best possible chance of thinking well.

In the next chapter, we will look more closely at System 2 at how much energy it uses, how quickly it gets tired, and at a remarkable and somewhat frightening phenomenon called **ego depletion**: the idea that the more decisions you make, the worse the later ones become.

## Grammar Focus

### 🔤 Grammar Structures in This Chapter

This chapter uses five important grammar structures. Here they are with examples from the text:

---

**1. Present Simple for facts and habits**
Use the Present Simple when something is always true, or when describing how something works in general.

- *"System 1 works automatically."*
- *"System 2 requires effort."*
- *"Losing money feels much worse than gaining the same amount of money feels good."*

→ See: [[Present Simple]]

---

**2. Past Simple for completed events**
Use the Past Simple for events that happened at a specific time in the past and are now finished.

- *"Kahneman was born in Tel Aviv in 1934."*
- *"The ball flew through the air."*
- *"Kahneman and Tversky studied one central question."*

→ See: [[Past Simple]]

---

**3. Modal verbs for ability and possibility**
Modal verbs like *can*, *cannot*, *could*, *might*, and *would* express ability, possibility, or what is generally true.

- *"System 1 can recognise faces instantly."*
- *"System 2 cannot do everything at once."*
- *"Appearances can be deceiving."*

→ See: [[Modal Verbs Concept]]

---

**4. Passive Voice for describing processes**
The Passive Voice is used when the focus is on what happens, not on who does it. It is very common in academic and scientific writing.

- *"The book was written by Daniel Kahneman."*
- *"The gorilla costume person was not seen by most viewers."*
- *"Information is presented in a particular way."* (framing effect)

→ See: [[Passive Voice Concept]]

---

**5. Conditional sentences for hypothetical situations**
Conditionals describe what would happen in a particular situation.

- *"If you get one percent better every day for a year, you will end up thirty-seven times better."*
- *"If a doctor says this operation has a ninety percent survival rate, patients are more likely to agree."*
- *"If you stop and ask this question, you might realise something important."*

→ See: [[Conditionals Concept]]
