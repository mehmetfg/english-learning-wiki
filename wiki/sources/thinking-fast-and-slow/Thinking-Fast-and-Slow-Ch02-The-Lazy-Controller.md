# Thinking, Fast and Slow
## Chapter 2 The Lazy Controller
### Extended A2/B1 Reader

---

*Previously in Chapter 1: We learned that the human mind has two systems. System 1 is fast, automatic, and emotional it works without effort, always running in the background. System 2 is slow, careful, and logical but it is lazy. It prefers not to work if it does not have to. In this chapter, we go deeper into System 2: how much energy it uses, how quickly it gets tired, and what happens to our decisions when it runs out of power.*

---

## Part 1 Walking and Thinking

Here is a simple experiment you can try yourself.

Find a friend. Stand still together, and ask your friend a moderately difficult question something like, "What is the capital of Australia?" or "Who wrote *War and Peace*?" They will answer quickly, probably without much effort.

Now try something different. Start walking together at a brisk pace not running, but walking fast. While walking, ask your friend a genuinely difficult question something like, "What is the square root of seventy-three, rounded to one decimal place?" Something that requires real calculation.

Almost certainly, your friend will slow down. They may even stop walking completely. They will look down or away. They will become quiet. And they will take much longer to answer than they did when standing still.

This is not a coincidence. It reveals something fundamental about how System 2 works.

When your friend slows down their walking, they are freeing up mental resources for the calculation. Walking even fast walking requires some cognitive effort. For a familiar route, System 1 handles most of it. But when the cognitive demand of the thinking task increases, System 2 claims a larger share of the available mental resources. And to get those resources, it reduces the effort going to other tasks including walking.

The same thing happens in reverse. When people are asked to walk at a very slow pace slower than their natural speed they often find it easier to think and talk. When the physical task requires conscious attention (because it is unusual and uncomfortable), less cognitive resource is available for mental tasks.

Kahneman noticed this in his own work. He would go for long walks while thinking through difficult ideas. He found that easy thinking was fine while walking. But genuinely hard thinking working through a complex argument, holding many ideas in mind at once required him to stop. His body and mind were drawing from the same limited pool of energy.

This observation is simple, but its implications are profound. If walking and thinking compete for the same resources, then everything you do throughout the day competes with everything else. Every decision you make, every problem you solve, every temptation you resist all of these consume from the same finite resource. And when that resource runs low, System 2 gets weaker. It becomes less able to check System 1's work. It becomes less able to override impulse with reason.

## Part 2 The Battery of the Mind

Think of System 2 as a battery. At the beginning of the day, the battery is fully charged. System 2 is at its best sharp, alert, careful, and able to override System 1's automatic responses when necessary.

But every time System 2 does work every time it concentrates, calculates, resists temptation, makes a decision, or solves a problem it uses some of that battery's charge. And unlike a smartphone battery, you cannot see how much charge is remaining. You do not get a warning when System 2 is running low. You simply continue to feel confident in your thinking, even as its quality gradually declines.

As the battery drains, several things begin to happen. First, System 2 starts to cut corners. Instead of fully evaluating options, it begins to accept the first answer that feels reasonable which is usually the answer System 1 already suggested. Instead of carefully weighing the evidence, it starts to rely more on impressions and emotions. The lazy part of System 2 gets lazier.

Second, self-control becomes harder. Self-control the ability to resist what you want to do now in favour of what is better for you in the long run is a System 2 function. It requires effort. When System 2 is depleted, self-control weakens. This is why, late in the evening, after a hard day of work, people are more likely to eat unhealthy food, make impulsive purchases, send messages they later regret, or give in to habits they were trying to break. The mental battery is low, and System 2 no longer has the power to say no.

Third, decisions become worse. Not dramatically worse you do not suddenly become incapable of making any decisions. But the decisions are subtly less good. They are more influenced by irrelevant factors. They are more affected by how options are presented. They are more swayed by the order in which information is presented.

## Part 3 The Judges and the Prisoners

One of the most striking pieces of research Kahneman discusses in his book concerns judges and prisoners in Israel.

A team of researchers studied 1,112 decisions made by Israeli judges at parole hearings. A parole hearing is a meeting where a judge decides whether a prisoner can be released from prison early before their full sentence is finished. The prisoner, their lawyer, and sometimes other people are present. The judge listens and then decides: yes, this person can go free, or no, they must stay in prison.

These decisions are enormously important. For the prisoners, being granted parole means freedom. Being denied parole means continuing to live in a prison cell. The stakes could not be higher.

The researchers wanted to know: what factors actually determined whether a prisoner was granted parole?

They looked at many variables the type of crime, the length of the sentence, the prisoner's behaviour in prison, the arguments made by the lawyers. All of these things, you would expect, should influence the judge's decision. And they did but not as much as something that had nothing to do with justice at all.

The most powerful factor in determining whether a prisoner was granted parole was the time of day.

Prisoners who appeared before the judge early in the morning when the court had just started for the day were granted parole approximately sixty-five percent of the time. As the morning progressed, the rate of parole fell steadily. By the time of the mid-morning break, it had dropped close to zero. Almost no prisoners were being released.

Then the judges had a break. They rested, ate a snack, and had some food and drink. After the break, the parole rate jumped back up to approximately sixty-five percent. Then it fell again through the second session, until the lunch break. After lunch, it jumped back up. And so it continued throughout the day.

The pattern was clear and consistent. Well-rested judges, who had recently eaten, granted parole at a high rate. Tired judges, who had been working for a long time without a break, denied almost all parole requests. And this was true regardless of the merits of the individual cases.

## Part 4 What the Judges Reveal

The judges in this study were not bad people. They were not corrupt. They were trained professionals who genuinely wanted to make fair decisions. But they were human beings with human brains and human brains have limited energy.

When the judges were tired and hungry, their System 2 was depleted. And what does System 2 do when it is depleted? It takes the path of least resistance. It defaults to the safest, easiest answer.

In a parole hearing, the default the safest, easiest answer is to say no. Keeping a prisoner in prison is the conservative choice. Nothing bad happens if you deny parole and the prisoner was actually safe. But if you grant parole and the prisoner commits another crime, that is very bad. A tired System 2, unwilling to do the hard work of carefully evaluating each case, simply defaults to no. It is the safe choice. It requires no justification.

When the judges were rested and fed when their System 2 had been refreshed by food and rest they had the mental energy to engage with each case properly. They could listen, weigh the arguments, consider the evidence, and sometimes say yes.

This finding is deeply disturbing. It means that a prisoner's fate freedom or continued imprisonment might be partly determined not by the strength of their case, but by whether their hearing happened to fall before or after the judge's lunch break.

And this is not just true of Israeli judges. Research suggests that similar patterns exist in many other contexts. Doctors make different decisions depending on the time of day. Teachers grade papers differently depending on how many they have already graded. Executives in companies make better decisions in the morning than in the afternoon. People are more likely to agree to an additional task even a reasonable one early in the day, and more likely to refuse it late in the day.

The mental battery affects everything. And we almost never think about it.

## Part 5 Ego Depletion: The Research

The scientific name for this phenomenon the weakening of System 2 through use is **ego depletion**. This term was created by the American psychologist Roy Baumeister. He studied self-control and willpower for many years, and his research had a major influence on Kahneman's ideas.

Baumeister's most famous experiment went like this. Participants came into a laboratory and were told they were going to take part in a study about taste. They were brought into a room where there were two things on a table: a plate of freshly baked chocolate biscuits, and a bowl of radishes.

Half the participants were asked to eat the biscuits as many as they liked. The other half were asked to eat only the radishes. If they wanted to eat the biscuits which of course they did, because the biscuits smelled wonderful and the radishes were plain and unappealing they had to resist. They had to sit there, smelling the warm biscuits, and eat only the cold, bitter radishes.

Resisting the biscuits required System 2. It required self-control. It required the participants to override a very clear and immediate desire the desire to eat the delicious thing in front of them.

After this phase of the experiment, both groups were given a completely different task. They were asked to work on a series of difficult geometric puzzles. The puzzles looked solvable, but they were actually impossible to solve. The researchers wanted to see how long people would persist before giving up.

The result was dramatic. The biscuit-eaters who had not had to use any self-control persisted for an average of twenty minutes before giving up. The radish-eaters who had already used up self-control resisting the biscuits persisted for only eight minutes. They gave up more than twice as quickly.

The radish-eaters were not less intelligent than the biscuit-eaters. They were not less motivated. They were simply depleted. They had used up their store of willpower on the biscuit-resisting task. And when they needed willpower again for the puzzle task, the tank was empty.

## Part 6 Sugar and the Brain

Baumeister's research went further. He wanted to understand the biology behind ego depletion. What was actually happening inside the body when willpower ran out?

He discovered that the brain uses glucose a simple form of sugar as its primary energy source. And System 2, with its effortful, demanding work, uses a great deal of glucose. When blood glucose levels drop either because you have not eaten, or because you have been doing a lot of mental work System 2's performance declines.

In one experiment, participants did a task that required significant self-control. Afterwards, half of them were given a sweet drink containing glucose. The other half were given a drink that looked and tasted similar but contained artificial sweetener instead of real sugar no glucose.

Then both groups were given another demanding task.

The participants who had drunk the glucose drink performed significantly better on the second task than those who had drunk the artificial sweetener. The glucose had literally refuelled their System 2. The artificial sweetener, which provided no actual energy, had not.

This research has a number of practical implications. It suggests that eating a good meal before making an important decision is not just a physical nicety it is cognitively important. It suggests that having a healthy snack during a long meeting is not a distraction from serious work it may actually improve the quality of the thinking done in that meeting. And it suggests that the most important decisions in your life should not be made when you are hungry.

The link between blood sugar and decision quality also partly explains the judges' pattern from Part 3. After their breaks during which they had food and drink their blood glucose levels were restored, their System 2 was refreshed, and they were able to make more careful and generous decisions.

## Part 7 The Demands of Cognitive Load

Related to ego depletion is another concept Kahneman calls **cognitive load**. While ego depletion is about how mental energy is depleted over time through effort, cognitive load is about how much mental work your mind is trying to handle at any single moment.

When your cognitive load is low when you are relaxed, your mind is clear, and you do not have too many things to keep track of System 2 can do its job well. It is available to think carefully, to check System 1's work, to override impulsive responses.

But when your cognitive load is high when you are trying to remember a long number, carry out a complex plan, manage strong emotions, or keep track of many things at once System 2 becomes occupied. It has too much to handle. And when System 2 is occupied, System 1 takes over more fully than usual.

Here is a demonstration. Researchers asked people to remember a number while performing a different task. Some people were asked to remember a two-digit number something like "52". Others were asked to remember a seven-digit number something like "4,763,821." Remembering seven digits places a much higher cognitive load on System 2 than remembering two digits.

While they were holding these numbers in memory, both groups were given a choice: they could choose either a piece of chocolate cake or a fruit salad. The healthy, rational choice is clearly the fruit salad. But which group was more likely to choose the chocolate cake?

The seven-digit group chose the chocolate cake at a significantly higher rate than the two-digit group.

The people trying to remember seven digits had less System 2 available for self-control their cognitive load was higher. So when the tempting option appeared, System 1 said "cake!" and System 2 was not available to say "wait, actually, I should choose the fruit salad." They went with the cake.

## Part 8 Priming by Comfort

One of Kahneman's most fascinating observations about System 2 concerns what he calls the **comfort of cognitive ease**. When System 2 is tired or overloaded, the mind naturally gravitates toward things that are familiar, easy, and comfortable. This is not just a matter of choosing easy tasks. It affects how people evaluate ideas, judge statements, and make decisions.

When you read something that is written in a clear, simple font, you process it more easily. And because you process it easily, it feels more fluent more smooth. And because it feels fluent, it feels more true. This is a remarkable finding. The physical experience of reading easily actually makes statements seem more believable.

In experiments, people were shown statements in clear, easy-to-read fonts, and also in difficult, faint, or blurry fonts. The statements were identical the same words, the same claims. But participants rated the statements shown in clear fonts as more likely to be true than the identical statements shown in blurry fonts.

Think about what this means. If you receive a report printed in a slightly difficult-to-read font, your brain specifically System 2 has to work a little harder to read it. This extra effort costs mental energy. And System 2 registers this difficulty and translates it into a feeling of suspicion: "this is hard to process, therefore something might be wrong with it." The statement feels less trustworthy, not because of its content, but because of its font.

This is called the **fluency effect**, or the **ease-equals-truth heuristic**. If something is easy to think about, it feels true. If something is hard to think about, it feels uncertain or suspicious.

The fluency effect shows up in many places beyond font choice. A stock market investment seems more trustworthy if the company has a name that is easy to pronounce. A person seems more reliable if their name is simple and familiar. An argument seems more convincing if it is expressed in clear, simple language even if the content is identical to a more complex version.

## Part 9 The Two Systems Under Stress

We have seen that System 2 is vulnerable to tiredness, hunger, high cognitive load, and the comfort of cognitive ease. But there is one more situation where System 2 reliably fails: stress.

When people are stressed frightened, anxious, angry, or under time pressure System 1 takes over almost completely. System 2 becomes very difficult to activate. The evolutionary reason for this is clear: in a genuine emergency, there is no time for slow, careful analysis. You need to act fast. System 1 was built for precisely this situation.

But the problem is that modern life contains many situations that feel like emergencies but are not actually dangerous. A difficult conversation with your boss. A presentation in front of many people. An unexpected bill. A conflict with a partner or family member. These situations trigger the same stress response that a predator would have triggered for our ancestors but they require very different responses.

When you are stressed and frightened, System 1 runs the show. This means you are more likely to:
- React to the emotional surface of a situation rather than its actual content
- See threats where there are none
- Jump to conclusions about what other people mean or intend
- Make decisions based on emotion rather than information
- Say things that feel satisfying in the moment but are counterproductive in the long run

Many of the worst decisions people make in their lives happen under stress. Arguments that damage relationships, financial decisions made in panic, professional choices made out of fear or anger these are often the work of a System 2 that has been overwhelmed by stress, leaving System 1 entirely in control.

## Part 10 What the Lazy Controller Means for Daily Life

Let us take everything we have learned in this chapter and bring it into the real world. The central lesson is this: **the quality of your thinking changes dramatically depending on your mental state.** And your mental state is not fixed it changes constantly throughout the day, depending on how much you have used your mind, how well you have slept, how much you have eaten, how stressed you are, and how much cognitive load you are currently carrying.

This has profound practical consequences.

---

**Consequence 1: Timing matters for important decisions**

If you have an important decision to make whether to accept a job offer, how to respond to a difficult email, whether to make a large purchase, how to handle a conflict the time at which you make that decision matters. Not because the facts change, but because your ability to process those facts changes.

The best time to make important decisions is when you are:
- Rested (after sleep, not at the end of a long day)
- Fed (not hungry; blood glucose levels are good)
- Calm (not stressed, anxious, or angry)
- Free of distraction (low cognitive load)

The worst time to make important decisions is when you are tired, hungry, stressed, distracted, or at the end of a long series of other decisions.

Many people do the opposite of this. They spend their freshest, most alert hours of the morning dealing with emails and minor tasks, saving the "important work" for later in the day when their System 2 is already depleted. Kahneman's research suggests this is a mistake.

---

**Consequence 2: Design your environment for a tired System 2**

Because System 2 will inevitably get tired, you cannot rely on it to always make the right choice through pure willpower. Instead, the smarter approach is to design your environment so that the right choice is also the easiest choice so that System 1 naturally leads you toward it.

Want to eat more healthily? Keep healthy food at the front of the fridge, where System 1's eye will find it first. Keep unhealthy food out of sight, or do not buy it at all.

Want to exercise more? Put your exercise clothes next to your bed the night before. Remove the friction that stops System 1 from starting.

Want to spend less time on your phone? Put the phone in another room. The physical distance reduces the automatic impulse to pick it up.

This is not about willpower. It is about designing a world in which System 2 does not have to fight System 1 a world in which the default choice, the lazy choice, the easiest choice, is already the right one.

---

**Consequence 3: Understand others' behaviour differently**

The knowledge of ego depletion and cognitive load should also change how you understand the behaviour of other people.

When someone is rude, dismissive, or makes a bad decision, it may not be because they are a bad person. It may be because their System 2 is depleted. They have been working hard, dealing with stress, managing difficult emotions and by the time they interact with you, they have very little mental energy left for patience and careful thought.

This does not excuse bad behaviour. But it does explain it. And understanding the explanation can make you less likely to take things personally, and more likely to choose good moments to have important conversations moments when both people have enough mental energy available to listen, think, and speak carefully.

---

**Consequence 4: Beware of high-load situations**

When your cognitive load is high when you are very busy, managing many things at once, or trying to hold a lot of information in memory your ability to make good decisions is reduced. This is exactly when salespeople, advertisers, and others who want to influence your decisions try to approach you.

A car salesperson who shows you ten different options before asking you to decide is increasing your cognitive load. A phone contract with many confusing terms and conditions is increasing your cognitive load. A supermarket that places the most expensive products at eye level is making those products the default choice for a System 1 that does not want to work hard.

Being aware of these tactics does not make you immune to them. But it does give you the option to pause, reduce your cognitive load, and bring System 2 back online before making an important choice.

## Part 11 The Question of Self-Knowledge

One of the most unsettling aspects of everything Kahneman describes in this chapter is this: **you cannot feel your own ego depletion.** You cannot feel your own cognitive load. You cannot feel when System 2 has gone quiet and System 1 has taken over.

When you are tired and making bad decisions, you do not feel tired. You feel normal. When your self-control has been depleted by a long day of resisting temptation, you do not notice that your willpower is weaker. You just feel like you want something, and you do not quite manage to resist it, and later you wonder why.

Kahneman describes this as "the illusion of understanding" the feeling of being fully informed and fully in control, even when you are not. Your System 1 creates a continuous narrative of understanding. It narrates your experience to you in real time: "I am thinking clearly, I understand what is happening, my decisions are based on good reasons." This narrative does not stop when it is false. It continues, confidently, even as System 2 fades and System 1 takes over.

This is one of the most important lessons in the entire book, and it will appear again in many different forms in the chapters ahead. We are the last to know when our thinking is going wrong. We do not notice our own biases. We do not notice when we are tired, hungry, or stressed to the point where our thinking has become significantly worse. We feel, always, like the reasonable, careful, thoughtful person we believe ourselves to be even when the evidence suggests otherwise.

What can be done about this? Not everything. You cannot monitor yourself in real time with perfect accuracy. But you can do a few things:

You can create **decision rules** that apply regardless of how you feel in the moment. Rules like: "I will never make a major financial decision on the same day I am introduced to the idea." Or: "I will sleep before responding to any email that upset me." Or: "I will eat before having an important conversation." These rules are applied by System 2 in a calm, clear moment and they reduce the influence of the depleted System 2 that will be present in the moment of decision.

You can **seek feedback** from others. Because you cannot see your own blind spots, other people can sometimes see them for you if you ask, and if you are genuinely willing to hear what they say.

And you can practise **recognising the warning signs** of depletion: increasing irritability, diminishing patience, a tendency to default to "no" without careful thought, or an unusual level of confidence combined with a reluctance to listen to alternatives. These are the signals that System 2 is running low and System 1 is beginning to run the show.

## Grammar Focus

### 🔤 Grammar Structures in This Chapter

**1. Past Continuous for ongoing background actions**
The Past Continuous describes an action that was in progress at a specific time in the past it sets the background scene.

- *"The participants were holding numbers in memory while making a choice."*
- *"The judges were working for a long time without a break."*
- *"While they were walking at a brisk pace, they found it harder to think."*

→ See: [[Past Continuous]]

---

**2. Gerunds and Infinitives after verbs**
Some verbs are followed by gerunds (-ing), and some by infinitives (to + verb). Both express actions, but the grammar is different.

- *"Resisting the biscuits required System 2."* (gerund as subject)
- *"They began to cut corners."* (infinitive after "begin")
- *"They stopped doing anything else."* (gerund after "stop")
- *"They managed to find the answer."* (infinitive after "manage")

→ See: [[Gerunds and Infinitives]]

---

**3. Passive Voice for describing research findings**
Scientific and academic writing uses Passive Voice when the focus is on what was found or done, not on who did it.

- *"Participants were given a sweet drink."*
- *"The pattern was clear and consistent."*
- *"1,112 decisions were studied by researchers."*

→ See: [[Passive Voice Concept]]

---

**4. Conditional sentences (Type 1 and Type 2)**
Conditionals describe what happens or would happen under particular conditions.

- *"If you have an important decision to make, the time at which you make it matters."* (Type 1 real possibility)
- *"If someone is rude or dismissive, it may be because their System 2 is depleted."* (Type 1)
- *"If System 2 were fully functioning, it would recognise that the two sentences mean the same thing."* (Type 2 hypothetical)

→ See: [[Conditionals Concept]]

---

**5. Modal verbs for ability and recommendation**
Modal verbs express what is possible, likely, or advisable.

- *"You cannot feel your own ego depletion."* (impossibility)
- *"You can create decision rules."* (ability / possibility)
- *"Important decisions should not be made when you are hungry."* (advice / recommendation)
- *"Other people can sometimes see your blind spots for you."* (ability)

→ See: [[Modal Verbs Concept]]
