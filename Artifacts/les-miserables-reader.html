Three years had passed since Jean Valjean left the Bishop's town. The silver candlesticks stood on the mantelpiece of a house in Montreuil-sur-Mer, where they would remain for the rest of his life — a daily reminder of the promise he had made to himself.

He had changed his name to Monsieur Madeleine, and had built a factory that gave work to hundreds of people. The town had prospered, and the townspeople, who had once been poor and desperate, now had steady wages and warm homes. They were grateful to him, though they knew nothing of his past.

Among those who worked in his factory was a young woman named Fantine. She was twenty-three years old, pretty, and hardworking, but she carried a secret that she would not have shared with anyone for the world. She had a daughter — a little girl named Cosette — who had been left in the care of an innkeeper and his wife in a distant village called Montfermeil.

The innkeeper's name was Thenardier. He was a small, cunning man who had the habit of smiling when he wanted something, and frowning when he had already taken it. His wife was no better. They had agreed to look after Cosette in exchange for a monthly payment, but they treated the child as a servant rather than as a guest, dressing their own daughters in fine clothes while Cosette went barefoot even in winter.

Every month, Fantine sent them what little she had managed to save. She worked longer hours than anyone else in the factory, taking on extra tasks whenever they were offered, spending almost nothing on herself. Her hair, which had once been her great beauty, she cut off and sold. Her teeth — two white, healthy teeth — she had extracted and sold to a dentist for enough money to pay the Thenardiers for three months.

No one in the factory knew about Cosette. Fantine had kept her secret carefully, fearing that if her fellow workers discovered she was an unmarried mother, she would be dismissed. In those days, such things were not forgiven easily.

But secrets, no matter how carefully they are kept, have a way of coming out.

One afternoon, a fellow worker who had grown suspicious followed the postman and learned the truth. By the next morning, the entire factory knew. The forelady — a bitter woman who had never liked Fantine — went directly to the manager's office.

Monsieur Madeleine was not there. In his absence, the forelady made the decision herself.

When Fantine returned from her midday break, she found her place at the workbench taken by another woman, and her few belongings wrapped in a cloth by the door.

She had been dismissed without warning, without explanation, and without pay.

💭 Philosophy Layer (İngilizce kelime servisiyle birlikte)

%20 ağırlık — kavramlar İngilizce ediniminin aracı

Fantine's story is about sacrifice (fedakarlık) — but it is a sacrifice without recognition (tanınma/fark edilme olmadan). She gives everything: her wages, her hair, her teeth. Hugo uses this accumulation (birikim, üst üste yığma) — wage, hair, teeth — to show that poverty does not take all at once; it takes piece by piece (parça parça).

This connects to a question Aristotle asks in the Nicomachean Ethics: can a person remain virtuous (erdemli) under extreme deprivation (yoksunluk)? Aristotle says that external goods (dışsal iyiler — para, sağlık, statü) matter: a person cannot flourish in total poverty. Fantine is Hugo's answer: she tries to remain good, but the system makes it structurally impossible (yapısal olarak imkansız).

The forelady's decision is also worth examining. She acts in the absence of (yokluğunda) the one person who might have shown mercy. This is not coincidence — Hugo is saying that institutional cruelty (kurumsal zalimlik) often depends on the absence of the merciful (merhametlinin yokluğu).

Key English vocabulary from this analysis: sacrifice, recognition, accumulation, piece by piece, virtuous, deprivation, external goods, structurally impossible, in the absence of, institutional, mercy, merciful.
